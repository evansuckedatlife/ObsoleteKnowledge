# Genesis

## summary

**Genesis** is the first book of the *Hebrew Bible* (*Tanakh*) and the *Old Testament*, narrating humanity's origins, God's creation, the fall of humanity through sin, and the patriarchal history of Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph. Written in ancient *Hebrew* and traditionally attributed to *Moses* (though scholars date it to multiple sources spanning centuries), Genesis establishes foundational myths, genealogies, and covenants central to *Judaism* and *Christianity*.

## you gotta know

- The book opens with the *Creation narrative*: God creates the heavens and earth in six days and rests on the seventh, establishing the *Sabbath*; the ordering reflects ancient *Mesopotamian* and *Egyptian* cosmologies reframed through *Israelite* monotheism.
- *Adam and Eve*, the first humans, are created in God's image and placed in the *Garden of Eden*; their disobedience (eating the forbidden fruit) introduces sin, shame, and mortality, explaining human suffering and death.
- *Noah* and the *Great Flood* represent God's judgment on human wickedness and destruction of all but the righteous; the story reflects ancient *Mesopotamian* flood narratives (*Gilgamesh Epic*) adapted into *Israelite* theology.
- *Abraham* is introduced as the ancestor chosen by God to found a great nation; his covenant with God (circumcision, promised descendants) establishes the special relationship between God and the *Israelite* people.
- *Isaac* and *Jacob* continue the patriarchal line; *Jacob* is renamed *Israel* after wrestling with an angel, and his twelve sons become the ancestors of the twelve tribes of *Israel*.
- *Joseph*'s story (sold into slavery, rise to power in *Egypt*, reunion with family) concludes Genesis and transitions to the *Exodus* narrative, explaining how *Israelites* came to live in *Egypt*.
- Genesis contains diverse literary forms: creation myths, genealogies (*begat* lists), narrative stories, poetry, and legal material, suggesting compilation from multiple sources over centuries; modern scholars identify *Priestly*, *Jahwist*, and *Elohist* sources.
