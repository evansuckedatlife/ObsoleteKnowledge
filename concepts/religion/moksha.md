## summary

**Moksha**, also termed **mukti**, is the ultimate spiritual liberation and supreme goal of human existence within [[hinduism]] and related Indian religious systems. Formulated across the first millennium BCE through the foundational speculative dialogues of the *[[upanishads]]*, it denotes the final emancipation of the soul from [[samsara]], the recurring cycle of birth, death, and transmigration governed by the inexorable law of *karma*. The concept stands as the philosophical culmination of classical South Asian thought because it reorients human endeavor away from transient worldly achievements and ritual duties toward absolute self-realization, achieved through the profound experiential awakening that one's inner essence is identical with [[brahman]], the unconditioned cosmic reality.

## you gotta know

- Constitutes the fourth and supreme objective among the *purusharthas*, the classical quartet of human life pursuits in [[hinduism]], standing hierarchically above *dharma* (ethical duty), *artha* (material security and wealth), and *kama* (sensual pleasure and desire).
- Achieved through the complete breaking of the cycle of [[samsara]] and the exhaustion of all accumulated *karma*, thereby permanently liberating the transmigrating soul from the suffering, limitation, and mortality inherent in phenomenal existence.
- Attained through multiple recognized spiritual paths (*margas* or *yogas*) synthesized in the revered *[[bhagavad-gita]]*, including *jnana yoga* (the path of experiential knowledge), *bhakti yoga* (loving devotion to a personal deity), *karma yoga* (selfless duty performed without attachment to fruits), and *raja yoga* (systematic meditative contemplation).
- Formulated in the non-dualist *Advaita Vedanta* school of *Adi Shankara* as the direct cognitive recognition of non-duality (*advaita*): realizing that the individual self (*atman*) has never truly been distinct from the cosmic ground of being ([[brahman]]), which dissolves the epistemic illusion of *maya*.
- Distinguished in theological and scholastic debate between *jivanmukti*, living liberation attained by an enlightened sage while still inhabiting a physical body, and *videhamukti*, final discarnate release realized at the moment of physical death when karmic embodiment ends permanently.
- Interpreted conversely in the dualist *Dvaita Vedanta* tradition of *Madhva*, which explicitly rejects monistic identity and instead defines liberation as the soul attaining eternal, blissful communion with and loving servitude toward a personal supreme deity in a celestial realm.
- Paralleled by sister Dharmic concepts of ultimate spiritual release, corresponding to *nirvana* in [[buddhism]] and *kevala jnana* in Jainism, although [[buddhism]] distinctly rejects the existence of a permanent soul (*anatman*) while Jainism conceptualizes liberation as the soul shedding all binding karmic matter.
- Evoked throughout the *[[vedas]]* and early philosophical literature through celebrated allegories, including rivers losing their distinct names and boundaries upon dissolving into the vast ocean, a snake casting off its outgrown skin, or a dreamer waking to true reality.

## connections

- [[hinduism]] — the primary religious tradition in which liberation stands as the ultimate spiritual aspiration.
- [[upanishads]] — the foundational speculative texts that first articulated liberation through identity with the divine.
- [[vedas]] — the ancient scriptures whose sacrificial rituals evolved into the contemplative quest for transcendence.
- [[bhagavad-gita]] — the seminal scripture detailing practical paths of action, devotion, and wisdom to reach liberation.
- [[buddhism]] — the companion Dharmic tradition that adapted the pursuit of release into the doctrine of *nirvana*.
- [[brahman]] — the ultimate, unconditioned reality with which the individual soul realizes unity upon achieving emancipation.
- [[samsara]] — the continuous cycle of birth, death, and rebirth from which spiritual liberation offers final release.
