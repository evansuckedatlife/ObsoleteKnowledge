## summary

The **Sunni and Shia** divide forms the fundamental denominational schism within [[islam]], originating in the seventh-century succession crisis following the death of the Prophet [[muhammad]] in 632 CE. While Sunni Muslims favored selecting community leadership through consensus, leading to the historical institution of the [[caliphate]], Shia partisans maintained that divine guidance and legitimate authority belonged exclusively to the Prophet's household through his cousin and son-in-law *Ali*. Crystallized by early civil conflicts across the [[middle-east]] and deepened by centuries of theological and legal divergence, this split remains one of the defining religious, political, and cultural fault lines in world history.

## you gotta know

- The schism erupted immediately after the death of [[muhammad]] in 632 CE, when prominent companions gathered at the *Saqifah* to choose *Abu Bakr* as leader, bypassing the claims of *Ali ibn Abi Talib*.
- Sunnis, meaning followers of the *Sunnah* (prophetic custom) and community consensus (*ijma*), comprise roughly 85 to 90 percent of the global Muslim population and honor the first four *Rashidun* ("Rightly Guided") caliphs.
- Shias, meaning "partisans of *Ali*" (*Shi'at Ali*), believe that [[muhammad]] explicitly designated *Ali* as his spiritual and temporal successor at *Ghadir Khumm*, establishing the hereditary line of the *Imamah*.
- The division was irrevocably sanctified in blood at the *Battle of Karbala* in 680 CE, where *Ali*'s son *Husayn ibn Ali* and his family were killed by the forces of the Umayyad caliph *Yazid I*.
- The annual commemoration of *Husayn*'s martyrdom during *Ashura* became the theological and emotional epicenter of Shia identity, elevating themes of righteous suffering, sacrifice, and resistance to tyranny.
- Shia theology developed distinct eschatological doctrines, notably belief in the *Mahdi* or *Hidden Imam*—in the majority *Twelver* tradition—who entered occultation (*ghaybah*) and will return before the Day of Judgment.
- While both branches venerate the [[quran]] and observe the [[five-pillars-of-islam]], they diverge on acceptable collections of *hadith* (prophetic traditions), legal jurisprudence (*madhhabs*), and the clerical authority granted to senior scholars like *ayatollahs*.
- Major geopolitical rivalries historically crystallized around this sectarian boundary, most prominently between the Sunni [[ottoman-empire]] and the Persian *Safavid Empire*, which established Twelver Shi'ism as its state religion in 1501.

## connections

- [[islam]] — the global faith whose historical development, theology, and legal traditions are divided between these two major branches.
- [[muhammad]] — the prophet whose passing without a universally acknowledged succession plan precipitated the split.
- [[caliphate]] — the supreme political office established by Sunni consensus that Shia partisans rejected in favor of the *Imamah*.
- [[quran]] — the common scripture revered by both communities, though expounded through differing hermeneutical and commentary traditions.
- [[five-pillars-of-islam]] — the fundamental devotional duties shared across both branches, despite modest differences in ritual performance and legal rulings.
- [[middle-east]] — the geographic theater where the dispute originated and where sectarian dynamics continue to influence modern statecraft.
- [[ottoman-empire]] — the dominant Sunni imperial power whose centuries-long struggles against the Shia Safavids entrenched modern regional borders.
