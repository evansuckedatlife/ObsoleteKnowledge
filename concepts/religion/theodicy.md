## summary

**Theodicy** is the philosophical and theological effort to justify the existence of evil and suffering in a world governed by an omnipotent, omniscient, and benevolent God. The term, coined by *Leibniz*, encapsulates the central paradox: if God is all-powerful and all-good, why does evil persist? This inquiry has shaped religious thought, literature, and philosophy for millennia.

## you gotta know

- The word itself combines Greek *theos* (God) and *dike* (justice), literally "God's justice"; it was coined by philosopher *Leibniz* in the 17th century.
- The classical logical formulation of the problem: if God is omnipotent, He can prevent evil; if benevolent, He would want to; if omniscient, He foresees it. Yet evil exists — how can all three divine attributes coexist with evil's reality?
- Traditional theological responses include the *free will defense* (evil results from human freedom, which God permits to preserve genuine choice) and the *soul-making theodicy* (suffering develops moral and spiritual growth through adversity).
- *Augustine's* early theodicy emphasized human free will and the privation of good (evil as absence rather than positive force); *Aquinas* later refined this distinction between natural and moral evil.
- The [[book-of-job]] presents theodicy without resolving it: *Job* suffers unjustly, questions God, and receives no rational explanation — only a reassertion of God's incomprehensible power and sovereignty.
- *Leibniz* argued this is "the best of all possible worlds" — a formulation later mocked by *Voltaire* in *Candide* as naive optimism that ignores real suffering and natural disasters.
- Modern theodicy often abandons the attempt to reconcile evil with omnipotence, instead exploring *process theology* (God is not omnipotent but co-creative) or *skeptical theism* (human cognitive limits prevent full understanding of divine purposes).
- The distinction between *moral evil* (human cruelty and choice) and *natural evil* (disease, earthquakes, suffering not caused by human will) complicates theodicy, as the free will defense applies only to the former.

## connections

- [[suffering]] — the empirical reality that theodicy attempts to explain and justify.
- [[book-of-job]] — the biblical text that epitomizes the theodicy problem without offering philosophical resolution.
- [[the-brothers-karamazov]] — *Dostoevsky's* novel wherein the character *Ivan* articulates a devastating critique of theodicy, arguing that no cosmic harmony justifies innocent suffering.
- [[god]] — the being whose justice theodicy defends.
- [[evil]] — the phenomenon theodicy must account for and explain within monotheistic theology.
