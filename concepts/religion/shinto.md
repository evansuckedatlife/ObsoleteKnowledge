# Shinto

## summary

**Shinto** is Japan's indigenous polytheistic religion, emphasizing the veneration of *kami* (spirits or deities) present in nature, ancestors, and imperial lineage. Blending animism, ancestor worship, and state ideology, Shinto has been woven into Japanese culture and identity for over two millennia, coexisting peacefully with Buddhism and later becoming intertwined with Japanese nationalism. Unlike religions centered on a founding prophet or sacred text, Shinto remains diffuse and adaptive, focused on purification, harmony with nature, and respect for the sacred within everyday life.

## you gotta know

- Shinto literally means "the Way of the *Kami*"—sacred spirits or deities; kami inhabit natural features (mountains, waterfalls, ancient trees), animals, ancestors, and other entities, making the natural world inherently sacred.
- *Mount Fuji* is among Shinto's most sacred sites, venerated as the abode of kami and a pilgrimage destination; thousands of adherents ascend it annually as a spiritual practice.
- Shinto has no founder, sacred scripture, or unified doctrine; instead, it comprises diverse local and regional practices centered on shrines, rituals of purification, seasonal festivals, and relationships with kami.
- *Kami worship* occurs at Shinto shrines (*jinja*), where worshippers perform purification rituals, make offerings, and pray for blessings; the torii (gate) marks the boundary between mundane and sacred space.
- Shinto developed alongside Buddhism in Japan: rather than displacing it, the two religions coexist and often overlap in practice; many Japanese observe both Shinto rituals (for life events, seasonal festivals) and Buddhist practices (funerals, memorial services).
- The *Shinto priesthood* (shrine maidens and priests) perform rituals, maintain shrines, and conduct ceremonies; they do not preach or convert, reflecting Shinto's non-proselytizing character.
- *Kami-musubi* (the productive creative force of kami) underlies Shinto theology; fertility, growth, renewal, and harmony with natural cycles are central spiritual values rather than moral law-giving or salvation doctrine.
- During Japan's imperial expansion (late 19th–mid-20th centuries), Shinto was instrumentalized as state ideology, linking the emperor to the kami and emphasizing national essence; postwar disestablishment separated Shinto from state control, though its cultural and spiritual significance remains profound.
