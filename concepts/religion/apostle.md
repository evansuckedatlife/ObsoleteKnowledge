## summary

An **apostle** is one of the twelve (or sometimes more) followers of Jesus chosen to be his closest companions and primary witnesses to his ministry. The term comes from the Greek *apostolos* (one sent forth) and refers specifically to those commissioned to spread Jesus's teachings after the Resurrection. The apostles served as the foundation of the early Christian Church and their authority and experiences shaped Christian theology and practice.

## you gotta know

- Jesus selected twelve apostles as his inner circle, though interpretations of exactly who comprised the group varies between gospels.
- The twelve traditionally include *Peter*, *James*, *John*, *Andrew*, *Philip*, *Bartholomew*, *Matthew*, *Thomas*, *James the Less*, *Judas*, *Simon*, and *Thaddeus*.
- After the Resurrection, the apostles became the leaders of the Jerusalem church and witnesses whose testimony authenticated Jesus's divine nature.
- *Peter* emerged as the chief apostle and *Paul*, though not one of the original twelve, claimed apostolic authority as a direct witness to the risen Jesus.
- Apostles performed miracles, baptized converts, and were imprisoned and martyred for spreading Christianity throughout the Mediterranean world.
- The apostolic age ended with the death of the last apostle, traditionally dated to the late first century, marking the transition to second-generation Christian leadership.
- Apostolic succession—the doctrine that authority and spiritual legitimacy pass directly from the apostles through a continuous chain of bishops—became foundational to Catholic and Orthodox church governance.

## connections

- [[peter]] — the chief apostle, given authority over the church.

- [[new-testament]] — the writings authored by or attributed to apostles.
