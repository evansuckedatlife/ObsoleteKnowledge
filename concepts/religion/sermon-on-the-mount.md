# Sermon on the Mount

## summary

The **Sermon on the Mount** is a central teaching of *Jesus* recorded primarily in the *Gospel of Matthew* (chapters 5–7), presenting his moral and spiritual vision for his followers. Delivered on a mountainside in *Galilee*, it includes the *Beatitudes* (blessings of the poor, mourning, meek), teachings on the law, and the *Golden Rule*, and has become the ethical and spiritual cornerstone of *Christianity*, emphasizing inner virtue, love, and God's kingdom over legalism.

## you gotta know

- The *Beatitudes* open the sermon, promising blessings to the poor, those who mourn, the meek, those hungry for righteousness, the merciful, the pure-hearted, peacemakers, and the persecuted; they invert worldly values, exalting spiritual poverty and suffering.
- Jesus reinterprets the *Old Testament* law (*Torah*) spiritually rather than literally: anger is equivalent to murder, lustful thoughts equivalent to adultery; he emphasizes inner righteousness over external compliance.
- The *Golden Rule* appears near the sermon's end: "Do unto others as you would have them do unto you"; it summarizes the law and the prophets and has been adopted by numerous religious and secular ethical systems.
- Jesus teaches radical love and forgiveness: love your enemies, turn the other cheek, give to those who ask, do not judge others—teachings that challenged both *Roman* law and *Jewish* legalism.
- The sermon concludes with parables of foundation: building on rock (following Jesus's words) versus sand (ignoring them), establishing the consequences of accepting or rejecting Jesus's teaching.
- The sermon's ethical demands—absolute honesty, radical forgiveness, sacrificial love—are interpreted variously: as ideals to aspire to, as critique of hypocrisy, as norms for monastic communities, or as impossible standards revealing humanity's need for grace.
- The sermon reflects Jesus's proclamation of the *Kingdom of God* as a present or imminent reality where God's will is done, justice reigns, and the poor are exalted; it offers both spiritual and social vision.
