## summary

**Harmonic function** is music-theoretical framework explaining why certain chord progressions feel satisfying, stable, or tension-filled based on their relationship to a tonal center. In the key of C major, for instance, a C-major chord feels "home" (tonic), an F-major feels like departure (subdominant), a G-major creates tension (dominant), and returning to C major resolves that tension—this psychological grammar of harmony is harmonic function.

## you gotta know

- Harmonic function categorizes chords into three main types: *tonic* (the home chord, typically I or i), *subdominant* (moving away from home, typically IV or iv), and *dominant* (creating tension and pulling back home, typically V or V7).
- The *dominant* chord (V or V7) is the most powerful functional chord; the V-I progression is the most conclusive cadence in Western music, satisfying a fundamental harmonic need to resolve.
- *Roman numeral analysis* is the notational system for harmonic function: uppercase numerals (I, IV, V) for major chords, lowercase (i, iv, v) for minor chords; the numeral indicates the chord's scale degree.
- A *cadence* is a harmonic progression that punctuates a phrase; the *authentic cadence* (V→I) is conclusive, the *plagal cadence* (IV→I) is gentle, the *half cadence* (ending on V) is inconclusive.
- The *deceptive cadence* (V→vi) subverts expectation by not resolving to tonic, creating surprise; this play on listener expectations became increasingly important in 19th-century music.
- Harmonic function explains why progressions work psychologically; moving from tonic to subdominant to dominant to tonic creates a narrative of departure and return, tension and release.
- Composers exploit function to create meaning; a repeated tonic is stasis, a prolonged dominant creates dramatic tension, an unexpected modulation (change of key) can signal emotional shift.
