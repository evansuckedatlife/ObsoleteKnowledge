## summary

**West Africa** is a geographic and cultural region stretching from the Atlantic coast to the *Sahel*, encompassing nations like *Ghana*, *Senegal*, *Mali*, *Ivory Coast*, and *Nigeria*. Musically, it is the birthplace of some of the world's most influential musical traditions—rhythmically complex percussive ensembles, call-and-response vocals, and the griot tradition of oral storytelling through song. These West African aesthetics profoundly shaped the diaspora musics of the *Americas*, particularly jazz, blues, and hip-hop.

## you gotta know

- *Drumming* is central; West African drummers deploy multiple percussion instruments in interlocking rhythmic patterns that create dense polyrhythmic textures—the opposite of Western emphasis on harmony and melodic line.
- The *djembe*, a goblet-shaped drum played with hands, and the *talking drum* (a drum whose pitch can be bent by hand pressure) are iconic West African instruments that have entered global musical practice.
- *Griots* are professional musician-poets who preserve and perform history, genealogy, and social commentary through song and storytelling, often accompanying themselves on the *kora* (harp) or *balafon* (wooden xylophone).
- The emphasis on *call-and-response* vocals—a soloist sings a phrase and a chorus responds—became foundational to American spirituals, work songs, gospel, and later rock and rap.
- West African rhythmic concepts (syncopation, polyrhythm, the emphasis on off-beat rather than downbeat) were central to the African-American musical innovations that defined *jazz* and the *blues*.
- Geographic and cultural features like *Lake Volta* shaped settlement patterns and trade routes, influencing which musical traditions diffused and developed.
- The *kora*, a plucked string instrument with a resonant calabash body, allowed griots to accompany their narratives with harmonic and melodic sophistication unique in African instrumental traditions.
- Specific West African nations like *Senegal* and *Mali* developed distinctive musical styles; *Wolof*, *Mandinka*, and *Yoruba* musical traditions carry their own rhythmic vocabularies and spiritual meanings.
- The Atlantic slave trade forcibly exported West African musical DNA to the Americas; enslaved peoples maintained and transformed these traditions into new forms—from work songs to field hollers to spiritual music.
- Modern West African musicians like Fela Kuti synthesized traditional percussion with contemporary genres, proving the ongoing vitality and relevance of these traditions in contemporary contexts.
