# Gregorian Mode

## summary

**Gregorian modes** (also called *church modes* or *ecclesiastical modes*) are a system of eight melodic scales used in Gregorian chant and medieval liturgical music. Unlike the major and minor keys that dominate Western music since the Baroque period, each Gregorian mode has a distinct *final* (ending note, analogous to a tonal center) and a characteristic *range*, creating different melodic characters and emotional associations. The modes were explicitly associated with liturgical function and emotional character, and their study remains foundational to understanding medieval and Renaissance music.

## you gotta know

- The eight Gregorian modes traditionally divide into four *authentic* modes (with higher ranges) and four *plagal* modes (with lower ranges and more restricted tessitura), though the distinction is primarily pedagogical.
- The modes are numbered I through VIII and named historically (though names vary): *Dorian*, *Phrygian*, *Lydian*, *Mixolydian* (authentic); and *Hypodorian*, *Hypophrygian*, *Hypolydian*, *Hypomixolydian* (plagal counterparts).
- Each mode has a distinctive melodic character: Dorian sounds minor-ish but brighter than modern minor; Lydian contains a raised fourth (like a major scale with a sharp 4); Phrygian has a half-step above the final, creating an exotic, Spanish-sounding quality.
- *The final* is the melodic endpoint of a chant and the mode-determining anchor; it is not necessarily the lowest note (that varies by range), distinguishing modes from modern scales where the root/tonic is typically lowest.
- Each mode carried liturgical associations and emotional character: Dorian was solemn and authoritative; Lydian was bright and joyful; Phrygian was penitential; Mixolydian was festive and bold.
- Gregorian modes are *diatonic* (built from the white keys of the piano in various configurations), using only natural notes without chromatic alterations—a fundamental constraint of medieval modal melody.
- The major and minor keys eventually replaced the modal system as harmonic (vertical) thinking superseded purely melodic (horizontal) organization, though modal thinking persists in jazz, folk music, and contemporary classical music.
