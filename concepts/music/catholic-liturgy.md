## summary

**Catholic liturgy** refers to the formal worship practices of the Roman Catholic Church, centered on the *Mass*—a ritualistic reenactment and commemoration of Christ's sacrifice. Music is integral to Catholic liturgical practice, serving both practical functions (guiding the congregation through ritual) and devotional purposes (inspiring spiritual contemplation). From the medieval period through the present, Catholic liturgical worship has been inseparable from sacred music, spawning entire musical genres and shaping the development of Western classical music.

## you gotta know

- The **Mass** consists of *ordinary* (unchanging parts that recur in each Mass: Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei) and *proper* (changing parts specific to each liturgical day), each with designated liturgical chant.
- *Gregorian chant* is the traditional monophonic liturgical music of the Catholic Church, used in the proper and ordinary; the 6th-century codification (attributed to Pope Gregory I) standardized chants across the Church.
- *Polyphonic* settings of the Mass (composing multiple vocal lines for the ordinary) became a major compositional genre in the Renaissance and Baroque periods; composers from Palestrina to Mozart wrote masses as their most prestigious sacred works.
- The *responsorial* form (cantor/choir antiphon-response) structures much Catholic chant, allowing congregational participation while trained singers elaborate ornamentally.
- *Vespers* (evening prayer service) and *Lauds* (morning prayer service) complement the Mass; both used chant and polyphony, and became vehicles for elaborate musical composition (particularly in the Baroque period).
- After the *Second Vatican Council* (1962–1965), the Latin Mass was largely replaced by Mass in the vernacular (local languages), and many traditional Gregorian chants were simplified or replaced, though traditional Latin Mass still exists in some Catholic communities.
- Catholic liturgical music influenced not only sacred genres (motet, antiphon, hymn) but indirectly shaped instrumental music, as composers trained in sacred polyphony applied contrapuntal techniques to secular instrumental forms.
