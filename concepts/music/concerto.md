## summary

A **concerto** is an instrumental musical composition in which one or more solo instruments dialogue with, contest, or alternate against an accompanying orchestral ensemble. Developing in *[[italy]]* during the late seventeenth century before spreading rapidly across *[[germany]]* and the rest of Europe, the genre evolved from the polychoral vocal motet and church sonata into the premier medium for virtuosic instrumental display across the Baroque, Classical, and Romantic periods. It remains an enduring pillar of the Western concert hall, demanding extraordinary technical mastery, expressive maturity, and close musical dialogue between soloist and orchestra.

## you gotta know

- Classical concertos established a standardized fast-slow-fast three-movement structure popularized by *Antonio Vivaldi* and codified in the Classical era by *Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart*, standing in distinct contrast to the four movements customary in a symphony.
- The genre branched early into two contrasting formats: the **concerto grosso**, which contrasts a small group of soloists known as the *concertino* against the full orchestra known as the *ripieno* or *tutti*, and the **solo concerto**, which spotlights a single virtuosic performer against the collective ensemble.
- A signature feature of the first movement is the *cadenza*, an extended, unaccompanied passage historically left to spontaneous improvisation where the orchestra ceases playing so the soloist can exhibit unfettered technical brilliance.
- The double exposition format emerged during the Classical era, blending traditional ritornello architecture with *[[sonata-form]]* so that the full orchestra introduces the main themes before the soloist repeats and develops them.
- *Johann Sebastian Bach* adapted Italian models into masterworks like the *Brandenburg Concertos* and invented the keyboard concerto by elevating the harpsichord from its standard accompanying background role into a front-rank soloist.
- Romantic composers transformed the architecture of the genre, with *Felix Mendelssohn* abandoning the opening orchestral exposition in his *Violin Concerto in E minor* and *Franz Liszt* pioneering cyclic, continuous single-movement works.
- Twentieth-century modernist innovations expanded the genre through neoclassicism and reimagined orchestrations, exemplified by *[[igor-stravinsky]]*'s chamber concertos and Béla Bartók's *Concerto for Orchestra*, which treats every instrumental choir within the ensemble as a solo virtuoso.

## connections

- [[continuio]] — the fundamental harmonic engine that supported early orchestral ensembles and keyboard soloists throughout the Baroque era.
- [[baroque-music]] — the historical period during which the concerto arose through early experiments by *Arcangelo Corelli* and *Antonio Vivaldi*.
- [[classical-music]] — the era that fused the concerto with balanced orchestral dramaturgy, expressive clarity, and *[[sonata-form]]*.
- [[sonata-form]] — the primary compositional structure adapted for first-movement double expositions in Classical solo concertos.
- [[counterpoint]] — the polyphonic compositional technique woven between soloists and tutti in complex Baroque concertos.
- [[italy]] — the geographic birthplace of both the concerto grosso and the virtuoso solo violin concerto.
- [[pyotr-ilyich-tchaikovsky]] — composer of celebrated Romantic virtuoso concertos for violin and piano that remain cornerstones of the concert repertoire.
- [[igor-stravinsky]] — modernist composer who transformed concerto conventions through neoclassical works such as the *Violin Concerto* and *Dumbarton Oaks*.
