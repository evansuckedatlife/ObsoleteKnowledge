## summary

**Liturgical music** encompasses all musical compositions created for use in religious services and worship. Born from the need to enhance prayer, praise, and ritual, it forms the backbone of Western classical music tradition. From Gregorian chants to polyphonic masses, liturgical works shaped the development of harmony, counterpoint, and large-scale musical form across centuries.

## you gotta know

- The oldest surviving liturgical tradition in the West: Gregorian chant, monophonic plainchant tied to Latin Catholic ritual.
- Liturgical music dominated composition through the Middle Ages and Renaissance; most great composers wrote for the church first.
- The mass — a complete musical setting of the unchanging parts of the Catholic service — became the primary large form.
- Motets: shorter polyphonic works, often set to sacred Latin texts, that showcased compositional ingenuity and evolved alongside the mass.
- Organ music rose to prominence as instrumental accompaniment and solo work within the liturgy.
- The Reformation splintered liturgical music practice: Protestant traditions developed chorale and hymn forms diverging from Catholic polyphony.
- Beyond Catholicism, liturgical music shaped Jewish cantorial traditions, Orthodox chant, and later Protestant sacred music.

## connections

- [[mass-form]] — the largest liturgical form, containing multiple movements for full choir and orchestra.
- [[motet]] — shorter polyphonic liturgical work, often for smaller choir or soloists.
- [[gregorian-chant]] — the foundational monophonic liturgical tradition of the Western church.
- [[sacred-music]] — the broader category encompassing all religious music, including non-liturgical types.
- [[polyphony]] — the harmonic technique essential to medieval and Renaissance liturgical composition.
- [[counterpoint]] — the compositional discipline refined through creating independent vocal lines in liturgical works.
