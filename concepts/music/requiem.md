## summary

A **Requiem** is a liturgical mass for the dead in the Christian tradition, set to Latin texts that express prayers for the deceased's soul. As a musical form, it became a major outlet for composers to create large-scale, emotionally profound works blending choir, soloists, and orchestra. From Renaissance polyphony to the Romantic era, the Requiem attracted some of the greatest composers, transforming a liturgical obligation into profound art.

## you gotta know

- The text derives from the Latin Mass for the Dead, centered on the *Dies Irae* (Day of Wrath), a fearsome meditation on divine judgment that inspired composers across centuries.
- *Palestrina's* polyphonic Requiem (16th century) established the Renaissance template for meditative, multi-voice writing.
- *Mozart's* Requiem (1791), left incomplete at his death and finished by Franz Süssmayr, is one of the most performed and celebrated examples—combining Classical elegance with emotional depth.
- *Brahms's* "German Requiem" (19th century) used German vernacular texts rather than Latin, emphasizing consolation over judgment.
- *Verdi's* Requiem treats the liturgical text with operatic grandeur, featuring dramatic choral sections and virtuosic soloists, blurring the line between opera and church music.
- The Requiem form allows composers to work at orchestral and vocal scales rarely demanded by standard liturgy, making it a prestige vehicle for large-scale instrumental writing.
- *Fauré's* Requiem (1887) presented a gentler, less apocalyptic vision than the traditional text, emphasizing peace and rest rather than divine wrath.
- The *Lacrimosa* (Weeping), from the Dies Irae section, has inspired countless musical settings and remains one of the most recognizable segments in the entire repertoire.
- Modern composers continue the tradition—*Britten's* War Requiem (1962) combined the traditional Latin Mass with English war poetry, showing the form's continuing relevance.
- Many Requiems blur the boundary between sacred liturgical work and concert music, performed as much in concert halls as in churches.
