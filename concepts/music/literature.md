## summary

**Literature and music** have been intertwined since antiquity, but operatic composers from the 18th century onward made literary adaptation a central creative practice. Playwrights, novelists, and poets provided narrative frameworks and emotional arcs that composers transformed into vocal music, creating a symbiotic relationship where great stories acquired new dimensions through melody and orchestration. This tradition continues to shape how audiences encounter both literature and music.

## you gotta know

- Opera emerged as the primary musical form for literary adaptation, with librettists (text writers) translating dramatic narratives into sung drama.
- Composers drew on mythology, fairy tales, historical events, and contemporary literature—anything with dramatic potential.
- *Benjamin Britten* famously adapted *Herman Melville*'s *Billy Budd* and Henry James's *The Turn of the Screw* into operas, respecting their narrative subtlety.
- *Modest Mussorgsky*'s *Boris Godunov* is based directly on *Alexander Pushkin*'s historical play, capturing the political intrigue and psychological depth of the original.
- *Richard Strauss*'s *Salome* faithfully follows *Oscar Wilde*'s decadent French play, translating its perverse eroticism into symphonic language.
- Literary works provide dramatic structure, character development, and thematic resonance that pure instrumental music cannot achieve alone.
- Symphonic poems and song cycles also draw on literature, translating poetry and prose into instrumental narratives.
- A libretto is not mere translation: composers and librettists must cut, compress, and reshape source material to fit operatic pacing and the demands of sung language.
- Great literary adaptations in music balance fidelity to the original text with the needs of musical form, creating something that honors both arts simultaneously.
- The relationship is symbiotic: literature gains new emotional dimensions through music, while composers find ready-made narrative scaffolding that frees them to explore pure musical invention.
