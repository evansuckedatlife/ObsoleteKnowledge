## summary

**Major-minor tonality** is the system of organizing pitch and harmony that dominated Western music from roughly 1650 to 1900 and persists widely today. It anchors all music to a central note and uses two primary scales—major (bright, stable) and minor (darker, more ambiguous)—to create harmonic and melodic unity. Understanding major-minor tonality is essential to grasping how classical, Baroque, and Romantic composers achieved both drama and coherence.

## you gotta know

- The major scale consists of a specific pattern of whole and half steps (W-W-H-W-W-W-H) that produces a stable, affirmative sound.
- The minor scale exists in three forms—natural, harmonic, and melodic—each altering the sixth and seventh scale degrees differently.
- A key (e.g., C major, A minor) is the tonal center and set of pitches; the key signature indicates which notes are consistently sharp or flat.
- Every major key has a relative minor key (sharing the same pitches but starting from a different note); C major and A minor share no accidentals.
- Harmonic function—the role of chords as tension-builders or resolution points—depends entirely on their relationship to the tonal center.
- Modulation (changing keys mid-piece) creates drama and structural interest while remaining within the major-minor system.
- The system collapsed in the early twentieth century when composers like Schoenberg and Debussy abandoned tonality for atonality and other systems.
