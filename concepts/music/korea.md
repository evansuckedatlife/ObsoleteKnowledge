## summary

**Korea** refers to the Korean Peninsula in East Asia, historically a unified kingdom but divided since 1953 into South Korea (Republic of Korea) and North Korea (Democratic People's Republic of Korea). As a cultural region, Korea has developed a rich musical heritage spanning millennia, from court music and folk traditions to contemporary styles. Korean classical music (*gugak*) remains one of East Asia's most sophisticated musical traditions, characterized by unique instruments, complex rhythmic systems, and expressive vocal techniques that reflect Korean aesthetics and spiritual life.

## you gotta know

- Korea's musical traditions include court music reserved for royal ceremonies, folk music for communal celebrations, and religious music for Buddhist and shamanic rituals.
- *Gugak* (Korean classical music) uses instruments like the gayageum (zither), geomungo (plucked string instrument), daegeum (bamboo flute), and janggu (double-headed drum).
- Korean music emphasizes *jeong* (feeling/emotion), often evoking subtle emotional states through non-Western approaches to tonality and microtonal inflection.
- The rhythmic system *jangdan* provides complex temporal frameworks, particularly in genres like *pansori* (narrative vocal music) and court dance music.
- Traditional Korean aristocratic court music was preserved through strict transmission lineages and is now protected as UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage.
- Modern Korea has produced globally influential contemporary music genres, from K-pop to film scores and fusion experiments.
- Korea's geographic and cultural position between China and Japan gave it a distinct musical identity while maintaining historical connections to both neighbors.
- The gayageum's 12 or more strings allow for microtonal bending and sliding pitches; playing technique emphasizes expressive control rather than rapid technical display.
- *Pansori* (the narrative vocal form) requires extraordinary lung capacity and emotional range; a single performance can last several hours, with a solo singer and supporting drummer (*buk* player) comprising the entire ensemble.
- Court music in Korea served diplomatic and ceremonial functions; specific repertoires were restricted to royal occasions, creating a class-based musical hierarchy.
- Shamanic music traditions in Korea (*mudang* rituals) coexist with classical traditions; these ritual performances employ different instruments, vocal styles, and spiritual purposes than court or folk music.
- The Korean music transmission system emphasizes transmission from teacher to student; many styles are designated as "Important Intangible Cultural Properties" with official masters responsible for training successors.
- North and South Korea developed divergent musical traditions after the 1950 division: South Korea maintained traditional *gugak* and developed K-pop, while North Korea emphasized state-sponsored performance and propaganda music.
