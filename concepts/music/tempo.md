## summary

**Tempo** is the speed or rate at which a musical beat unfolds, serving as the foundational temporal framework that dictates the duration and pacing of a performance. Historically articulated through expressive descriptive terminology in [[italy]] during the rise of [[baroque-music]] and [[classical-music]], it acquired mechanical precision during the [[industrial-revolution]] through the invention of the metronome. In music history and performance analysis, it is essential for discerning stylistic interpretation, navigating compositional instructions from *grave* to *presto*, and comparing rhythmic structures across traditions ranging from European art music to [[jazz]] and Indian [[tala]].

## you gotta know

- Standard performance speed is universally measured in beats per minute, abbreviated as *BPM*, which quantifies how many fundamental quarter-note pulses or metric subdivisions occur within sixty seconds.
- Traditional Western notation conveys speed using Italian verbal directives arranged systematically from slowest to fastest, notably *grave*, *largo*, *adagio*, *andante*, *moderato*, *allegro*, *vivace*, and *presto*.
- The mechanical metronome was patented in 1815 by *Johann Nepomuk Maelzel*, prompting *Ludwig van Beethoven* to become one of the earliest major composers to supply exact numerical metronome markings for his orchestral scores.
- Expressive elasticity of pace within a musical phrase is designated as *rubato*, a performance technique famously championed in nineteenth-century romantic keyboard repertoire by *Frédéric Chopin* to counterbalance rigid metric regularity.
- Gradual modifications of speed across a passage are directed by *accelerando* for quickening and *ritardando* or *rallentando* for deceleration, which are typically resolved by an *a tempo* indication restoring the primary pace.
- The twentieth-century modernist composer *Elliott Carter* pioneered metric modulation, a compositional technique where a proportional tempo change is achieved by pivoting on a shared note value between successive meters.
- In medieval mensural notation and early [[renaissance-music]], speed was governed by the concept of *tactus*, a steady physical pulse that anchored the temporal relationships of proportions like *tempus perfectum* without requiring abstract numeric markers.

## connections

- [[classical-music]] — Codified standardized Italian expressive descriptors to establish unambiguous performance speeds across increasingly large orchestral ensembles.
- [[baroque-music]] — Relied on the doctrine of the affections and conventional dance forms like the sarabande and gigue to dictate an unyielding, unified pulse throughout an entire movement.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — Stimulated precision clockwork engineering and mass manufacturing, providing the mechanical technology that enabled *Johann Nepomuk Maelzel* to popularize the metronome.
- [[italy]] — Provided the universal linguistic vocabulary for musical direction during the seventeenth century, cementing terms like *allegro* and *andante* as international standards.
- [[jazz]] — Reimagined rhythmic momentum through the swing subdivision of eighth notes, frequent double-time feels, and subtle phrase-level fluctuations during collaborative [[improvisation]].
- [[igor-stravinsky]] — Disrupted conventional expectations of uniform pacing in modernist works such as *The Rite of Spring* by combining shifting meters with abrupt, jarring alterations of pulse.
- [[tala]] — Serves as the rhythmic framework in [[hindustani-classical-music]], regulating cyclical patterns and relative speeds categorized into distinct tempo tiers known as *laya*.
- [[gregorian-chant]] — Embodied an early vocal tradition devoid of rigid metric pulses, where pace was dictated entirely by Latin speech accentuation and liturgical breath rather than measured beats.
