## summary

**Djembe** is a goblet-shaped hand drum from West Africa, traditionally played in Mali, Senegal, Guinea, and other countries throughout the region. Carved from a single piece of hardwood and topped with goatskin, the djembe produces three distinct tones depending on where and how it is struck—a deep bass from the center, a high pitch from the rim, and a slap in between. Originally reserved for griot musicians and trained percussionists, the djembe has become globally recognized and widely taught, making it the most familiar West African drum to international audiences.

## you gotta know

- The djembe's goblet shape and single stretched head allow for tonal variety rarely matched by other hand drums.
- Three primary tones: *bass* (heel of the hand, dead center), *slap* (fingers striking near center), and *tone* (high pitch at the rim).
- In West African ensembles, djembe drummers play both rhythmic patterns and improvisational solos, responding to and complementing griot vocalists.
- The djembe is traditionally associated with celebrations, rites of passage, storytelling, and community gatherings in West African cultures.
- Beginning in the 1960s, the djembe was adopted by world music enthusiasts and world-music teachers, spreading it globally to drum circles and classroom settings.
- Unlike some West African drums which are deeply sacred or require apprenticeship, the djembe became more accessible to outsiders, accelerating its international adoption.
- Modern djembe construction ranges from authentic handmade versions to mass-produced instruments, with significant variation in quality and cultural authenticity.
- Traditional djembe drumming patterns are learned through oral transmission and apprenticeship; young drummers spend years learning complex polyrhythmic patterns that interlace with other drummers.
- The physical technique of djembe playing involves specific hand positions and strike points to achieve each tone; the instrument is held upright or slightly tilted, not horizontally like some drums.
- Djembe ensembles typically include a lead drummer (*djembelaw*) who directs and improvises over supporting rhythmic patterns played by other drummers, creating dynamic dialogue.
- The international adoption of the djembe has led to fusion styles, mixing West African traditional patterns with influences from world music, jazz, and contemporary percussion.
- Djembe tuning is achieved by tightening rope or cord systems that hold the skin head; temperature and humidity affect tuning, requiring adjustment for optimal sound.
