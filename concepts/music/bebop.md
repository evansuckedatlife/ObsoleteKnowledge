## summary

**Bebop** (or *bop*) is a revolutionary jazz style that emerged in the early 1940s, pioneered by musicians like *Charlie Parker* and *Dizzy Gillespie*. Bebop rejected the dance-oriented swing of the big bands, instead emphasizing fast, complex improvisation over sophisticated harmonies, virtuosic technique, and harmonic innovation—transforming jazz from popular dance music into a serious art form.

## you gotta know

- Bebop built directly on *Louis Armstrong*'s improvisational breakthroughs but pushed them vastly further; rather than embellishing a clear melody, bebop soloists constructed entirely new melodic lines over chord changes, phrase by phrase.
- *Charlie Parker* (saxophone) and *Dizzy Gillespie* (trumpet) are the genre's founding figures; their recordings ("Ko-Ko," "Ornithology," "A Night in Tunisia") established the bebop vocabulary and sound.
- Bebop rejected the smooth, swing-era groove in favor of complex, syncopated rhythms and unexpected accents; tempos were often extremely fast, requiring extraordinary technical facility from musicians.
- Harmonically, bebop incorporated chromatic passing tones, altered chords (flat-5 substitutions, extended harmonies), and reharmonization—moving jazz harmonic language away from simple blues changes toward sophisticated chord progressions.
- Bebop was performed in small groups (trios, quartets, quintets) rather than big bands; this intimacy allowed for interactive, conversation-like improvisation between instruments.
- The movement faced criticism from swing-era musicians and critics who saw bebop as chaotic and pretentious; bebop musicians countered that they were advancing jazz as an art form requiring serious study.
- Bebop laid the foundation for all subsequent jazz styles—cool jazz, hard bop, free jazz, fusion—and established jazz as a form of musical and intellectual complexity rather than pure entertainment.
