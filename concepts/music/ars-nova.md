## summary

**Ars Nova** (Latin for "new art") was a transformative musical style and notation system that flourished across late medieval [[france]] and the Low Countries during the fourteenth century. Coined by composer and theorist *Philippe de Vitry* in his influential theoretical treatise written around 1322, the movement marked a decisive departure from the older, more rigid *Ars antiqua* tradition. By establishing revolutionary innovations in musical [[rhythm]], mensural notation, and secular [[polyphony]], the style granted musicians unprecedented expressive freedom. Its harmonic developments and compositional techniques transformed liturgical practice and laid the essential foundations of [[counterpoint]] that guided Western art music toward the early [[renaissance]].

## you gotta know

- *Guillaume de Machaut* served as the movement's preeminent composer and poet, crafting acclaimed masterworks in courtly lyric poetry and sacred polyphony while composing the monumental *Messe de Nostre Dame*, the earliest surviving complete setting of the [[mass-form]] Ordinary created by a single identifiable composer.
- Established revolutionary principles of mensural notation through seminal treatises by *Philippe de Vitry* and *Johannes de Muris*, which dismantled the restrictive rhythmic modes of the thirteenth century and allowed durations to be determined by specific note shapes rather than repetitive context.
- Legitimized duple meter alongside triple meter by granting equal validity to imperfect division and introducing smaller subdivisions like the minim and semiminim, creating precise time signatures and enabling intricate syncopation across independent voices.
- Championed the widespread structural technique of isorhythm, which organized individual vocal lines—most notably the foundational [[tenor]]—around an interlocking architecture of repeating rhythmic units called *talea* and recurring melodic segments called *color*.
- Cultivated courtly secular vocal music through the poetic formes fixes—specifically the *ballade*, *rondeau*, and *virelai*—which established standardized musical and textual structures for polyphonic chansons centered on chivalric romance and courtly devotion.
- Found a famous early vehicle in the interpolated manuscript of the *Roman de Fauvel*, a biting allegorical satire targeting political and ecclesiastical corruption that integrated dozens of newly composed polyphonic [[motet]] pieces reflecting the emerging aesthetic.
- Triggered intense ecclesiastical pushback, culminating in Pope John XXII's 1324 papal bull *Docta Sanctorum Patrum*, which harshly reprimanded composers for obscuring sacred liturgical texts with dizzying syncopations, rapid hockets, and worldly sensibilities.

## connections

- [[polyphony]] — provided the multi-voice compositional framework that fourteenth-century theorists expanded with greater rhythmic independence and voice-leading complexity.
- [[rhythm]] — underwent fundamental theoretical transformation through mensural signs, establishing proportional relationships and elevating duple meter alongside triple meter.
- [[motet]] — served as the primary genre for sophisticated isorhythmic experimentation, polytextual layering, and sharp political commentary.
- [[mass-form]] — achieved a historic milestone through Machaut's cyclical polyphonic setting of the liturgical Ordinary in the *Messe de Nostre Dame*.
- [[france]] — served as the intellectual and artistic heartland where Parisian scholars, aristocratic courts, and cathedral chapels developed the style.
- [[counterpoint]] — progressed toward modern voice-leading rules by integrating imperfect consonances like thirds and sixths alongside traditional perfect fifths and octaves.
- [[tenor]] — functioned as the foundational voice carrying the cantus firmus and governing the repeating rhythmic cycles of isorhythmic works.
- [[renaissance]] — inherited the precise rhythmic notation, text-setting strategies, and contrapuntal developments originated by fourteenth-century French masters.
