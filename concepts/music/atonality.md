## summary

**Atonality**, along with **atonal music**, defines a compositional practice and aesthetic in Western [[classical-music]] that deliberately avoids establishing a central pitch, tonic key, or traditional hierarchical harmonic structure. Developed in the early twentieth century primarily within [[germany]] and Austria as a defining breakthrough of musical [[modernism]], it represented the culmination of decades of progressive chromatic expansion following the [[renaissance]] and [[baroque-music]]. Composers embraced this idiom to mirror psychological alienation and expressive angst, casting aside major and minor scales in favor of unrestricted melodic and vertical intervals. Understanding this harmonic evolution is indispensable for analyzing twentieth-century concert literature, expressionist theater, and the subsequent development of systematic serial composition.

## you gotta know

- Formally pioneered in Vienna by *Arnold Schoenberg* and his *Second Viennese School* students *Alban Berg* and *Anton Webern*, who began systematically abandoning functional key signatures in works composed around 1908.
- Characterized by *Arnold Schoenberg* as the "emancipation of the dissonance," asserting that complex intervals such as tritones and major sevenths no longer required resolution into consonant triads within traditional [[tonality]].
- Marked by foundational early masterworks including *Arnold Schoenberg*'s *String Quartet No. 2*—whose final movement introduces a [[soprano]] singing "I feel the air of another planet"—as well as his expressionist song cycle *Pierrot Lunaire*.
- Synthesized into psychological theater by *Alban Berg* in his landmark [[opera]] *Wozzeck*, which merged free atonal expressive palettes with rigorous historical instrumental forms like [[sonata-form]], passacaglias, and variations.
- Created an aesthetic problem of structural unity over large temporal spans without functional cadences, motivating *Arnold Schoenberg* to invent the [[twelve-tone-technique]] in the 1920s to organize chromatic material into egalitarian rows.
- Formalized analytically by American music theorist *Allen Forte* in his 1973 text *The Structure of Atonal Music*, which established pitch-class set theory to categorize non-diatonic harmonic collections and unordered pitch groupings mathematically.
- Anticipated historically by nineteenth-century experiments in extreme chromaticism, such as *Franz Liszt*'s unresolving late miniature *Bagatelle sans tonalité* and the mystical synthetic sonorities of Russian composer *Alexander Scriabin*.

## connections

- [[tonality]] — the traditional hierarchical pitch system anchored by major and minor keys that atonal music was explicitly designed to dismantle.
- [[modernism]] — the overarching twentieth-century cultural and intellectual movement that encouraged European composers to break decisively with classical conventions and bourgeois tastes.
- [[classical-music]] — the historical Western art music lineage within which atonal innovations expanded harmonic vocabulary and challenged concert audience expectations.
- [[opera]] — the theatrical genre that demonstrated atonality's unique capacity for depicting psychological trauma, madness, and dark expressionist drama.
- [[counterpoint]] — the linear interaction of independent voices that served as an essential structural scaffold after functional harmonic cadences were abandoned.
- [[polyphony]] — the multi-voiced compositional fabric whose intricate melodic lines anchored complex musical textures in place of chordal accompaniment.
- [[germany]] — the central European cultural and artistic sphere, alongside Austria, where the Second Viennese School developed and disseminated atonal theory.
- [[igor-stravinsky]] — contemporary modernist composer who championed an alternative rhythmically driven aesthetic before eventually adopting twelve-tone serial methods in his late career.
