## summary

The **blues scale** (most commonly designating the **minor blues scale** or **hexatonic blues scale**) is a six-note melodic pitch collection characterized by the inclusion of an altered diminished fifth degree alongside minor pentatonic intervals. Originating within the African American oral musical traditions of the rural American South during the late nineteenth century, it became the primary melodic and tonal architecture underpinning early country blues, work songs, field hollers, and sacred [[spirituals]]. Its deliberate exploitation of chromatic friction, unresolved dissonance, and microtonal blue notes fundamentally challenged traditional European diatonic [[tonality]]. Today, it stands as an indispensable foundational grammar for expressive [[improvisation]] across [[jazz]], rock, soul, and modern global popular music.

## you gotta know

- Built in its standard minor form by adding a flatted fifth or diminished fifth interval—the quintessential *blue note*—to the minor pentatonic scale, producing the characteristic hexatonic formula: root, minor third, perfect fourth, diminished fifth, perfect fifth, and minor seventh (1–♭3–4–♭5–5–♭7).
- Frequently played directly over dominant seventh chords in standard twelve-bar blues progressions, creating an iconic acoustic tension between the soloist's melodic minor third and the underlying accompaniment's major third, a clash that defined the harmonic identity of modern American popular music.
- Possesses a corresponding major variant derived from the major pentatonic scale by inserting a flatted third passing tone (1–2–♭3–3–5–6), producing a brighter, swinging, gospel-inflected sound widely used by blues pianists, country guitar pickers, and Southern rock bands.
- Formed the foundational improvisational vocabulary of seminal [[jazz]] pioneers like cornetist [[louis-armstrong]] and bandleader [[duke-ellington]], who translated vocal slurs and barrelhouse piano licks into brass idioms and sophisticated big band arrangements in Chicago and [[new-york-city]].
- Arose historically out of the field hollers, acoustic work chants, and spiritual traditions developed by enslaved and emancipated African Americans across the rural southern [[united-states]] during the [[reconstruction-era]] following the [[american-civil-war]].
- Performed in authentic folk, vocal, and guitar contexts not with rigid equal temperament pitches, but through fluid microtonal inflections—frequently called *neutral thirds* or *worried notes*—that float dynamically in the expressive microtonal spaces between minor and major intervals.
- Codified during the mid-twentieth century within jazz theory, chord-scale methodology, and educational play-along materials by pedagogues such as *Jamey Aebersold*, establishing the hexatonic scale pattern as the ubiquitous introductory vehicle for novice improvisers.

## connections

- [[jazz]] — adopted the scale's characteristic blue notes, chromatic passing tones, and bent inflections as its primary soloing vernacular.
- [[tonality]] — challenged traditional Western diatonic functional harmony by treating diminished fifths and minor-major clashes as expressive consonances.
- [[improvisation]] — serves as the universal gateway framework for teaching students spontaneous melodic invention across blues, rock, and jazz.
- [[louis-armstrong]] — revolutionized jazz performance by translating the vocal bends and microtonal phrasing of the blues scale into virtuoso trumpet technique.
- [[duke-ellington]] — orchestrated rustic blues scale inflections into extended, highly structured twentieth-century concert works and big band suites.
- [[reconstruction-era]] — the critical post-war historical period in the southern [[united-states]] during which vernacular blues traditions began crystallizing.
- [[rhythm]] — interacts closely with syncopation, backbeats, and swung shuffle grooves to establish the distinctive rhythmic momentum of the blues.
