# Blues

## summary

The **blues** is an American musical genre born from African-American experience in the post-Civil War South, characterized by a fixed repeating chord progression (typically twelve bars), a minor-inflected scale, and lyrics expressing hardship, resilience, desire, and social commentary. Emerging in the late 19th century from spirituals, work songs, and folk traditions, the blues became foundational to jazz, rock, and modern popular music.

## you gotta know

- The *twelve-bar blues* is the structural backbone: a repeating 12-bar harmonic cycle (typically I-I-I-I-IV-IV-I-I-V-IV-I-V or similar variants) over which singers and musicians improvise and compose.
- Blues lyrics follow a call-and-response pattern reflecting African musical traditions; the typical blues couplet states a problem in the first line, repeats it, then offers a resolution or punchline in the third line.
- The *blues scale* uses *bent* or *flattened* third and seventh scale degrees (the "blue notes"), creating a minor-tinged sound that distinguishes blues from major-key popular music.
- Early blues was performed by solo singers with acoustic guitar (Robert Johnson, Blind Lemon Jefferson, Muddy Waters) or piano (W.C. Handy); the genre later expanded to include full bands and electric instruments.
- The blues is fundamentally improvisational; while the harmonic structure is fixed, the melody and specific phrases vary from performance to performance, allowing for emotional expression and spontaneity.
- Blues lyrical themes center on love, betrayal, economic hardship, racism, and social injustice; the genre became a voice for African-American experiences historically silenced in mainstream culture.
- Blues influenced virtually all subsequent American popular music: jazz musicians built bebop on blues changes, rock and roll took the twelve-bar structure and amplified it, soul and R&B directly descended from blues traditions.
