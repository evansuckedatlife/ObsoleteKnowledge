## summary

**Medieval music** encompasses the musical traditions of Europe from roughly the 6th to the 15th centuries, a period defined by the dominance of the Catholic Church in musical life. Spanning from single-voiced *Gregorian chant* to complex polyphonic motets, medieval composers developed the notation systems and harmonic principles that would underpin all European concert music. The era reflects a world where sacred music was virtually synonymous with serious composition.

## you gotta know

- Began with *Gregorian chant* (also called *plainchant*): monophonic, Latin liturgical singing organized in modes rather than major/minor tonality.
- *Pope Gregory I* (traditionally credited, though modern scholars debate the attribution) is the nominal source of the Gregorian tradition.
- Gradually incorporated *polyphony*: by the 12th century, parallel voices singing different lines emerged (organum), leading to the *motet*.
- The *motet* became the highest art form: a sacred polyphonic form in which each voice sang different Latin texts simultaneously, creating harmonic density.
- Composers like *Josquin des Prez* and *Palestrina* perfected Renaissance polyphony, which grew from medieval foundations.
- *Hildegard of Bingen*, a 12th-century German nun and visionary, composed liturgical chants and texts that survive as rare examples of female medieval authorship.
- The development of musical notation allowed composers to specify exactly which notes to sing, revolutionizing the transmission of music.
- Medieval churches became the first concert halls; the acoustics of cathedrals became an essential part of composers' creative vocabulary.
- The modes used in medieval music (Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian) provided the organizational system from which Western tonality later evolved.
