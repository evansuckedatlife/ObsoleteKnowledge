## summary

**Soprano** is the highest standard female voice classification in Western classical and operatic music, characterized by brightness, agility, and projection in the upper register. Spanning roughly two octaves from middle C to the C two octaves above, the soprano voice has been the principal female vocal type in opera since the form's inception. Its prominence in leading roles reflects both its acoustic prominence and its capacity for lyrical expression and virtuosic display.

## you gotta know

- The term derives from the Italian "sopra" (above), indicating it sits above other voices.
- Sopranos historically sang the highest line in choral music, carrying the melody while other voices provided harmonic support.
- In opera, sopranos traditionally sing protagonist roles (heroines, queens, ingénues); the soprano voice commands dramatic and musical centrality.
- Subtypes exist: coloratura soprano (extremely agile and high, for rapid passagework); lyric soprano (warm and expressive); dramatic soprano (powerful and darker-toned).
- Famous soprano arias include *Verdi*'s "Sempre libera" (*La Traviata*), *Bellini*'s "Casta diva" (*Norma*), and *Puccini*'s "O mio babbino caro" (*Gianni Schicchi*).
- The soprano voice complements the *tenor* (the principal male voice) in duets and romantic scenarios, though *mezzo-sopranos* and *altos* perform many soprano repertoire pieces with darker timbre.
- Voice classification depends on range, resonance, and timbre; a singer's voice type can shift with age and training.
- Historically, *castrato* singers (which became controversial by the 18th century) occupied the soprano/alto register in all-male church choirs.
