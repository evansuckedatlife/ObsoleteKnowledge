## summary

**Alto** (from the Italian *altus*, "high") is the lowest female voice type in the standard four-voice singing classification, with a range roughly from the F below middle C to the F two octaves above. The alto voice combines the depth and richness of lower vocal registers with the agility required for classical singing, and has played crucial roles in choral, operatic, and liturgical music across centuries.

## you gotta know

- Alto is strictly a female voice category in the classical system; the male equivalent at this low register is either baritone or bass, depending on range.
- The alto range is deeper and warmer than the soprano or mezzo-soprano, suited to earthy, grounded, or maternal character roles in opera.
- In Baroque music, alto roles were historically sung by castrati—male singers surgically treated before puberty to retain high voices—giving the parts remarkable virtuosity and range.
- The traditional four-part choral texture (SATB: soprano, alto, tenor, bass) places the alto as the second-highest harmonic line, often carrying inner melodic material.
- Famous alto/contralto roles include Orpheus in Monteverdi's *L'Orfeo* and the Composer in Richard Strauss's *Ariadne auf Naxos*.
- In liturgical contexts, particularly in Gregorian chant and Catholic polyphony, the alto line provides harmonic foundation and countermelodic balance.
- The contralto (or true alto) voice is rarer and more highly prized than mezzo-soprano for its dramatic power and darkness of tone.
