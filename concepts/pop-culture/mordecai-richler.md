## summary

**Mordecai Richler** was a celebrated Canadian novelist, essayist, and polemical satirist known for his caustic wit, unapologetic social commentary, and vivid depictions of Jewish life in mid-twentieth-century Montreal. Rising to international prominence with his breakout 1959 novel *The Apprenticeship of Duddy Kravitz*, Richler captured the abrasive, ambitious immigrant experience along Montreal's Saint Urbain Street. His prolific output bridged literary fiction, biting journalism, children's literature, and Hollywood screenwriting, cementing his status as one of Canada's foremost twentieth-century men of letters.

## you gotta know

- Born and raised in the working-class, largely Jewish neighborhood of Mile End along Saint Urbain Street in Montreal, Quebec, an environment that served as the primary backdrop for his most famous novels.
- Breakthrough masterpiece *The Apprenticeship of Duddy Kravitz* (1959) depicts an ambitious, unscrupulous youth whose grandfather teaches him that "a man without land is nobody," leading Duddy to ruthlessly pursue property acquisition in the Laurentians.
- Wrote *St. Urbain's Horseman* (1971), a Governor General's Literary Award-winning novel exploring the psychological turmoil of expatriate film director *Jake Hersh* and his obsession with an avenging cousin hunting Nazi war criminals.
- Garnered late-career acclaim with *Barney's Version* (1997), a tragicomic, unreliable first-person memoir of television producer *Barney Panofsky* confronting three failed marriages, alcoholism, the unsolved murder of his best friend *Boogie*, and encroaching Alzheimer's disease.
- Wrote popular children's fiction, notably creating the *Jacob Two-Two* series, inspired by his own young son who had to say everything twice to be heard in a large family, beginning with *Jacob Two-Two Meets the Hooded Fang* (1975).
- Known for provocative, satirical political journalism targeting Quebec nationalism, notably in his controversial 1992 book *Oh Canada! Oh Quebec! Requiem for a Divided Country*, which ignited furious debate over provincial language laws and antisemitism.
- Spent extended periods living as an expatriate writer in Paris and London, absorbing European literary trends and contributing screenplays and criticism before permanently returning to Montreal in 1972.
- Multiple of his works were adapted into major motion pictures, including *The Apprenticeship of Duddy Kravitz* (1974), starring *Richard Dreyfuss*, and *Barney's Version* (2010), starring *Paul Giamatti*.

## connections

- [[realism]] — primary literary mode utilized in his detailed, unvarnished chronicling of postwar urban ethnic enclaves and familial conflict.
- [[american-literature]] — significant stylistic and thematic counterpart, sharing critical territory with contemporaries like *Philip Roth* and *Saul Bellow*.
- [[england]] — home to Richler during his influential expatriate years in London, where he worked as a freelance journalist and television writer.
- [[france]] — European destination of his youthful post-collegiate sojourn in Paris, following in the footsteps of the Lost Generation.
- [[world-war-ii]] — formative historical event lingering as memory, trauma, and moral touchstone throughout his novels and essays.
- [[modernism]] — aesthetic tradition that influenced his non-linear narrative framing, biting irony, and skeptical exploration of moral identity.
