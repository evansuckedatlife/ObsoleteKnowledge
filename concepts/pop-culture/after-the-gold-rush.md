## summary

**After the Gold Rush** is the third studio album by Canadian-American singer-songwriter *Neil Young*, released in August 1970 on Reprise Records. Written partly as an unproduced screenplay soundtrack and recorded primarily in a makeshift basement studio in Topanga Canyon, California, the record blends delicate country-folk acoustic ballads with searing hard-rock guitar tracks. It remains one of the defining singer-songwriter statements of the 1970s, capturing the disillusionment and ecological anxiety of the post-counterculture era while solidifying *Young's* critical and commercial stature in the [[united-states]].

## you gotta know

- Contains "Southern Man", a blistering critique of racism in the American South, which famously provoked *Lynyrd Skynyrd* to write the rebuttal "Sweet Home Alabama".
- Features the melancholic acoustic track "Only Love Can Break Your Heart", written for *Graham Nash* following his breakup with *Joni Mitchell*, which became *Young's* first solo top-40 hit single.
- The title track "After the Gold Rush" presents an apocalyptic, science-fiction-inflected vision of environmental destruction that culminates in "flying Mother Nature's silver seed to a new home in the sun."
- Includes the hard-hitting rocker "Don't Let It Bring You Down", known for *Young's* distinctive double-dropped-D acoustic guitar tuning, alongside the jaunty, waltz-tempo piano track "Birds".
- Features a then-eighteen-year-old *Nils Lofgren* on piano, despite *Lofgren* primarily being a guitarist with little prior experience on keyboards.
- Includes a country-flavored cover of *Don Gibson's* 1958 standard "Oh, Lonesome Me", drastically slowed down into an introspective, mournful dirge.
- Recorded largely in *Young's* basement home studio in Topanga Canyon with backing contributions from *Crazy Horse* members *Danny Whitten* and *Ralph Molina*, alongside *Jack Nitzsche* and *Greg Reeves*.
- Inspired by an unproduced screenplay by *Dean Stockwell* and *Herb Bermann*, whose surreal, apocalyptic narrative shaped the thematic atmosphere of the songs.
