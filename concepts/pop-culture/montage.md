## summary

**Montage** is the cinematic technique of selecting, editing, and piecing together separate sections of film to form a continuous whole, generating meaning and emotion through the juxtaposition of images rather than within individual frames. Originating during the early development of [[silent-film]] through the innovative narrative cutting of *D. W. Griffith* in the [[united-states]], the practice revolutionized visual storytelling by demonstrating that cinema could manipulate psychological tension, spatial continuity, and temporal progression. The technique subsequently inspired the revolutionary film theorists of the [[soviet-union]], who transformed narrative editing into a dynamic aesthetic of ideological conflict and dialectical collision, laying the cornerstone for twentieth-century visual culture and modern screen literacy.

## you gotta know

- The modern Hollywood montage sequence typically uses rhythmic cuts set to music to compress narrative time, depicting arduous training regimens, romantic courtship, or extensive investigations in a matter of minutes.
- *D. W. Griffith* pioneered cross-cutting and parallel editing in landmark silent films such as *The Birth of a Nation* and *Intolerance*, establishing that alternating between concurrent actions could build unprecedented dramatic suspense.
- The famous *Kuleshov effect*, demonstrated by Soviet filmmaker *Lev Kuleshov*, proved that viewers derive more emotional and intellectual meaning from the interaction of two sequential shots than from a single shot in isolation.
- *Sergei Eisenstein* codified the theory of dialectical [[soviet-montage]], proposing that the collision of opposing visual elements generates a synthetic, higher concept in the mind of the spectator.
- *Eisenstein* distinguished five distinct methods or categories of cutting: metric, rhythmic, tonal, overtonal, and intellectual montage, using the slaughter of striking workers juxtaposed with a butchered bull in *Strike* as a prime [[visual-metaphor]].
- The Odessa Steps sequence in *Eisenstein's* masterpiece *Battleship Potemkin* remains cinema's most famous demonstration of metric and rhythmic cutting, stretching perceived time through fragmented angles of descending soldiers and fleeing citizens.
- French film critics like *André Bazin* later contrasted montage with deep-focus cinematography and [[mise-en-scene]], arguing that excessive cutting manipulates reality rather than preserving the spatial ambiguity and temporal integrity of physical space.
- Early cinema pioneers in [[france]], including *Georges Méliès*, initially stumbled upon jump cuts by accident when camera jams replaced omnibus carriages with hearses, foreshadowing the deliberate spatial disruptions of modern cinematic cutting.

## connections

- [[film-editing]] — the overarching craft of post-production assembly of which montage is the foundational artistic discipline.
- [[d-w-griffith]] — early American director whose cross-cutting innovations in silent epics demonstrated the emotional power of alternating shots.
- [[soviet-montage]] — avant-garde film movement that radicalized editing into a revolutionary vehicle for intellectual and political ideology.
- [[silent-film]] — the artistic era whose reliance on purely visual storytelling catalyzed rapid breakthroughs in editing grammar.
- [[mise-en-scene]] — the theatrical arrangement of elements within a single shot, often viewed as the philosophical opposite of montage editing.
- [[visual-metaphor]] — symbolic meaning produced when unrelated shots are edited together to evoke an abstract concept.
- [[modernism]] — cultural movement whose fragmented aesthetic and machine-age sensibilities deeply informed twentieth-century editing theory.
