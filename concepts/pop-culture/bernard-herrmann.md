## summary

**Bernard Herrmann** was an acclaimed twentieth-century American composer and conductor whose revolutionary scores redefined cinematic scoring and the psychological architecture of dramatic sound in the [[united-states]]. Renowned for his close, volatile partnership with director *Alfred Hitchcock*, he rejected the sprawling late-Romantic leitmotif conventions of Hollywood in favor of brief, repetitive harmonic cells and striking orchestral colorations. His sonic innovations across psychological thrillers, radio dramas, and dark character studies fundamentally elevated the expressive relationship between musical composition and visual suspense.

## you gotta know

- Entered the national spotlight through radio collaboration with *Orson Welles* and *The Mercury Theatre on the Air*, conducting the live dramatic score during the notorious 1938 broadcast of *The War of the Worlds*.
- Earned an Academy Award nomination for his very first feature film score in Welles's ground-breaking masterpiece *Citizen Kane* in 1941.
- Won the Academy Award for Best Original Score that same year for *The Devil and Daniel Webster*, utilizing innovative recorded sound manipulation on magnetic tape.
- Composed the legendary shrieking, percussive string glissandi for the shower murder sequence in Hitchcock's 1960 thriller *Psycho*, scored entirely for a string orchestra to mirror the stark black-and-white cinematography.
- Constructed the hypnotic, cyclical arpeggios and habanera rhythms of *Vertigo* to evoke profound obsession, romantic longing, and crippling psychological disorientation.
- Emphasized unusual instrumentation to match thematic content, such as twin theremins in *The Day the Earth Stood Still* and dense bassoons, contrabass clarinets, and tubas in *Journey to the Center of the Earth*.
- Severed his decade-long creative alliance with *Alfred Hitchcock* over the musical direction of the Cold War thriller *Torn Curtain* when the studio demanded a commercial pop soundtrack.
- Completed his final film score for *Martin Scorsese's* gritty psychological neo-noir *Taxi Driver*, recording the haunting, bluesy saxophone motif just hours before his death in December 1975.
