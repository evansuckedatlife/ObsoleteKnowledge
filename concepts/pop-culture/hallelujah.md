## summary

**Hallelujah** is a transcendent folk-rock ballad written by Canadian poet and songwriter *Leonard Cohen*, originally released on his 1984 studio album *Various Positions*. Marked by its lilting 6/8 waltz cadence, sparse melodic progression, and audacious interplay of sacred scripture and sensual heartbreak, the song languished in obscurity before rising to worldwide acclaim through iconic covers. Today it occupies a singular place in popular culture as a secular hymn, continually performed at ceremonies of grief, solace, and celebration.

## you gotta know

- Composed by *Leonard Cohen* across several tortuous years in New York City's *Royalton Hotel*, where he drafted dozens of alternate verses before narrowing the lyrical sequence for *Various Positions*.
- Rejected initially by *Columbia Records* executive *Walter Yetnikoff*, who refused to issue *Various Positions* commercially within the [[united-states]], claiming Cohen's poetic folk aesthetic lacked modern radio appeal.
- Reworked in 1991 by *John Cale* of *The Velvet Underground* for the tribute album *I'm Your Fan*, who stripped away Cohen's synthesizers, arranged the signature arpeggiated piano part, and selected more sensual alternate verses sent by Cohen via fax.
- Elevated to mythic status in 1994 by *Jeff Buckley* on his album *Grace*, whose fragile, soaring vocal performance and electric guitar arrangement cemented the track as an emotional touchstone of alternative music.
- Reached global mainstream audiences through the 2001 animated film *Shrek*, which featured Cale's recording in the narrative sequence and a version by *Rufus Wainwright* on the multi-platinum soundtrack.
- Invokes vivid Old Testament imagery in its opening stanzas, recounting the secret chord struck by *King David* to soothe *King Saul*, David gazing down at *Bathsheba* bathing on her rooftop, and *Samson* betrayed and shorn by *Delilah*.
- Concludes by embracing the duality of spiritual redemption and human disillusionment, declaring that a broken and humbled praise remains worthy of utterance.
