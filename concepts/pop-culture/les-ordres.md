## summary

**Les Ordres** (released in English as **The Orders**) is a landmark 1974 Canadian political drama film written and directed by Michel Brault. Set against the backdrop of the 1970 October Crisis in Montreal, the film dramatizes the civilian impact of the federal government invoking the *War Measures Act*, which suspended basic civil liberties and led to the arbitrary arrest of hundreds of innocent citizens. Celebrated for its blend of direct cinema realism and emotional depth, it stands as one of the defining masterpieces of Quebec and Canadian cinema.

## you gotta know

- Dramatizes the real-life consequences of the October Crisis (*Crise d'Octobre*) of autumn 1970, during which the Canadian federal government under Prime Minister Pierre Elliott Trudeau invoked the peacetime *War Measures Act* following the separatist FLQ kidnappings of British trade commissioner James Cross and Quebec labor minister Pierre Laporte.
- Focuses on five fictionalized composite characters drawn directly from comprehensive post-crisis interviews with roughly fifty actual detainees who were arbitrarily rounded up in dawn raids, imprisoned without legal charges, and subjected to systemic psychological intimidation.
- Won the prestigious Best Director Award (*Prix de la mise en scène*) at the 1975 Cannes Film Festival for Michel Brault, who shared the prize with Costa-Gavras and became the first Canadian filmmaker in history to receive the honor.
- Opens with a distinctive alienating prologue in which lead actors Jean Lapointe, Hélène Loiselle, Claude Gauthier, Guy Provost, and Guy L'Écuyer break the fourth wall, look into the lens, state their real names, and introduce the historical persons their roles synthesize.
- Employs a pioneering visual aesthetic alternating between gritty monochrome footage evoking direct cinema newsreels and unexpected shifts into vivid color sequences that emphasize the characters' interior vitality and fragile humanity amidst state repression.
- Showcases the devastating performance of Jean Lapointe as Clermont Boudreau, a modest unionized factory worker falsely suspected of sedition whose traumatic strip-search and solitary confinement symbolize the dehumanizing nature of carceral overreach.
- Highlights the institutional complicity and bureaucratic indifference of local police forces and prison guards who justify illegal detainment and abuse by mindlessly repeating that they are simply carrying out official directives.
- Swept the 1975 Canadian Film Awards by claiming four top prizes, including Best Feature Film, Best Director, Best Original Screenplay, and Best Actress for Hélène Loiselle's understated portrayal of Clermont's steadfast wife Marie.

## connections

- [[leonard-cohen]] — celebrated Montreal poet and balladeer whose work during the 1960s and 1970s reflected the cultural ferment and existential unease of contemporary Quebec.
- [[mise-en-scene]] — visual arrangement of stark prison cells and cramped domestic flats that reinforces the overwhelming theme of political confinement.
- [[realism]] — aesthetic philosophy inherited from Quebec *cinéma direct* that privileges unvarnished observation and documentary authenticity over melodramatic contrivance.
- [[film-editing]] — rhythmic cutting between multiple simultaneous arrests across Montreal that captures the widespread paranoia of state surveillance.
- [[camera-movement]] — mobile handheld cinematography pioneered by Michel Brault that generates claustrophobia inside interrogation cells and squad cars.
- [[cold-war]] — broader international era of geopolitical tension and national security crackdowns during which civil liberties were curtailed across Western democracies.
