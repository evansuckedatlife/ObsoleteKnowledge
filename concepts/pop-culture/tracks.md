## summary

**Tracks**, frequently catalogued in scholarly reference as **Tracks (novel)**, is an acclaimed 1988 novel by Native American author *Louise Erdrich*, forming the foundational historical anchor of her multi-generational North Dakota reservation saga. Set between 1912 and 1924 during a period of catastrophic land loss, economic exploitation, and tuberculosis epidemics, the novel alternates between the perspectives of the traditional elder *Nanapush* and the religious fanatic *Pauline Puyat*. It holds an enduring place in *[[american-literature]]* for its polyphonic storytelling, exploration of Anishinaabe survival, and tragic confrontation with systemic American *[[colonialism]]*.

## you gotta know

- Revolves around the fierce and mysterious *Fleur Pillager*, a powerful medicine woman believed to have survived drowning in Lake Matchimanito through her supernatural bond with the horned water monster *Misshepeshu*.
- Alternates narrative chapters between two contrasting storytellers: the wise, resilient Anishinaabe tribal elder *Nanapush*, who talks continuously to fend off death, and the self-hating, assimilate-seeking mixed-blood woman *Pauline Puyat*.
- Features a famous sequence where Fleur works in an Argus butcher shop, repeatedly out-gambling male employees at poker before they assault her and lock her in a meat locker, preceding a freak tornado that wrecks the town.
- Chronicles the catastrophic dispossession of Indigenous land spurred by the Dawes General Allotment Act, unpaid lumber fees, and aggressive exploitation by commercial timber companies in the *[[united-states]]*.
- Depicts the terrifying descent of *Pauline Puyat* into self-mortifying, warped *[[christianity]]*, leading her to join a Catholic convent under the name Sister Leopolda and murder Napoleon Morrissey in the woods.
- Climaxes when Fleur purposefully saws through the green trunks of her ancestral forest so that the arrival of commercial logging equipment triggers a thunderous domino collapse of the Pillager woods.
- Framed as a spoken oral legacy told by Nanapush to his adopted granddaughter *Lulu Nanapush* to instill pride and preserve ancestral identity after her return from a government boarding school.

## connections

- [[american-literature]] — stands as a cornerstone achievement of the Native American Renaissance and late twentieth-century historical fiction.
- [[colonialism]] — dramatizes the predatory alienation of Indigenous ancestral territory, federal allotment bureaucracy, and enforced cultural assimilation.
- [[christianity]] — represented through the masochistic, distorted Catholic zealotry adopted by Pauline Puyat as she renounces her heritage.
- [[tragedy]] — suffuses the narrative through tribal starvation, devastating epidemic illness, and the irreversible loss of native old-growth timberland.
- [[united-states]] — provides the historical federal backdrop whose reservation laws and boarding school systems drive the characters' struggles.
- [[realism]] — balances unsparing historical documentation of reservation poverty with traditional Anishinaabe mythic and spiritual beliefs.
- [[modernism]] — informs the novel's fractured, multi-perspective narrative structure and complex, unreliable first-person narration.
