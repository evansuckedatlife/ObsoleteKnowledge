## summary

A **close-up** is a foundational cinematographic framing technique that renders a subject, most frequently a human face or a critical object, in tight scale to fill the vast majority of the screen. Originating during the experimental dawn of [[silent-film]] in the late nineteenth century and refined into a dramatic storytelling device during the 1910s, it transformed screen acting from stage-bound theatricality into an art of nuanced psychology. It remains one of the primary building blocks of modern visual storytelling, directing audience attention, heightening emotional identification, and magnifying physical details invisible in long shots.

## you gotta know

- Defined primarily as a camera shot that frames the subject tightly, typically showing a person from the neck or shoulders up to capture micro-expressions.
- Early cinema pioneers such as the *Brighton School* filmmaker *George Albert Smith* in *The Grandma's Reading Glass* (1900) introduced early examples to highlight isolated objects.
- Director *[[d-w-griffith]]* revolutionized the technique during his tenure at *The Biograph Company*, using facial shots to reveal inner psychological states rather than mere visual novelties.
- Pioneering silent actresses like *Lillian Gish* modified their performance style specifically for the shot, replacing grandiose stage melodrama with subtle eye shifts and micro-movements.
- Theorists of [[soviet-montage]] like *Béla Balázs* later celebrated the technique as the disclosure of the human soul and the creation of a "physiognomy" unique to cinema.
- Variations include the medium close-up (chest up) and the extreme close-up (ECU), famously weaponized by directors like *Sergio Leone* to display intense eyes during tense shootouts in the [[western-film]] genre.
- Often paired systematically with [[cross-cutting]] to alternate between a character's reaction and the escalating events surrounding them.

## connections

- [[silent-film]] — the historical era where the shot evolved from an optical trick into a storytelling staple.
- [[film-editing]] — the overarching discipline that juxtaposes tight frames with wider establishing shots to structure cinematic narrative.
- [[cross-cutting]] — an editing technique frequently intercut with facial reaction shots to build psychological tension.
- [[d-w-griffith]] — the pioneering director who established the shot as a core component of narrative film grammar.
- [[mise-en-scene]] — the broader arrangement of visual elements within the frame that works alongside shot scale.
- [[soviet-montage]] — the theoretical film movement that analyzed how tight framing functions within visual juxtaposition.
- [[camera-movement]] — the physical translation of the camera, often tracking inward to transition dynamically into a tight framing.
