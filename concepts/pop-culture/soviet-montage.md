## summary

**Soviet montage** is a film theory and technique that emerged from 1920s Russian cinema, asserting that meaning is created not within individual shots but through the collision and juxtaposition of images. Pioneered by directors like *Sergei Eisenstein*, the theory posited that editing was the central act of filmmaking — cutting together shots of different subjects could create emotional responses and ideological meanings independent of the shots' individual content. This revolutionary approach influenced cinema worldwide and redefined what editing could accomplish.

## you gotta know

- Emerged from post-Revolutionary Soviet cinema as both artistic experiment and political instrument, meant to mobilize audiences ideologically.
- Lev Kuleshov demonstrated experimentally (the "Kuleshov effect") that viewers attribute meaning to image sequences based on juxtaposition, not the shots themselves.
- Sergei Eisenstein codified montage theory in *Battleship Potemkin* (1925), where editing creates emotional and intellectual impact — the Odessa Steps sequence exemplifies this principle.
- Montage operates on collision: a shot of a face + a shot of food triggers the emotional reading "hunger," though neither shot explicitly depicts it.
- Soviet montage emphasized rapid, percussive editing as opposed to smooth, invisible transitions — the cut itself should be *felt* as an act of meaning-making.
- The theory influenced French avant-garde, German Expressionism, and eventually Hollywood — even filmmakers who rejected montage theory's principles acknowledged its centrality to cinema language.

## connections

- [[battleship-potemkin]] — the definitive work of montage cinema; its Odessa Steps sequence remains the canonical example.
- [[sergei-eisenstein]] — the primary theorist and practitioner of Soviet montage, whose writings codified editing as cinema's essential art.
- [[film-editing]] — the broad technical and artistic practice that montage theory placed at cinema's conceptual center.
- [[silent-film]] — the medium in which montage theory developed, since dialogue could not carry meaning that editing had to create.
- [[camera-movement]] — complementary to montage; Murnau's mobile camera contrasts with Eisenstein's collisional editing as alternative approaches to meaning.
- [[russian-revolution]] — the historical and ideological context motivating Soviet cinema's political urgency and experimental form.
- [[buster-keaton]] — contemporary of Eisenstein; Keaton's logical, spatial editing parallels montage principles through different cultural and comic contexts.
