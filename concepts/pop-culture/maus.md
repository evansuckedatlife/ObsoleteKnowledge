## summary

**Maus** is a landmark graphic memoir created by American cartoonist *Art Spiegelman* and serialized between 1980 and 1991. The narrative depicts the author interviewing his father, *Vladek Spiegelman*, a Polish Jew and Holocaust survivor living in *Rego Park*, *New York City*. Celebrated for its emotional honesty, intricate framing, and historical gravity, it became the first graphic novel to win a *Pulitzer Prize* in 1992, decisively elevating comics into the sphere of serious literature.

## you gotta know

- Awarded a special *Pulitzer Prize* in Letters in 1992, validating the graphic novel format as a legitimate vehicle for complex historical testimony and literary art.
- Employs an extended zoomorphic conceit where different nationalities and ethnicities are depicted as distinct animals: Jews appear as mice, Germans as predatory cats, Poles as pigs, Americans as dogs, and French citizens as frogs.
- Intertwines two parallel timelines: *Vladek Spiegelman*'s terrifying survival of the ghettos and *Auschwitz* during [[world-war-ii]], alongside Artie's tense, guilt-ridden interactions with his aging, cantankerous father in postwar America.
- Incorporates *Prisoner on the Hell Planet*, an early expressionist underground comic written by Spiegelman in 1973 that brutally details the suicide of his mother, *Anja Spiegelman*, and his own psychiatric confinement.
- Centers intensely on generational trauma, particularly the haunting phantom of Artie's deceased older brother *Richieu Spiegelman*, who was poisoned by his aunt *Tosia* to escape deportation to the gas chambers and to whom the second volume is dedicated.
- Features a radically self-reflexive second volume ("Time Flies") where the cartoonist depicts himself as a human wearing a mouse mask, paralyzed by artistic paralysis and commercial exploitation while sitting atop a mound of decomposing corpses.
- Concludes on a poignant, devastating note as an exhausted Vladek turns to sleep after recounting his liberation and reunion with Anja, mistakenly addressing Artie as his deceased son *Richieu*.

## connections

- [[world-war-ii]] — the catastrophic global conflict and systematic genocide that forms the historical core of Vladek's harrowing memories.
- [[american-literature]] — the broader literary canon fundamentally transformed by Spiegelman's innovative non-fiction comic form.
- [[visual-metaphor]] — the central aesthetic strategy utilizing anthropomorphic animal identities to interrogate racial essentialism and Nazi ideology.
- [[new-york-city]] — the contemporary postwar setting where Artie visits his father's home to document his oral history.
- [[germany]] — the fascist state whose totalitarian regime and death camps orchestrate the destruction of European Jewry.
- [[tragedy]] — the overarching dramatic framework capturing collective historical devastation and intimate domestic grief.
- [[realism]] — the rigorous psychological verisimilitude Spiegelman employs, refusing to sanitize Vladek's personal bigotry, stinginess, and neuroses.
