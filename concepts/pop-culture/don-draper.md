## summary

**Don Draper**, born **Dick Whitman**, is the enigmatic protagonist of the acclaimed AMC period drama series *Mad Men*, created by *Matthew Weiner*. Portrayed by *Jon Hamm*, Draper serves as the brilliant, charismatic, and emotionally hollow creative director at the fictional advertising agency *Sterling Cooper* in [[new-york-city]] during the turbulent 1960s. His dual identity, masking an impoverished upbringing behind an immaculate mid-century executive facade, stands as one of television's defining portraits of identity, alienation, and the disillusionment of the American Dream.

## you gotta know

- Portrayed across seven seasons by *Jon Hamm*, winning widespread critical acclaim for capturing the debonair charm and simmering self-destruction of an advertising executive in 1960s [[new-york-city]].
- Born into rural poverty as **Dick Whitman** to a deceased prostitute and abusive father Archibald, he escaped by enlisting in the Korean War and assumed the stolen identity of his commanding officer, Lieutenant Donald Draper, after a fatal artillery accident.
- Renowned for spellbinding advertising pitches that weaponize nostalgia, vulnerability, and human longing, most famously the slide projector pitch for the *Kodak Carousel* ("nostalgia—it's delicate, but potent"), the "It's toasted" slogan for *Lucky Strike*, and a disastrously confessional pitch to *Hershey's* chocolate.
- Married suburban former model Betty Hofstadt (*January Jones*) and fathered three children—Sally, Bobby, and Gene—before their bitter divorce; he later impetuously married his French-Canadian secretary Megan Calvet (*Jessica Paré*).
- Formed a deeply complex creative and emotional mentorship with copywriter Peggy Olson (*Elisabeth Moss*), whom he promoted from secretary after recognizing her raw talent during the *Belle Jolie* lipstick campaign.
- Orchestrated the midnight founding of the boutique agency *Sterling Cooper Draper Pryce* alongside partners Roger Sterling, Bertram Cooper, and Lane Pryce to escape corporate absorption by British conglomerate *PPL*.
- The series concludes with Draper abandoning his executive life for a cross-country nomadic journey that ends at a California spiritual retreat; meditating on a coastal cliff, he smiles peacefully as the camera cuts to the real-world 1971 *Coca-Cola* "Hilltop" television advertisement ("I'd Like to Buy the World a Coke"), implying his creative genius reclaimed the counterculture for corporate consumerism.

## connections

- [[new-york-city]] — the Madison Avenue setting where Draper crafts marketing illusions while navigating bohemian Greenwich Village and suburban Westchester.
- [[cold-war]] — the geopolitical backdrop of existential dread, civil defense drills, and the Cuban Missile Crisis that mirrors Draper's internal anxiety.
- [[existentialism]] — philosophical framework underpinning Draper's persistent belief that identity is arbitrary and life is a continuous struggle to invent oneself.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — the societal upheaval of the 1960s that slowly challenges the monocultural, segregated boardrooms of Sterling Cooper.
- [[downton-abbey]] — fellow prestige television drama of the early 2010s that explored aristocratic societal shifts while *Mad Men* examined corporate mid-century capitalism.
- [[realism]] — aesthetic and narrative commitment to psychological depth, period historical accuracy, and moral ambiguity over tidy melodrama.
- [[modernism]] — cultural movement reflected in the evolving aesthetics, interior decor, and fractured psychology of the series.
