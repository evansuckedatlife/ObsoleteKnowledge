## summary

**Virtue** in philosophy is a settled excellence of character—a stable disposition to act, think, and feel in ways that are admirable and conducive to human flourishing. *Aristotle* defined virtue as the mean between extremes (courage as the mean between cowardice and recklessness) and argued that virtue is cultivated through habit and practice until the virtuous person acts rightly "without deliberation." Virtue-centered ethics has experienced a renaissance in modern philosophy as an alternative to rule-based and consequence-based approaches, emphasizing that ethics is ultimately about becoming a good *person*, not just following rules or maximizing outcomes.

## you gotta know

- *Aristotle* identified two types of virtue: *intellectual virtues* (wisdom, understanding, prudence—cultivated through teaching) and *moral virtues* (courage, temperance, justice, liberality—cultivated through habituation and practice).
- The *doctrine of the mean* states that virtue lies between vice and excess: courage is the mean between cowardice (deficiency) and rashness (excess); generosity is the mean between stinginess and profligacy. This applies to feelings as well as actions: feeling anger at appropriate times, toward appropriate people, in appropriate amounts, is virtue; chronic rage or total passivity are both vicious.
- For *Aristotle*, virtue is not innate but acquired through repeated practice: we become brave by performing brave acts, temperate by practicing self-control, just by acting justly. The virtuous person eventually performs virtuous acts effortlessly and with pleasure, not from duty or fear.
- *Plato* treated virtue as unified and intellectual: the virtuous person possesses knowledge of the Good (a transcendent form), and if they truly know what is good, they will do it; vice results from ignorance, not malice or weakness of will.
- The *Stoics* held that virtue is the *only* true good and the sole path to happiness (*eudaimonia*); external things (health, wealth, reputation) are "preferred indifferents"—nice to have but not essential to flourishing. A virtuous slave is happier than a vicious king.
- The *Cynics* embraced virtue in the form of freedom from desire: they rejected social conventions, lived ascetically, and cultivated virtue as liberation from dependence on others' approval—an extreme form of virtue as *self-mastery*.
- *Epicurus* and his followers defined virtue not as asceticism but as *prudent pleasure-seeking*: a virtuous life involves choosing pleasures that lead to lasting satisfaction (simple food, reliable friendships, philosophical conversation) over fleeting indulgences (excess food, empty social climbing) that bring pain.
- Modern *virtue ethics* (*Philippa Foot*, *Rosalind Hursthouse*, *Alasdair MacIntyre*) has revived Aristotelian ethics as a third way: virtue ethics asks "What kind of person should I be?" (not "What rules should I follow?" or "What outcomes should I maximize?"), making character central to moral philosophy.

## connections

- [[aristotle]] — the founder of virtue ethics and the doctrine of the mean.
- [[virtue-ethics]] — the ethical theory centered on virtue and character.
- [[plato]] — proposed virtue as unified knowledge of the Good.
- [[stoicism]] — the school that made virtue the only true good.
- [[cynicism]] — a school emphasizing virtue as freedom from social dependence.
- [[epicureanism]] — virtue as prudent, moderate pleasure-seeking.
- [[kant]] — a deontologist who rejected virtue ethics in favor of duty-based ethics.
- [[utilitarianism]] — a consequentialist theory that competes with virtue ethics for moral guidance.
