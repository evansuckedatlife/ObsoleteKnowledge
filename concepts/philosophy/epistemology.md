## summary

**Epistemology**, or the theory of knowledge, is the major branch of *[[philosophy]]* dedicated to investigating the nature, sources, scope, and limits of human knowledge and justified belief. Developed from foundational debates in *[[ancient-greece]]* through the *[[enlightenment]]* to contemporary analytic thought, it examines what distinguishes genuine knowledge from mere opinion or true belief. Its inquiries address central problems such as philosophical skepticism, the structure of justification, and the relative epistemic roles of reason and sensory experience.

## you gotta know

- Traditionally defined knowledge in the Western tradition following *[[plato]]*'s dialogue *Theaetetus* as "justified true belief" (JTB), establishing a tripartite standard that prevailed for millennia.
- Shaken in 1963 by *Edmund Gettier*'s landmark paper, which provided counterexamples demonstrating that a belief can be both justified and true by sheer luck without counting as genuine knowledge.
- Polarized during the early modern era between *[[rationalism]]*, which argued that substantive knowledge derives primarily from innate reason (championed by *[[descartes]]* and *Spinoza*), and *[[empiricism]]*, which insisted that all knowledge originates in sensory observation.
- Revolutionized by *[[descartes]]*, who employed methodological skepticism in his *Meditations on First Philosophy*, doubting all sensory inputs to arrive at the indubitable foundation of the *cogito* (*cogito, ergo sum*).
- Pushed to radical skeptical conclusions by *[[david-hume]]*, who argued that inductive generalizations lack rational justification and that causal connections cannot be observed, only inferred through psychological habit.
- Synthesized by *[[kant]]* in his *Critique of Pure Reason*, which proposed a "Copernican revolution" uniting rationalist structure with empirical content, famously claiming that thoughts without content are empty and intuitions without concepts are blind.
- Explores structural models of epistemic justification, primarily foundationalism (beliefs rest on basic, self-justifying axioms) versus coherentism (beliefs are justified by mutual support within an interlocking web).
- Features contemporary developments such as naturalized epistemology (pioneered by *W. V. O. Quine*), virtue epistemology, contextualism, and social epistemology, which investigates how knowledge is created and transmitted across communities.

## connections

- [[empiricism]] — the epistemological stance holding that sensory experience and observation are the primary foundations of all genuine knowledge.
- [[rationalism]] — the philosophical view that reason, intellect, and innate ideas provide knowledge independent of sensory experience.
- [[descartes]] — the French philosopher whose radical method of doubt sought an unshakable epistemic foundation for scientific knowledge.
- [[david-hume]] — the Scottish philosopher whose rigorous skeptical empiricism famously challenged induction and the reality of causal connections.
- [[kant]] — the German thinker who resolved the debate between rationalism and empiricism through transcendental idealism.
- [[plato]] — the Athenian philosopher who distinguished between changing opinion (*doxa*) and immutable knowledge (*episteme*).
- [[logic]] — the formal discipline supplying the principles of valid inference, deduction, and reasoning indispensable for epistemic justification.
- [[aristotle]] — the ancient thinker whose *Posterior Analytics* laid out the first formal axiomatic theory of scientific demonstration.
