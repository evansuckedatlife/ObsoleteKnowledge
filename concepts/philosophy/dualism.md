# Dualism

## summary

**Dualism** is the metaphysical thesis that reality consists of two fundamentally different substances: the *physical* (matter, space, time, physical laws) and the *mental* (consciousness, qualia, subjective experience, thought). *René Descartes* formalized substance dualism in the 17th century (mind and body are separate substances interacting at the pineal gland), and it remains philosophically influential because consciousness feels categorically distinct from objective physical properties. Yet dualism faces the "interaction problem": if mind and body are separate substances, how do they causally influence each other? How does a decision (mental) move your arm (physical)?

## you gotta know

- *Descartes* was the founder of modern substance dualism: he argued that the mind (*res cogitans*—thinking substance) is immaterial, private, and accessible only to the subject ("I think therefore I am"), while the body (*res extensa*—extended substance) is material, public, and governed by physics.
- Dualism seems to match intuition: your subjective experience of red is not obviously a physical property of neurons; your conscious thoughts feel like they arise from something over and above chemistry and electromagnetism.
- The **interaction problem** is the central objection: if mind and body are truly separate substances, how can the mental cause the physical (your decision causes your arm to move)? And how can the physical cause the mental (light hitting your retina causes the sensation of red)? Physical causation seems closed—no room for the mental to influence anything.
- *Occasionalism* (e.g., *Malebranche*) attempted to solve the problem by arguing God orchestrates every mind-body interaction: when you decide to move your arm, God moves it; the appearance of causal interaction is an illusion.
- *Epiphenomenalism* accepts that the physical causes the mental, but denies the reverse: consciousness is a causally inert byproduct of brain activity (like the whistle on a steam engine—it makes noise but doesn't affect the engine's operation). This seems to make consciousness useless, which is counterintuitive.
- Modern physics and neuroscience have made dualism harder to defend: every mental phenomenon (emotion, decision, belief) correlates with brain states; we can induce sensations and alter mood with drugs; consciousness seems entirely dependent on brain function. Dualists must concede that the mental is supervenient on the physical.
- **Property dualism** (a weaker version) grants that only one substance exists (the physical), but argues that some of its properties (consciousness, subjective experience) are irreducibly mental and cannot be reduced to physics—an attempt to preserve dualism's insights while accepting physicalism.
- Some philosophers have revived dualism in light of *quantum mechanics*: if quantum indeterminacy and observer effects are genuine, consciousness might play a causal role in wavefunction collapse—though most physicists reject this "consciousness causes collapse" interpretation.
