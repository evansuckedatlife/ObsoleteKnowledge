## summary

**Freedom** is a core value across Western philosophy, but understood differently in different intellectual traditions and contexts. For *existentialists* like *Sartre*, freedom is radical, vertiginous, and inescapable — the fundamental human condition of having to choose and create oneself without any predetermined essence or divine guidance. For *Kant*, freedom means rational autonomy: the capacity to legislate universal moral law for oneself through reason, independent of mere appetite and desire. Political freedom (liberty from interference) differs from metaphysical freedom (absence of causal determination) and existential freedom (conscious choice). Freedom remains philosophically complex and contested: what constraints are compatible with genuine freedom? Can we be free within social systems? Is freedom compatible with causality?

## you gotta know

- *Sartre* claimed humans are "condemned to be free" — we must choose our values and make ourselves, with no predetermined nature to fall back on.
- Existentialist freedom entails radical responsibility; we cannot blame society, fate, or human nature for our choices — we are what we choose.
- The concept of *bad faith* describes the existentialist sin: pretending we lack freedom by hiding behind roles, institutions, or claims of determinism.
- *Kant's* freedom is autonomy: the rational capacity to legislate universal moral laws that override mere inclination or desire.
- Freedom as a political concept (freedom of speech, conscience, assembly) is distinct from metaphysical freedom (free will) and existential freedom (radical choice).
- Philosophers debate whether freedom is compatible with *determinism* (compatibilism) or requires that the future be genuinely open (libertarianism).

## connections

- [[existentialism]] — a twentieth-century philosophical movement treating freedom as fundamental to human existence and the source of authentic meaning.
- [[jean-paul-sartre]] — existentialist philosopher who made radical, unchosen freedom central to his philosophy and ethics.
- [[kant]] — defined moral freedom as rational autonomy: the capacity to legislate universal moral law through reason.
- [[bad-faith]] — the existentialist concept describing the inauthentic denial of one's freedom through self-deception and role-playing.
- [[free-will]] — the metaphysical question underlying much political and philosophical debate about whether humans have genuine freedom.
- [[moral-responsibility]] — logically requires freedom; only free agents can be morally responsible for their actions.
- [[determinism]] — the opposing view claiming freedom is illusory and the future is entirely fixed by prior causes.
