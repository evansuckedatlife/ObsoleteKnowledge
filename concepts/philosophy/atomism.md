## summary

**Atomism** is the foundational philosophical doctrine that all physical reality and natural phenomena in the universe are composed of indivisible, eternal particles moving through empty space or void. Originated in [[ancient-greece]] by the fifth-century BCE thinkers *Leucippus* and *Democritus*, the system offered a strictly naturalistic solution to the paradoxes of change raised by the Eleatic school and became a cornerstone of [[pre-socratic-philosophy]]. Later adapted by *Epicurus* to liberate humanity from irrational dread of divine intervention and immortal torment, it established an enduring tradition of philosophical [[materialism]] that profoundly influenced the development of natural science.

## you gotta know

- Formulated systematically by *Democritus* of Abdera and his shadowy mentor *Leucippus*, asserting that the cosmos consists entirely of microscopic, unchangeable bodies called *atoma* (literally "uncuttables") colliding, interlocking, and rebounding within the infinite void (*kenon*).
- Integrated into moral philosophy by *Epicurus*, who used physical atomism to demonstrate that nature operates without supernatural intervention, thereby freeing humanity from the fear of punitive gods and establishing peace of mind (*ataraxia*) as life's highest goal.
- Systematised for Roman readers by *Lucretius* in his six-book didactic poem *De Rerum Natura* (*On the Nature of Things*), whose rediscovery in a German monastery in 1417 sparked a sweeping scientific and intellectual transformation across the [[renaissance]] and the [[enlightenment]].
- Postulated the crucial mechanism of the atomic swerve (known in Latin as the *clinamen*), an unpredictable, infinitesimal deviation from straight downward trajectories introduced by *Epicurus* to explain both how atoms initially collide to form compound bodies and how moral agents possess genuine [[free-will]].
- Vehemently rejected by [[plato]] and [[aristotle]], who dismissed the existence of an empty void and argued instead for teleological design, formal causes, and continuous elementary substances composed of geometric forms or prime matter.
- Established the groundbreaking epistemological division between primary properties (inherent atomic attributes such as shape, size, mass, and motion) and secondary qualities (sensory phenomena such as colour, taste, and temperature, which exist purely "by convention").
- Maintained an infinite multiplicity of worlds (*kosmoi*) that are spontaneously generated, endure, and eventually disintegrate within the boundless void, directly anticipating modern cosmological theories of multiverses and non-teleological cosmic evolution.

## connections

- [[materialism]] — the metaphysical position that physical matter constitutes fundamental reality, historically inaugurated by the mechanistic worldview of the atomists.
- [[pre-socratic-philosophy]] — the intellectual movement that sought natural, non-theological principles (*archai*) for the universe, culminating in the atomic synthesis of *Democritus*.
- [[stoicism]] — the rival Hellenistic philosophy that rejected mechanical atomism in favour of a pantheistic, rational cosmos guided by divine providence and continuous physical tension.
- [[ancient-greece]] — the civilisational context where early speculative physics emerged to resolve the philosophical crisis between Parmenidean permanence and Heraclitean flux.
- [[aristotle]] — the ancient authority whose rigorous arguments against empty space and for teleological physics eclipsed atomism for nearly two millennia.
- [[free-will]] — the central philosophical dilemma of moral agency and determinism, famously preserved in atomist ethics via the indeterminate atomic swerve.
- [[lucretius]] — the Roman poet whose eloquent exposition of Epicurean natural philosophy preserved the most detailed surviving classical account of atomist mechanics.
- [[renaissance]] — the era of intellectual rebirth during which the rediscovery of Epicurean and Lucretian manuscripts revitalised European natural philosophy and mechanical science.
