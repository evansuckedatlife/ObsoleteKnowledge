# David Hume

## summary

**David Hume** (1711–1776) was a Scottish Enlightenment philosopher whose radical empiricism and skeptical questioning of causation, morality, and religion profoundly shaped modern philosophy. Applying empirical reasoning to domains where rationalism had previously dominated, Hume demonstrated that fundamental beliefs about cause-and-effect, the self, and divine necessity cannot be rationally justified by experience alone, forcing later thinkers to reckon with the limits of human knowledge.

## you gotta know

- Hume's central argument about causation was revolutionary: we never observe "causation" itself, only the constant conjunction of events (A always followed by B); our belief in causal necessity is a *habit of mind*, not a rational conclusion from experience.
- He applied this skeptical method to the self, arguing that introspection reveals no unified "self," only a "bundle of perceptions" in constant flux; personal identity is not a metaphysical fact but a pragmatic fiction created by memory and resemblance.
- On morality, Hume argued that reason alone cannot motivate action; morality must be grounded in sentiment (sympathy, benevolence, and fellow-feeling), not rational principle—a radical departure from rationalist ethics.
- His *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* presented sophisticated critiques of the design argument for God's existence; though published posthumously, these dialogues showed that belief in divine providence cannot be rationally justified from observation of nature's disorder and suffering.
- Hume's *fork*—distinguishing between *relations of ideas* (mathematical and logical truths, knowable *a priori*) and *matters of fact* (empirical claims, knowable only through experience)—became foundational to modern epistemology and philosophy of science.
- *Immanuel Kant* famously said Hume "interrupted my dogmatic slumber" and inspired Kant's critical philosophy, though Kant tried to rescue metaphysics from Humean skepticism.
- Hume was a naturalist; he applied empirical observation and psychological reasoning to explain how the human mind works, essentially founding modern psychology and cognitive science.
