## summary

The **Categorical Imperative** is the supreme moral principle formulated by the German philosopher *Immanuel Kant* in his 1785 treatise *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*. Unlike hypothetical imperatives that command actions conditionally as means to an end, this unconditional command binds all rational agents regardless of their personal desires or inclinations. It forms the core foundation of [[deontological-ethics]] and serves as a landmark standard in modern Western moral philosophy.

## you gotta know

- Formulated by *Immanuel Kant* in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) and expanded in the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) as the single unconditional moral law binding all rational beings.
- Distinct from hypothetical imperatives, which take the conditional form "if you want X, do Y"; the categorical imperative commands duties unconditionally regardless of desires, emotions, or consequences.
- The first and most famous formulation is the Formula of Universal Law: act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law.
- The second major formulation is the Humanity Formulation (or Formula of the End in Itself): act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end.
- The third formulation involves the Kingdom of Ends: all rational beings must view themselves as both lawgivers and subjects in an ideal community of autonomous agents bound by universal moral laws.
- Distinguishes between perfect duties (strict prohibitions against actions whose maxims cannot even be conceived as universal laws without contradiction, such as lying or breaking promises) and imperfect duties (obligations to pursue commendable ends, like cultivating one's talents and helping others).
- Famously applied in the notorious thought experiment of the inquiring murderer at the door, where *Kant* argued in an essay responding to *Benjamin Constant* that telling a lie to a murderer seeking a friend is morally impermissible because truthfulness is an unconditional duty.
- Argues that moral worth stems solely from actions performed out of duty (*aus Pflicht*) through a good will, rather than out of natural inclination, fear of punishment, or anticipated pleasure.

## connections

- [[deontological-ethics]] — the broader moral framework centered on duty that is grounded directly in this universal moral command.
- [[kant]] — the Königsberg philosopher who formulated this unconditional moral principle across his critical ethical writings.
- [[critique-of-metaphysics]] — his epistemological project that eliminated theoretical proofs of God and freedom to make room for moral faith rooted in the moral law.
- [[enlightenment]] — the intellectual movement emphasizing human reason and intellectual autonomy that shaped this rationalist ethical system.
- [[david-hume]] — the Scottish empiricist whose claim that reason is slave to the passions provoked *Kant* to establish an objective, purely rational moral law.
- [[free-will]] — the indispensable practical presupposition without which the moral obligation demanded by the imperative would be impossible.
- [[virtue-ethics]] — the rival ancient ethical tradition focused on character and flourishing that *Kant* subordinated to strict duty.
