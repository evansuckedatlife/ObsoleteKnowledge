## summary

**Deontological Ethics**, derived from the Greek word *deon* meaning duty or obligation, is a normative ethical theory holding that the morality of an action is based on whether that action itself adheres to rules and duties rather than the goodness of its consequences. Developed extensively in the European [[enlightenment]] by thinkers like *Immanuel Kant*, it stands as one of the three primary frameworks of moral philosophy alongside consequentialism and [[virtue-ethics]]. Its principles establish non-negotiable moral obligations, individual rights, and strict prohibitions against treating human beings as mere instruments.

## you gotta know

- Derives its name from the Greek *deon*, meaning duty, obligation, or necessity, and asserts that actions are intrinsically right or wrong regardless of outcomes.
- Fundamentally opposes consequentialist theories such as utilitarianism by arguing that a good outcome can never justify an intrinsically immoral act.
- Most famously systematized by *Immanuel Kant*, who argued that morality must be grounded purely in practical reason and centered on the [[categorical-imperative]].
- Features modern contractarian reformulations, notably by *T. M. Scanlon* in *What We Owe to Each Other*, which grounds moral duties in principles that no rational person could reasonably reject.
- Developed into threshold deontology and agent-relative obligations by contemporary philosophers like *W. D. Ross*, who introduced the concept of *prima facie* duties (such as non-maleficence, fidelity, and reparation) that can conflict in complex situations.
- Influenced political and rights theory through *John Rawls*, who utilized deontological principles in *A Theory of Justice* to defend the lexical priority of liberty and the veil of ignorance.
- Frequently tested in ethical thought experiments like the trolley problem, where pure deontology prohibits pushing a heavy person off a footbridge to save five people, because intentionally using a person as a barrier violates their inherent rights.
- Distinguishes sharply between agent-relative duties (rules prescribing what a specific person may or may not do directly) and agent-neutral duties (general requirements regarding world states).

## connections

- [[categorical-imperative]] — the supreme foundational principle that dictates universal moral duty within the *Kantian* system.
- [[kant]] — the foremost philosopher of the tradition who grounded moral law in autonomous reason and a good will.
- [[virtue-ethics]] — the major competing tradition dating back to *Aristotle* that evaluates moral character rather than compliance with universal rules.
- [[critique-of-metaphysics]] — the theoretical limitation of speculative reason that established moral practical reason as the true domain of unconditional obligation.
- [[enlightenment]] — the historical era that sought universal, rational foundations for ethics independent of religious dogma.
- [[freedom]] — the fundamental concept necessary for moral accountability, since an agent must be capable of autonomous self-legislation.
- [[free-will]] — the metaphysical agency required to freely choose duty over bodily desires and psychological inclinations.
