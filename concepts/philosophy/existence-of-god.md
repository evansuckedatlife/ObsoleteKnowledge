## summary

The **existence of God** is the central question of natural theology and metaphysics: whether a divine being exists, and if so, on what grounds can such existence be demonstrated or reasoned about. Philosophers have offered proofs, refutations, and positions ranging from dogmatic faith to agnosticism and atheism.

## you gotta know

- The *ontological argument* (esp. *Anselm* and *Descartes*): God's concept as the greatest conceivable being entails existence, since existence is a perfection; Kant objected that existence is not a predicate.
- The *cosmological argument* (esp. *Thomas Aquinas*'s Five Ways): the universe's existence requires a first cause or necessary being, identified as God; modern versions debate whether the universe needs external causation.
- The *teleological argument* (design): the apparent order and purpose in nature point to a divine designer; *William Paley*'s watchmaker analogy likened the universe to a precision instrument.
- The *moral argument*: the objectivity of morality requires a transcendent source (God) to ground values and duties; critics argue morality can be grounded in reason, evolution, or social contract.
- *Hume*'s critique of design: natural selection and chance, not divine intent, explain apparent order; empiricist objections question whether order even requires a designer.
- *Kant*'s critical position: God's existence cannot be proven by reason alone; it is a regulative idea necessary for practical moral reasoning, not theoretical knowledge—separating the noumenal (God) from the phenomenal (world we know).
- The problem of evil: the existence of suffering and evil seems incompatible with an omnipotent, omniscient, all-good God—one of the strongest contemporary challenges; theodicy attempts to reconcile evil with God's perfection.
- Modern atheistic arguments (*Bertrand Russell*): if everything requires a cause, what caused God? The burden of proof lies on the believer; absence of evidence is often evidence of absence in scientific reasoning.
- Contemporary philosophy increasingly abandons "proof" as the standard: the debate centers on which worldview—theistic or secular—better explains reality and human experience.
