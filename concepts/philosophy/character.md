## summary

**Character** denotes the stable constellation of moral dispositions, habits, and practical virtues that determine how an individual perceives, deliberates, and acts over a lifetime. In classical antiquity and modern virtue theory, character forms the central foundation of ethics, contrasting sharply with rule-based deontology or consequentialist calculations of utility. Understanding character explains why ethical excellence relies on long-term cultivation and habituation rather than isolated moments of decision.

## you gotta know

- Forms the primary theoretical subject of *Nicomachean Ethics* by [[aristotle]], who identified moral virtue as a settled state of character acquired through deliberate habituation.
- Translates the ancient Greek term *ethos*, from which the modern discipline of ethics derives its name.
- Distinguished in classical thought between virtuous disposition, continent self-restraint, incontinent weakness of will, and ingrained vice.
- Central to classical [[confucianism]], which emphasizes moral self-cultivation and ritual propriety to develop the character of the exemplary person.
- Connects directly to issues of [[compatibilism]] and [[free-will]], as thinkers debate whether moral agents can be held responsible for actions that flow inexorably from their settled dispositions.
- Challenged in modern experimental psychology and moral philosophy by situationism, which argues that situational cues influence behavior far more powerfully than stable character traits.

## connections

- [[virtue-ethics]] — the ethical tradition that makes the cultivation of character its central normative goal.
- [[aristotle]] — philosopher who systematized character development as the habituation of the golden mean between vices of excess and deficiency.
- [[confucianism]] — Eastern philosophical tradition centered on lifelong moral self-cultivation and exemplary conduct.
- [[stoicism]] — Hellenistic school asserting that virtuous character alone constitutes true happiness and self-sufficiency.
- [[compatibilism]] — theory suggesting agents can be held morally accountable when actions express their inner character and desires.
- [[plato]] — thinker who mapped individual character virtues directly to the psychological faculties of the tripartite soul.
- [[kant]] — philosopher who contrasted duty and good will against reliance on natural character dispositions.
