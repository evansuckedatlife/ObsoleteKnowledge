## summary

The **Pythagorean theorem** states that in a right triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides (a² + b² = c²). Associated with *Pythagoras*, it ranks among the most famous mathematical discoveries in history and symbolizes the marriage of mathematical proof and philosophical insight—the rational order underlying reality.

## you gotta know

- The theorem is attributed to *Pythagoras* (6th century BCE), though evidence suggests earlier Babylonian and Indian mathematicians knew it; ancient proofs likely involved geometric dissection, not algebraic notation.
- *Euclid* provided the definitive proof in *The Elements* (Proposition 47, Book I), using rigorous geometric reasoning; this proof exemplified the axiomatic-deductive method that became the gold standard for mathematical demonstration.
- The theorem is the foundation of Euclidean geometry and trigonometry; it enables the distance formula, defines perpendicularity, and underlies countless engineering and architectural applications.
- Philosophical significance: Pythagoras and his followers believed numbers and geometric ratios revealed the ultimate structure of reality; the theorem embodied this rational cosmic order.
- Pythagorean mysticism: the school attributed spiritual and cosmological meaning to numbers; harmonic ratios (based on the theorem's structure) supposedly governed musical intervals and celestial motion.
- Incommensurability crisis: Pythagoreans discovered that the diagonal of a unit square (√2) is irrational—not a ratio of integers; this shattered their vision of a purely rational universe and triggered a foundational crisis in mathematics.
- Over 400 distinct proofs exist: geometric dissections, algebraic manipulations, coordinate geometry, and even dynamic/calculus-based approaches—testifying to its centrality.
- Modern significance: the theorem remains central to vector spaces, complex numbers, and non-Euclidean geometries; it is the prototype of mathematical proof and beauty.
