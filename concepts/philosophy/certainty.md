## summary

**Certainty** is an epistemic state characterized by the absence of all doubt, the complete warrant of a belief, or an indubitable truth claim that cannot possibly be false. Originating in the debates of [[ancient-greece]] between dogmatists and Pyrrhonian or Academic skeptics, it became the central obsession of modern Western [[philosophy]] following the foundational quest of *René Descartes*. From foundationalism and radical skepticism to contemporary fallibilism, the pursuit and critique of this standard has driven the development of [[logic]], epistemology, and the philosophy of science.

## you gotta know

- Contested in [[ancient-greece]] by skeptics such as *Pyrrho* of Elis, who advocated suspension of judgment (*epoche*) to achieve tranquility (*ataraxia*), and *Carneades* of the Academic skepticism tradition, who developed a doctrine of probability (*pithanon*) in place of unattainable truth.
- Formulated as the cornerstone of modern epistemology by *René Descartes* in *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641), where he employed universal methodical doubt to locate an indubitable foundation.
- Anchored by *Descartes* in the *cogito, ergo sum* ("I think, therefore I am"), establishing the thinking subject's existence as the bedrock immune to the deception of an evil demon.
- Distinguished in epistemological literature between subjective certainty (a psychological conviction or unshakable personal feeling that a proposition is true) and objective certainty (an epistemic property where a proposition is guaranteed to be true or maximally warranted).
- Undermined by the empiricist skepticism of *David Hume*, who argued that relations of ideas (such as mathematical tautologies) permit demonstrative truth, but matters of fact rely on induction, which lacks rational justification.
- Re-evaluated in the critical philosophy of *Kant*, whose [[critique-of-metaphysics]] restricted indubitable theoretical knowledge to synthetic *a priori* judgments regarding phenomenal experience, discarding claims about transcendent realities.
- Analyzed posthumously by *Ludwig Wittgenstein* in *On Certainty* (1969), who responded to *G. E. Moore*'s common-sense defense of hands by arguing that foundational beliefs form the hinge propositions around which doubt turns, functioning as the ungrounded framework of our world-picture rather than propositions verified through empirical testing.
- Displaced in twentieth-century philosophy of science and physics by fallibilism and indeterminism, notably through *Karl Popper*'s falsificationism and the probabilistic nature of modern physical laws.

## connections

- [[descartes]] — the French rationalist who made the methodical search for an indubitable truth the starting point of modern philosophy.
- [[logic]] — the formal study of valid inference that supplies deductive guarantees and mathematical precision.
- [[critique-of-metaphysics]] — the critical project that demonstrated why traditional speculative philosophy can never attain epistemic necessity.
- [[empiricism]] — the tradition that challenged rationalist indubitability by anchoring all knowledge in fallible sensory observation.
- [[rationalism]] — the epistemological stance that foundational truths can be grasped with absolute clarity through pure intellect.
- [[david-hume]] — the Scottish philosopher whose problem of induction demonstrated that empirical generalizations can never achieve definitive proof.
- [[ancient-greece]] — the birthplace of systematic epistemology where the Stoics, Epicureans, and Skeptics debated the possibility of secure knowledge.
- [[kant]] — the thinker who established synthetic *a priori* cognition as the only legitimate source of universal, necessary truth about nature.
