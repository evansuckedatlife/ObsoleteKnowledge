## summary

**Phenomenology** is a philosophical method and discipline that investigates the structure of consciousness from the first-person perspective: what is it like to experience, perceive, and act in the world? Rather than asking whether the external world exists (metaphysics) or how we come to know it (epistemology), phenomenology asks: *how is the world given to consciousness?* What are the essential structures of experience—intention, presence, time, embodiment, emotion? Edmund Husserl founded phenomenology as a rigorous "science of consciousness"; Martin Heidegger and Jean-Paul Sartre transformed it into existential phenomenology, examining how human beings exist in the world. Phenomenology rejects the view that consciousness is a ghostly observer of reality; instead, consciousness is always *of something*, always intentional, always embedded in a world.

## you gotta know

- *Intentionality* is phenomenology's cornerstone: consciousness is always consciousness *of* something (a perception of red, a memory of childhood, a fear of failure). Consciousness is fundamentally relational; it has a direction toward objects. This dissolves the traditional subject-object dualism.
- *Bracketing* (*epoché*) is Husserl's method: suspend judgment about whether the external world exists, and examine consciousness itself—the structures of how objects appear. This is not skepticism; it is a deliberate shift in perspective to describe pure experience.
- *Essence* in phenomenology is not a metaphysical Platonic form but the invariant structure revealed through imaginative variation. What is *essential* to anger, to memory, to visual perception? Phenomenology seeks the essence by varying experiences mentally and identifying what cannot be removed without changing the phenomenon.
- *Lived experience* (*Erlebnis*, *le vécu*) is the concrete, first-person experience of existence—walking through a familiar city, the feeling of boredom, the shock of loss. Phenomenology prioritizes this over abstract theorizing; it is a philosophy of lived life.
- *Embodiment*: Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty emphasized that consciousness is not a disembodied mind but is always embodied in a body that acts, perceives, and dwells in the world. Your body is not an object you observe; it is the lived perspective from which you experience everything.
- *Temporality*: phenomenology insists that consciousness and experience are intrinsically temporal. You perceive an object not as an instantaneous snapshot but as extended in time—retention of what just passed, perception of the present, protention toward what's coming. This structure of time is fundamental to consciousness.
- *Existence precedes essence*: Sartre appropriated phenomenology for existentialism—human existence is not determined by a fixed essence but is open-ended and free. Phenomenology becomes a method for describing how humans exist *for themselves*, always transcending what they are.
- *Phenomenological reduction* moves from the empirical ego (you as a person in the world) to the transcendental ego (consciousness itself). This reveals universal structures of consciousness that ground all particular experiences.
