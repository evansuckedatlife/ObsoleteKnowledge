## summary

**Jean-Paul Sartre** (1905–1980) was a French philosopher, novelist, and political activist who became the public face of existentialism in the mid-20th century. His radical claim—"existence precedes essence," meaning humans have no fixed nature and are radically free to create themselves—reversed millennia of philosophy. He wrote dense theoretical works (*Being and Nothingness*, *Critique of Dialectical Reason*) and accessible novels (*Nausea*, *The Stranger*) that made philosophy urgent and personal; he won the Nobel Prize in Literature but refused it. His partnership with *Simone de Beauvoir* and philosophical rivalry with *Albert Camus* defined European intellectual life during the Cold War.

## you gotta know

- *Existence precedes essence*: Sartre's central claim. Unlike manufactured objects (a knife has essence—its design and purpose—before it exists), humans exist first and then create their essence through choices and actions. We are "condemned to be free."
- Sartre developed *phenomenology* as a philosophical method: describing the structure of conscious experience from the first-person perspective. His massive *Being and Nothingness* (1943) is a phenomenological ontology of consciousness, freedom, and nothingness.
- *Bad faith* (*mauvaise foi*) is Sartre's term for the human tendency to deny freedom and responsibility by pretending we have no choice. A waiter who entirely identifies with his role, a woman who pretends not to recognize sexual advances, a person who says "I had no choice"—all exemplify bad faith. Recognizing bad faith requires acknowledging the anxiety of radical freedom.
- Sartre was a committed political activist, especially against colonialism and fascism. He supported Marxism and engaged in public controversy; he lived in cafés (Café de Flore, Café de Rotonde) where intellectuals gathered, making philosophy a public practice.
- His relationship with *Simone de Beauvoir* was lifelong and productive; they were philosophical partners and lovers who maintained an open relationship, radically unconventional for the era. Her *The Second Sex* applied existentialism to gender.
- *Consciousness is nothingness*: Sartre argued consciousness (unlike objects) has no fixed essence; it is pure negation, freedom, and transcendence. This made consciousness the ground of freedom but also the source of anguish—we cannot escape the burden of choice.
- Sartre's philosophical rivalry with *Albert Camus* came to a head in 1952 over Marxism and commitment; Camus rejected Marxism as deterministic and a betrayal of individual freedom, while Sartre defended it as the only viable politics. Their public break symbolized deeper tensions between existentialism and absurdism.
- Later in life, Sartre revised his philosophy: *Critique of Dialectical Reason* attempted to reconcile existentialism with Marxism and to account for how historical structures constrain freedom—a move toward "existential Marxism."

## connections

- [[existentialism]] — Sartre is the defining figure who popularized and systematized existentialism.
- [[albert-camus]] — philosophical rival; their debate over Marxism and absurdism shaped mid-century European thought.
- [[simone-de-beauvoir]] — philosophical partner; her existentialist feminism extended Sartre's work to gender and ethics.
- [[phenomenology]] — Sartre used phenomenological description as his philosophical method.
- [[freedom]] — radical freedom and responsibility are central to Sartre's existentialism.
- [[world-war-ii]] — Sartre lived through the Nazi occupation and was shaped by its moral urgency.
- [[cold-war]] — Sartre's political engagement and Marxism must be understood in Cold War context.
