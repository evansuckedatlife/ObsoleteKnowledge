## summary

**Deontology**, also known as **deontological ethics** or **duty ethics**, is the normative ethical position that judges the moral rightness or wrongness of actions based on whether they adhere to absolute duties, rules, and moral obligations rather than their outcomes. Formulated most systematically during the *[[enlightenment]]* in *[[germany]]* by *[[kant]]*, it asserts that certain actions are intrinsically impermissible regardless of any beneficial consequences they might generate. Alongside utilitarianism and *[[virtue-ethics]]*, it stands as one of the central pillars of moral *[[philosophy]]*, fundamentally shaping modern human rights theory and legal ethics.

## you gotta know

- Derives its etymology from the Greek *deon*, meaning duty, obligation, or that which is binding, combined with *logos*, signifying study or rational account.
- Articulated definitively by *[[kant]]* across his ethical treatises, notably the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, *Critique of Practical Reason*, and *Metaphysics of Morals*.
- Grounded in the *categorical imperative*, an unconditional moral obligation derived through *[[deductive-reasoning]]* from practical reason, commanding agents to act only according to maxims they can consistently will to become universal laws.
- Expressed prominently through the *formula of humanity*, which commands that rational persons must never be treated merely as a means to an end, but always simultaneously as ends in themselves.
- Rejects consequentialism through uncompromising moral absolutism, famously illustrated in Kant's essay *On a Supposed Right to Tell Lies from Benevolent Motives*, which prohibits lying even to a murderer seeking a friend.
- Links moral worth strictly to motive, insisting that an action possesses genuine moral value only if performed out of pure duty rather than inclination, sympathy, or self-interest.
- Distinguishes between *perfect duties*, which are strict and unyielding negative prohibitions such as not murdering or not making false promises, and *imperfect duties*, which allow latitude in how they are pursued, such as cultivating talents and aiding the needy.
- Refined in the twentieth century by intuitionist thinker *W. D. Ross* in *The Right and the Good*, who proposed non-absolutist *prima facie* duties to resolve conflicting obligations, and by *Christine Korsgaard* using contemporary constructivism.

## connections

- [[kant]] — the central philosopher of deontological thought who grounded duty in rational autonomy.
- [[deductive-reasoning]] — the method of deriving categorical obligations by testing maxims for internal logical contradiction.
- [[virtue-ethics]] — classical alternative framework focusing on the character of the moral agent rather than universal duties.
- [[free-will]] — the indispensable metaphysical prerequisite for moral responsibility and obedience to duty.
- [[enlightenment]] — the historical era that championed autonomous human reason as the independent source of moral law.
- [[germany]] — the cultural homeland where Kant and later German idealists developed duty-based philosophies.
- [[philosophy]] — the broader academic discipline within which deontology represents a fundamental normative theory.
