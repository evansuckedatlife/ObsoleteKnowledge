## summary

**Responsibility**, particularly in its philosophical dimension as **moral responsibility**, is the status of morally deserving praise, blame, reward, or punishment for an act or omission performed in accordance with ethical obligation. Originating in *[[ancient-greece]]* with *[[aristotle]]*, the inquiry investigates whether holding agents accountable requires genuine alternate possibilities or merely an appropriate psychological relationship between desire, intention, and action. In modern thought, the concept bridges the metaphysics of *[[free-will]]* with the practical governance of social relations, law, and personal identity.

## you gotta know

- *[[aristotle]]* inaugurated the classical theory in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, proposing that an agent is blameworthy or praiseworthy only for voluntary actions, which require absence of physical compulsion (the control condition) and absence of excusing ignorance (the epistemic condition).
- The *Principle of Alternate Possibilities* (*PAP*) long asserted that an agent is morally accountable only if they could have acted otherwise, an assumption famously challenged by *Harry Frankfurt* in 1969 through hypothetical cases featuring dormant counterfactual interveners.
- *P. F. Strawson* shifted contemporary debate in his 1962 paper *Freedom and Resentment* by grounding accountability in human "reactive attitudes"—such as resentment, indignation, guilt, and gratitude—rather than abstract metaphysical proofs of determinism.
- In the *[[existentialism]]* of *[[jean-paul-sartre]]*, humans are "condemned to be free" and carry total accountability for inventing their own values, whereas attempting to flee this radical burden constitutes the evasion of "bad faith" (*mauvaise foi*).
- *[[kant]]* connected accountability to autonomy and moral duty during the *[[enlightenment]]*, arguing under his principle of *ought implies can* that rational beings possess the necessary practical capacity to obey the categorical imperative.
- *Thomas Nagel* and *Bernard Williams* introduced the paradox of "moral luck," exposing how ordinary practices of blame and praise persistently judge agents for consequences, temperaments, and circumstances completely outside their control.
- Classical and modern disputes divide incompatibilists, who argue that causal determinism entirely undermines desert and blame, from compatibilists like *[[david-hume]]*, who maintain that actions flowing from an agent's internal character and desires are sufficient for accountability.

## connections

- [[free-will]] — the capacity for self-determined action widely considered the necessary metaphysical condition for holding an agent accountable.
- [[freedom]] — the condition of self-direction and autonomy whose exercise provides the essential baseline for ethical obligation.
- [[jean-paul-sartre]] — French existentialist who argued that individuals possess total personal accountability and cannot shift blame onto external circumstances.
- [[existentialism]] — philosophical framework holding that human beings define their own essence and must confront the dread of unexcused choice.
- [[aristotle]] — ancient thinker who first systematically codified the criteria of voluntariness, coercion, and ignorance in moral appraisal.
- [[kant]] — ethicist who grounded moral accountability in the rational will's capacity for self-legislation and unconditional duty.
- [[david-hume]] — empiricist philosopher who advanced compatibilism by identifying character traits and motives as the true objects of praise and blame.
