## summary

**Eudaimonia** is a classical Greek philosophical term frequently translated as "human flourishing," "well-being," or "happiness," signifying the highest human good (*summum bonum*). Systematized primarily in the moral treatises of *[[ancient-greece]]*, most notably by *[[aristotle]]* in the 4th century BCE, it denotes an active, lifelong realization of human excellence rather than a transient emotional state. The concept forms the cornerstone of ancient teleological ethics and continues to serve as the foundation for modern *[[virtue-ethics]]*.

## you gotta know

- Serves as the supreme, self-sufficient end (*telos*) of human existence in Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*, desired entirely for its own sake and never as an instrumental means toward any further objective.
- Grounded in Aristotle's "function argument" (*ergon*), which posits that human flourishing depends on performing the distinctively human activity—reason—in accordance with virtue (*arete*) over a complete lifespan.
- Distinct from modern conceptions of happiness as subjective pleasure or emotional contentment; ancient philosophers conceived it as an objective state of flourishing, living well, and acting well (*eu zen kai eu prattein*).
- Requires, in Aristotelian thought, certain external goods (*choregia*) such as health, adequate material wealth, good birth, and friendship, distinguishing his view from more ascetic schools.
- Redefined by *[[socrates]]* and the Stoics, including *[[epictetus]]*, who argued that virtue alone is completely necessary and sufficient for flourishing, rendering external misfortunes incapable of destroying a sage's happiness.
- Rejected hedonistic equations with pure sensory pleasure (*hedone*), an argument famously advanced by *[[plato]]* in the *Philebus* and reaffirmed by Aristotle's critique of the life of gratification.
- Re-emerged as a major focus in contemporary moral philosophy through the revival of virtue ethics by 20th-century thinkers like *Elizabeth Anscombe* and *Alasdair MacIntyre*, who challenged utilitarian and deontological frameworks.
- Parallels ethical ideals of living in harmony with one's cultivated moral nature found in world traditions such as *[[confucianism]]*, which emphasizes relational virtue and ritual propriety.

## connections

- [[aristotle]] — the ancient Greek philosopher who formulated the classical definition of human flourishing in the *Nicomachean Ethics*.
- [[virtue-ethics]] — the ethical tradition centered on cultivating virtuous character traits as the constitutive path to human well-being.
- [[epictetus]] — the Stoic teacher who maintained that moral virtue alone guarantees flourishing irrespective of external misfortune.
- [[stoicism]] — the philosophical school asserting that living in accordance with nature and reason provides complete self-sufficiency in flourishing.
- [[ancient-greece]] — the civilization whose intellectual debates across *Athens* established flourishing as the central problem of moral theory.
- [[plato]] — the philosopher whose dialogues explored the harmony of the tripartite soul as the prerequisite for a flourishing life.
- [[socrates]] — the foundational thinker who asserted that the unexamined life is not worth living and equated virtue directly with well-being.
