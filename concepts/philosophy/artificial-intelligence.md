## summary

In the philosophy of mind, **artificial intelligence** explores whether synthetic, non-biological computing machines can achieve authentic cognition, intentionality, and subjective experience. Developing alongside cybernetics and digital computation in the mid-twentieth century, this philosophical domain investigates whether mental states are computational and multiply realizable across diverse physical substrates. The field examines whether replicating human behavioral competence suffices for genuine mentality or if understanding necessitates biological and causal architecture. Ultimately, it occupies a central position within the modern [[mind-body-problem]], testing the explanatory boundaries of [[materialism]] and reshaping metaphysical definitions of [[consciousness]].

## you gotta know

- *Alan Turing* introduced the imitation game in his 1950 paper *Computing Machinery and Intelligence*, proposing an operational behavioral benchmark to sidestep metaphysical debates over machine thinking and evaluate whether an observer could distinguish machine communication from human discourse.
- *John Searle* formulated the *Chinese Room* thought experiment in 1980 to refute strong AI, arguing that executing syntactic rule-based programs over formal symbols never produces authentic semantic comprehension, subjective meaning, or genuine intentionality.
- The distinction between strong and weak AI separates hypothetical machines that authentically possess minds, understanding, and self-awareness from utilitarian systems that merely simulate cognitive tasks through algorithmic information processing.
- Functionalism, developed by philosophers such as *Hilary Putnam* and *Jerry Fodor*, asserts that mental states are defined by their functional roles and causal relations rather than their material composition, providing the core theoretical basis for multiple realizability in silicon.
- *Thomas Nagel* emphasized the subjective character of experience in his analysis of what it is like to be a bat, arguing that objective third-person computational models inherently fail to explain the first-person qualitative dimension of [[consciousness]].
- *Ned Block* contested functionalist theories of mind with the *China Brain* thought experiment, arguing that a nationwide system mimicking the functional organization of human neurons would still lack qualitative mental states, sensations, and qualia.
- *Hubert Dreyfus* drew upon [[phenomenology]] and *Martin Heidegger* to criticize symbolic artificial intelligence, contending that human expertise relies on unconscious embodied coping and background context rather than explicit algorithmic rules.
- *David Chalmers* formulated the hard problem of [[consciousness]], questioning why computational performance should give rise to inner experience, and used philosophical zombies to demonstrate that physical information processing does not conceptually entail subjective awareness.

## connections

- [[mind-body-problem]] — provides the foundational metaphysical debate over whether physical computing hardware can generate genuine mental properties and intentional states.
- [[consciousness]] — serves as the primary phenomenal boundary separating simulated computational behavior from authentic subjective inner awareness.
- [[materialism]] — offers the physicalist foundation asserting that mental states supervene entirely on physical interactions, implying artificial minds are physically possible.
- [[dualism]] — historically opposes physicalist artificial intelligence by maintaining that conscious minds possess non-physical properties that cannot emerge from mechanical computation.
- [[logic]] — supplied the formal foundations for classical symbolic architectures, automated reasoning systems, and early computational models of cognition.
- [[phenomenology]] — inspires embodied critiques asserting that genuine cognition requires situated physical involvement in the world rather than detached symbolic calculation.
- [[free-will]] — intersects with questions of mechanical determinism, debating whether algorithmic programs could ever possess genuine moral agency and autonomy.
- [[empiricism]] — aligns with sub-symbolic connectionism and neural networks, which construct representations from sensory data patterns rather than innate logical rules.
