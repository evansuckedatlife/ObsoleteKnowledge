## summary

**Morality** is the system of principles concerning the distinction between right and wrong, good and bad action. It is both a universal human phenomenon (all societies have moral norms) and a philosophical problem: Are moral truths objective facts independent of human opinion, or are they subjective preferences? Can we derive moral "ought" from factual "is"? What makes an action right—its consequences, the agent's intentions, virtue, or adherence to rules? Philosophers have spent millennia proposing answers: *Aristotle* championed virtue; *Kant* demanded universal principles; utilitarians calculate consequences; *Hume* grounded morality in sentiment. Yet moral disagreement persists, and neuroscience now suggests morality may be rooted in evolutionary biology and emotion rather than reason.

## you gotta know

- *Moral realism* says moral facts exist independent of human opinion; some actions are genuinely right or wrong, just as facts about physics are. *Moral relativism* denies this: morality is culturally or individually constructed, and no objective standard exists. *Moral nihilism* goes further: there are no moral facts at all; morality is illusion.
- *Aristotle's* *virtue ethics* holds that morality is about cultivating excellent character traits (virtues like courage, wisdom, temperance). Right action flows from a virtuous character; the goal is *eudaimonia* (flourishing or living well). This contrasts with rule-based ethics.
- *Deontology* (*Kant* is the paradigm) says actions are right or wrong based on duty and rules, not consequences. *Kant's* *categorical imperative* demands: act only in ways you could universalize as a moral law. This preserves moral dignity and rights but can demand sacrificing happiness for duty.
- *Consequentialism* (especially *utilitarianism*) says an action is right if it produces the best consequences—maximizing happiness, pleasure, or preference satisfaction for the most people. It is intuitive but faces hard questions: can we sacrifice innocents for greater good?
- *David Hume* argued morality is grounded in sentiment (emotion, sympathy, fellow-feeling) rather than reason. We feel moral approval or disapproval; reason cannot determine what is good. This makes morality seem less objective but explains why we care about morality emotionally.
- *Moral motivation* is a puzzle: why do we act morally? If morality is objective fact, why are we motivated by facts? If it's subjective feeling, is morality truly binding? *Kant* tried to ground motivation in reason; utilitarians in happiness; virtue ethicists in flourishing.
- *Moral psychology* questions whether ordinary people are moral realists (believing in objective moral facts) or more pragmatic and relativist. Research suggests people have "moral intuitions" that often outpace reasoned argument—we feel something is wrong before we can articulate why.
- The *is-ought problem* (*Hume*) challenges the logical move from descriptive facts about how things are to normative claims about how they should be. You cannot derive moral obligations from facts about nature, biology, or human preferences without smuggling in an unstated premise.

## connections

- [[virtue-ethics]] — the Aristotelian approach grounding morality in character excellence.
- [[david-hume]] — argued morality is grounded in sentiment, not reason.
- [[nihilism]] — the radical view that morality is illusion and moral facts do not exist.
- [[kant]] — developed deontological ethics based on universal rational principles.
- [[utilitarianism]] — the consequentialist view that right action maximizes overall happiness.
- [[meaning]] — moral meaning (finding purpose in moral action) is closely tied to morality itself.
- [[stoicism]] — an ancient virtue ethics emphasizing duty, reason, and acceptance of fate.
