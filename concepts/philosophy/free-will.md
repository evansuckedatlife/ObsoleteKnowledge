## summary

**Free will** is the philosophical question of whether humans have the capacity to make choices that are not entirely determined by prior causes, natural laws, or divine predestination. It sits at the intersection of metaphysics, ethics, and physics, because moral responsibility seems to require that we could have done otherwise. The tension between causality and agency remains one of philosophy's most enduring puzzles.

## you gotta know

- The core problem: if every event (including human decisions) is causally determined by prior events, how can we be free or morally responsible?
- Determinism holds that the future is entirely fixed by the laws of nature and the state of the past; it appears to eliminate free will.
- Compatibilism argues that free will and determinism are compatible—a choice can be both caused and free if it flows from your own desires and reasoning.
- Libertarian free will (the hard position) insists that genuine freedom requires that some events, particularly human choices, are not fully determined by prior causes.
- *Epicurus* invoked atomic swerve (clinamen) to preserve free will against deterministic atomism in ancient times.
- Quantum indeterminacy raised new hope for libertarians: if subatomic events are genuinely random, perhaps there is room for undetermined choice.
- The manipulation argument: if determinism is true and we are manipulated by an external agent (a demon, a scientist), are we still free or responsible?
- Theological determinism adds a layer: if God knows the future with certainty, does that mean our future actions are already determined and we cannot be free?
- The consequence argument: if the past and the laws of nature determine what we do, and we have no control over the past or the laws, how can we have control over our actions?
- The problem shapes debates in ethics (can we blame someone for what they were determined to do?), law, and neuroscience.

## connections

- [[determinism]] — the opposing view that all events, including choices, are fully caused and inevitable.
- [[compatibilism]] — the compromise position that reconciles determinism and moral responsibility.
- [[causality]] — the relationship between cause and effect that seems to threaten freedom.
- [[moral-responsibility]] — the ethical foundation that appears to require free will.
- [[quantum-mechanics]] — raises the possibility that some events escape classical determinism.
- [[epicurus]] — an ancient philosopher who deployed atomic randomness to defend freedom.
- [[descartes]] — grappled with how free will relates to God's foreknowledge and omnipotence.
