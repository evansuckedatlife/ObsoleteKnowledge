## summary

**Induction**, or **inductive reasoning**, is a method of logical inference in which general principles, universal laws, or probabilistic forecasts are derived from repeated observations of specific instances. Formulated in antiquity by *[[aristotle]]* and championed during the Scientific Revolution by *Francis Bacon*, it serves as the foundational epistemic engine of modern empirical science. However, it presents philosophy with one of its most persistent dilemmas—the **problem of induction**, first rigorously articulated by *[[david-hume]]*—which questions whether any finite number of past observations can rationally justify beliefs about unobserved or future events. Reconciling or bypassing this skepticism spurred major developments across [[philosophy]], ranging from the transcendental idealism of *[[kant]]* to the falsificationist epistemology of *Karl Popper*.

## you gotta know

- *[[david-hume]]* formulated the classic problem of induction in *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), arguing that inductive inferences depend on the principle of the uniformity of nature, which itself cannot be justified deductively (since nature's course changing implies no contradiction) or inductively (without vicious circularity).
- Unlike deductive arguments, whose valid structures guarantee that true premises yield true conclusions, inductive arguments establish conclusions that are only probable, meaning that empirical generalizations remain perpetually vulnerable to disconfirming instances—a vulnerability epitomized by the classic European conviction that all swans were white until black swans were discovered in Australia.
- *Karl Popper* rejected induction as an epistemic illusion in *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1934), insisting that genuine scientific practice proceeds not by inductive verification, but through deductive *falsification* via modus tollens.
- *[[kant]]* credited Hume's skeptical critique of induction with interrupting his "dogmatic slumber," prompting him to argue in the *Critique of Pure Reason* that the principle of causality is a synthetic *a priori* condition necessary for the possibility of experience rather than an inductive habit.
- *Nelson Goodman* introduced the "New Riddle of Induction" in *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* (1955) by defining the artificial predicate *grue*—applying to objects examined before time *t* and found green, or unexamined and blue—to prove that syntactically identical inductive rules cannot explain why green is projectible into the future while *grue* is not.
- *Carl Gustav Hempel* revealed a fundamental puzzle within inductive confirmation known as the "raven paradox," demonstrating that under classical [[logic]], observing a non-black non-raven (such as a white shoe) logically counts as confirming evidence for the hypothesis that all ravens are black.
- *Francis Bacon* established the modern philosophical framework for inductive inquiry in his *Novum Organum* (1620), rejecting simple enumerative induction in favor of systematic tables of presence, absence, and degrees to methodically isolate causal forms.

## connections

- [[david-hume]] — articulated the definitive skeptical critique of inductive reasoning and the uniformity of nature.
- [[empiricism]] — relies on induction as its primary epistemological bridge from sensory observations to general scientific laws.
- [[kant]] — responded to inductive skepticism by establishing causality as a necessary synthetic *a priori* category of the understanding.
- [[logic]] — formalizes the structural distinction between deductively valid entailments and inductively probabilistic inferences.
- [[rationalism]] — opposed inductive foundationalism by asserting that certain knowledge derives from innate ideas and deductive reasoning rather than experience.
- [[aristotle]] — analyzed *epagoge*, the intuitive transition from individual empirical perceptions to universal principles.
- [[descartes]] — sought an indubitable philosophical foundation through deductive skepticism, rejecting fallible inductive generalization.
