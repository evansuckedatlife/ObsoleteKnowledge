## summary

**Medieval philosophy** is the Western philosophical tradition spanning roughly the 5th to 15th centuries, dominated by the integration of *Aristotelian* logic and *Platonist* metaphysics with Christian theology. The central problem was reconciling faith (revealed truth from scripture) with reason (philosophical argument and argument)—can God's existence be proven by reason alone, or is faith a separate faculty? *Augustine*, *Aquinas*, and *Duns Scotus* spent centuries answering this question, developing sophisticated logical systems and theological arguments. Medieval philosophy is often dismissed as dogmatic, but it was intellectually rigorous, created the university system, and invented scholastic disputation, the ancestor of academic discourse.

## you gotta know

- **Scholasticism** is the defining method: it uses *Aristotle's* logic of syllogism and *Aquinas's* synthesis of reason and revelation. A scholastic asks a question, marshals arguments for and against from authorities (scripture, *Aristotle*, church fathers), and resolves the tension using careful logical reasoning. This method dominated universities until the 17th century.
- *Augustine* (354–430) attempted to reconcile *Platonism* with Christianity: God is the ultimate Form; the soul is immortal; the physical world is subordinate to the spiritual. His *Confessions* established autobiographical introspection as a philosophical genre.
- *Thomas Aquinas* (1225–1274) synthesized *Aristotle* with theology: he argued that *Aristotle's* logic and metaphysics were not heretical but compatible with Christianity. He proved God's existence using *Aristotle's* five ways (arguments from motion, causation, contingency, perfection, and design).
- The *problem of universals*—whether abstract properties (redness, humanity) exist independently or are mental constructs—dominated medieval logic. Realists said universals exist; nominalists said they are merely names; moderates (Aquinas) sought middle ground.
- *Divine omniscience vs. human free will*: if God knows the future with certainty, can humans have genuine free choice? Medieval philosophers struggled to reconcile God's foreknowledge with human moral responsibility. *Aquinas* argued God exists outside time, so God's knowledge is not causal.
- Medieval philosophers rediscovered *Aristotle* through Arabic translations and commentaries by *Averroes* and *Avicenna*. The recovery of *Aristotle* (particularly his metaphysics and natural philosophy) in the 12th–13th centuries revolutionized medieval thought, creating tension with established *Platonism*.
- The **university system** emerged in the 12th century (Bologna, Oxford, Paris) as an institutional home for scholastic philosophy and theology. Universities became centers of logical debate and rational inquiry about faith.
- *Duns Scotus* (1266–1308) challenged *Aquinas*: he argued reason cannot prove God's existence or many theological truths; faith and revelation are necessary. Scotism competed with Thomism for influence through the Renaissance.

## connections

- [[scholasticism]] — the dominant method and movement of medieval philosophy.
- [[thomas-aquinas]] — the most influential medieval philosopher, synthesizer of *Aristotle* and theology.
- [[humanism]] — emerged as a reaction against scholasticism's abstract logic and privileged classical literature over theological disputation.
- [[aristotle]] — medieval philosophers recovered and integrated *Aristotle's* logic and metaphysics.
- [[dante-alighieri]] — drew on medieval philosophical and theological frameworks for his epic poetry.
- [[christianity]] — the theological context that shaped all medieval philosophy.
- [[ancient-greece]] — medieval thinkers were reviving and reinterpreting ancient Greek philosophy.
