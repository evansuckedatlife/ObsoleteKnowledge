## summary

**Griswold v. Connecticut**, recorded in legal reporters as **381 U.S. 479** and commonly referred to as **Griswold**, was a watershed 1965 decision of the Supreme Court of the [[united-states]] that invalidated a state prohibition on contraceptive use by married couples. Writing for the majority, Justice *William O. Douglas* articulated an implied constitutional right to marital privacy emanated from the Bill of Rights and protected against state infringement under the [[14th-amendment]]. This landmark ruling established the doctrinal foundation for contemporary substantive due process and fundamentally transformed modern American jurisprudence regarding personal autonomy.

## you gotta know

- Invalidated an 1879 Connecticut Comstock law that prohibited using "any drug, medicinal article or instrument for the purpose of preventing conception" and criminalized medical professionals who counseled patients on birth control.
- Originated as an intentional legal challenge orchestrated by *Estelle Griswold*, executive director of the Planned Parenthood League of Connecticut, and Dr. *C. Lee Buxton* of the Yale School of Medicine, who were arrested after opening a New Haven clinic.
- Penned by Justice *William O. Douglas*, whose majority opinion famously declared that specific constitutional guarantees possess "penumbras, formed by emanations from those guarantees that help give them life and substance," creating a constitutionally protected "zone of privacy."
- Derived this right to marital privacy from the penumbral protections of the First, Third, Fourth, and Fifth Amendments, made binding on individual state legislatures through the Due Process Clause of the [[14th-amendment]].
- Generated an influential concurring opinion by Justice *Arthur Goldberg*, joined by Chief Justice *Earl Warren* and Justice *William J. Brennan Jr.*, which grounded the protection of unenumerated fundamental rights firmly in the Ninth Amendment.
- Included an important concurrence by Justice *John Marshall Harlan II*, who argued that marital privacy is protected directly by the concept of ordered liberty within the Due Process Clause, eschewing Douglas's penumbra theory.
- Drew pointed dissents from Justices *Hugo Black* and *Potter Stewart*, with Stewart dismissing the statute as "an uncommonly silly law" while asserting that the [[united-states-constitution]] contains no generalized right of privacy.
- Established the vital constitutional precedent that paved the way for *Eisenstadt v. Baird* (protecting unmarried individuals), *Roe v. Wade* (abortion access), *Lawrence v. Texas* (private intimate acts), and *Obergefell v. Hodges* (same-sex marriage).

## connections

- [[14th-amendment]] — the constitutional amendment whose Due Process Clause incorporated fundamental privacy protections against state statutory overreach.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — the foundational national charter whose provisions were interpreted to contain unenumerated zones of personal privacy.
- [[reproductive-rights]] — the legal movement for bodily and reproductive autonomy that gained its essential constitutional foundation in this ruling.
- [[womens-liberation]] — the feminist social struggle that relied heavily upon legal access to birth control to advance gender equality.
- [[public-health]] — the medical field elevated by affirming the right of physicians to prescribe modern contraceptives without criminal liability.
- [[margaret-sanger]] — the pioneer of birth control whose advocacy led to the establishment of Planned Parenthood and the clinics that prompted the litigation.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — the rights revolution of the 1960s that expanded judicial enforcement of personal liberties under the Warren Court.
- [[roe-v-wade]] — the 1973 landmark ruling on reproductive freedom that directly extended the constitutional privacy doctrine formulated in this case.
