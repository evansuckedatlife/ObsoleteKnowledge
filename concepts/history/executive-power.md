## summary

**Executive power** refers to the authority vested in a nation's chief executive—in the United States, the President—to enforce laws, conduct foreign policy, and exercise command over the military. The scope of executive power is inherently contested; the Constitution grants vague authority subject to checks by Congress and the courts. American history pivots on disputes over where those boundaries lie, particularly in crises.

## you gotta know

- The Constitution grants the President power to "take care that the laws be faithfully executed" but leaves room for vast interpretive expansion during emergencies.
- Franklin D. Roosevelt's *New Deal* programs (1933–1945) radically expanded executive power through emergency decrees and new agencies, fundamentally altering the balance between branches.
- During *World War II*, Presidents claimed sweeping war powers: *Korematsu v. United States* upheld executive authority to intern Japanese Americans based on military necessity, a landmark case for executive prerogative.
- The Steel Seizure Case (*Youngstown Sheet & Tube v. Sawyer*, 1952) limited executive power by ruling that Truman lacked authority to seize steel mills without congressional approval.
- The *Gulf of Tonkin* incident (1964) and the Vietnam War showed how Presidents exploit ambiguous executive power over foreign military action without formal declarations of war.
- Watergate and the *Iran-Contra Scandal* illustrated the tension between executive autonomy and accountability, leading to investigations of presidential overreach.
- Modern debates center on presidential emergency powers, executive orders, and the ability to wage war without congressional approval—tensions rooted in the Constitution's deliberate vagueness.
