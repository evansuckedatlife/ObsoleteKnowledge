## summary

**Buddhism in China** represents the adaptation and flourishing of Indian Buddhist teachings within Chinese culture, becoming one of the world's most significant religious and philosophical movements. Arriving during the Han Dynasty via the Silk Road, Buddhism was initially foreign but gradually synthesized with Confucianism and Daoism, creating distinctly Chinese Buddhist schools. The religion reached its cultural and political zenith during the Tang Dynasty, profoundly shaping Chinese art, philosophy, literature, and statecraft before later decline due to Confucian revival and state suppression.

## you gotta know

- Entered China during the 1st century CE through merchants and missionaries traveling the Silk Road; early Chinese audiences found it compatible with existing philosophical frameworks.
- The translation of Buddhist texts into Chinese—led by figures like *Kumarajiva*—was a monumental intellectual undertaking that created new philosophical vocabulary and concepts.
- Developed distinctly Chinese schools: Chan (Zen) Buddhism emphasized meditation and sudden enlightenment; Pure Land Buddhism focused on devotion to Amitabha Buddha; Tibetan Tantric Buddhism influenced the imperial court.
- Reached its zenith during the Tang Dynasty (618–907), when emperors patronized Buddhist monasteries, sponsored translations, and incorporated Buddhist concepts into governance and culture.
- Chinese Buddhist art—including the Dunhuang Caves with their wall paintings, sculptures, and manuscripts—created an artistic legacy that influenced East Asian aesthetics.
- Faced suppression during the Tang (especially under Emperor Wuzong, 845 CE) and later periods as Confucianism reasserted dominance; Buddhist institutions lost wealth and influence but remained culturally significant.
