## summary

The *Federalist Papers* were a series of essays written by *James Madison*, *Alexander Hamilton*, and *John Jay* between 1787 and 1788 to argue for ratification of the *United States Constitution*. Published under the pseudonym *Publius* and disseminated widely in newspapers, the essays defended the Constitution's structure of federal government, separation of powers, and system of checks and balances against critics who feared centralized authority.

## you gotta know

- *Alexander Hamilton*, *James Madison*, and *John Jay* collaboratively wrote the 85 essays, with Hamilton authoring the most (at least 51), Madison around 15, and Jay only 5 due to illness; the true authorship of some essays remained disputed for decades.
- The essays addressed specific constitutional controversies: Federalist No. 10 argued that the Constitution's large republic would moderate factional conflict and protect individual rights; Federalist No. 51 explained the separation of powers and checks and balances.
- Federalist No. 78, written by *Hamilton*, established the intellectual foundation for *judicial review*—the power of courts to strike down unconstitutional laws—later codified in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803).
- The essays were instrumental in securing ratification in key states, especially New York, where anti-Federalist opposition was fierce; they were reprinted as a collected volume shortly after ratification and have remained canonical texts of constitutional interpretation.
- The *Federalist Papers* represent the most rigorous defense of representative republicanism and separation of powers produced during the founding era, drawing on *Enlightenment* political philosophy while addressing the specific circumstances of post-Revolutionary America.
- Legal scholars and Supreme Court justices continue to cite the Federalist Papers when interpreting constitutional clauses, treating the essays as authoritative evidence of the Framers' *original intent*.
