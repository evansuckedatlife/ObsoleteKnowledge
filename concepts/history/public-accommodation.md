## summary

A **public accommodation** is a commercial entity, private business, or facility providing lodging, food, entertainment, or retail goods to the general public, subject to statutory non-discrimination mandates under civil rights law. Codified federally in the *Civil Rights Act of 1875* and reinstated under Title II of the *Civil Rights Act of 1964* during the [[civil-rights-movement]] in the [[united-states]], these statutes require open establishments to serve all patrons without arbitrary exclusion. Understanding this concept is central to American legal history because it marks the precise boundary where private commercial property yields to public anti-discrimination duties, generating sustained constitutional conflict between statutory equal protection and First Amendment liberties.

## you gotta know

- *Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964* serves as the primary federal benchmark, prohibiting discrimination based on race, color, religion, or national origin in hotels, motels, restaurants, and entertainment venues operating in interstate commerce.
- *Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States* (1964) unanimously upheld federal regulation under the Commerce Clause of the [[united-states-constitution]], concluding that racial discrimination substantially impeded interstate travel and economic activity.
- *Katzenbach v. McClung* (1964) expanded this reach to local dining establishments by ruling that Birmingham's *Ollie's Barbecue* was subject to federal law because meat and supplies had moved across state lines.
- *The Civil Rights Cases* (1883) previously invalidated the *Civil Rights Act of 1875*, establishing the state action doctrine under the [[14th-amendment]] and severely limiting federal power to regulate private businesses during the [[reconstruction-era]].
- *Title III of the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990* broadly expanded the statutory concept beyond historic categories, requiring physical access, barrier removal, and reasonable accommodations across nearly all commercial private enterprises.
- Modern Supreme Court rulings including *303 Creative LLC v. Elenis* (2023) and *Masterpiece Cakeshop v. Colorado Civil Rights Commission* (2018) narrowed enforcement against customized creative services, preventing states from compelling speech that conflicts with [[religious-freedom]].
- Rooted in English common law, the doctrine historically applied to innkeepers, blacksmiths, and common carriers, imposing a legal duty on monopoly-like callings to serve all peaceful travelers without unreasonable refusal.

## connections

- [[civil-rights-movement]] — mobilized sit-ins, boycotts, and legal campaigns that pressured Congress to dismantle segregation in commercial facilities.
- [[14th-amendment]] — provided the constitutional equality principles whose state action limitation restricted direct judicial oversight of private enterprises.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — supplied the Commerce Clause authority that Congress utilized to validate modern federal non-discrimination statutes.
- [[reconstruction-era]] — produced the first federal statutory guarantee of equal access to facilities before judicial nullification in 1883.
- [[religious-freedom]] — forms the constitutional basis asserted by commercial vendors seeking exemptions from anti-discrimination laws protecting LGBTQ+ patrons.
- [[masterpiece-cakeshop-v-colorado]] — adjudicated whether state civil rights commissions could compel wedding vendors to provide services that violate their religious beliefs.
- [[303-creative-v-elenis]] — curtailed state accommodation enforcement by holding that creative commercial services cannot be compelled to express messages contrary to their conscience.
