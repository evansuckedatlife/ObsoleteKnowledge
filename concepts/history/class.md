## summary

**Class**, or **social class**, is a hierarchical system of social stratification that divides individuals and groups according to economic position, wealth, labor status, and access to systemic power. Crystallized as a formal subject of political philosophy during the [[industrial-revolution]] and the [[french-revolution]], the concept illuminates how material conditions govern social mobility, cultural prestige, and historical change. Understanding class dynamics is fundamental to analyzing labor history, political revolutions, and the systemic structures of inequality that intersect with [[race]] and gender.

## you gotta know

- Formulated by *Karl Marx* and *Friedrich Engels* in *The Communist Manifesto* primarily through an individual's relationship to the means of production, asserting that modern capitalist society is fundamentally split between the property-owning *bourgeoisie* and the wage-earning *proletariat*.
- Theorized by German sociologist *Max Weber* as a multidimensional model distinguishing economic class from social status or prestige (*Stand*) and political party affiliations, demonstrating that social power cannot be reduced solely to economic wealth.
- Precipitated the dramatic overthrow of the *Ancien Régime* during the [[french-revolution]], which abolished the feudal system of the Three Estates that separated society into clergy, nobility, and the common Third Estate championed by the *Abbé Sieyès* in his seminal pamphlet *What Is the Third Estate?*.
- Transformed during the [[industrial-revolution]] in [[england]], an epochal shift famously analyzed by social historian *E. P. Thompson* in *The Making of the English Working Class*, which argued that class consciousness is an active cultural relationship forged through collective struggle rather than a passive economic category.
- Articulated in contemporary critical theory as an interlocking axis of domination alongside [[race]] and gender, central to the foundational work of the *Combahee River Collective* and feminist theorist *bell hooks* in *Where We Stand: Class Matters*.
- Analyzed by American institutional economist *Thorstein Veblen* in *The Theory of the Leisure Class*, which coined the phrase *conspicuous consumption* to describe how ruling elites display unproductive expenditure and waste to publicly signal social superiority.
- Redefined in late twentieth-century sociology by *Pierre Bourdieu*, who demonstrated how dominant groups reproduce privilege across generations through the unequal distribution of cultural, social, and symbolic capital, mediated by unconscious habits known as the *habitus*.
- Traced historically to ancient [[rome]] during the *Conflict of the Orders*, where disenfranchised *plebeians* conducted political strikes and military secessions to secure codified laws, debt protection, and civil magistracies against the entrenched *patricians*.

## connections

- [[industrial-revolution]] — generated the mechanization, factory system, and rapid urban migration that formed the modern wage-earning proletariat.
- [[french-revolution]] — dismantled the feudal legal privileges and hereditary hierarchies that defined the medieval Three Estates.
- [[soviet-union]] — attempted to implement Marxist state socialism by reorganizing the economy under the constitutional dictatorship of the proletariat.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — organized working-class African Americans and increasingly focused on labor union rights, fair wages, and economic justice.
- [[united-states]] — fostered an enduring national ethos of meritocratic classlessness despite experiencing violent industrial labor strikes in the Gilded Age.
- [[charles-dickens]] — dramatized the grim realities of Victorian workhouses, child labor, and stark socioeconomic disparity in nineteenth-century [[england]].
- [[race]] — operates alongside socioeconomic stratification to produce compounding disparities in housing, wages, and systemic access to capital.
- [[intersectionality]] — provides the theoretical framework explaining how class, racial, and patriarchal hierarchies mutually construct and reinforce one another.
