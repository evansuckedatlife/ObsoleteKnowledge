## summary

**Corporate personhood**, or the **legal personhood of corporations**, is the legal doctrine establishing that an incorporated commercial entity is recognized in law as a distinct legal subject with independent standing. Originating in civil law and English common-law jurisprudence before expanding extensively through *[[united-states]]* constitutional interpretation, it enables corporations to enter contracts, initiate lawsuits, hold property, and assert select constitutional protections. The doctrine represents one of the most controversial frameworks in legal history, balancing organizational convenience for commerce against civic concerns over the democratization of public life.

## you gotta know

- In *Citizens United v. FEC* (2010), the United States Supreme Court ruled 5–4 that independent political expenditures by corporations are protected under the First Amendment, barring government restrictions on election communications.
- The doctrine was applied to the *[[14th-amendment]]* in *Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad Company* (1886), when court reporter *J.C. Bancroft Davis* recorded in the headnotes that the equal protection clause applies to private corporations.
- In *Burwell v. Hobby Lobby Stores, Inc.* (2014), the Supreme Court recognized that closely held for-profit corporations can claim religious exemptions under the *Religious Freedom Restoration Act*.
- Chief Justice *John Marshall* authored an influential early characterization in *Dartmouth College v. Woodward* (1819), describing a corporation as an "artificial being, invisible, intangible, and existing only in contemplation of law."
- The Supreme Court ruled in *First National Bank of Boston v. Bellotti* (1978) that corporations have First Amendment speech rights to spend money in ballot initiative campaigns, focusing on the rights of listeners to hear discourse.
- The concept operates as a legal fiction enabling asset partitioning, which insulates business capital from the personal creditors of corporate shareholders.
- Corporate personhood does not extend every constitutional right; corporations cannot invoke the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination, nor can they claim the Privileges and Immunities clause or physical Fourth Amendment bodily rights.

## connections

- [[corporate-law]] — broader legal discipline governing the creation, fiduciary structure, capitalization, and dissolution of corporate entities.
- [[14th-amendment]] — post-Civil War constitutional guarantee of due process and equal protection invoked to secure judicial protections for corporate property.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — foundational legal text whose individual rights have been extended to commercial entities through judicial review.
- [[united-states]] — nation where judicial review and campaign finance litigation dramatically broadened the constitutional rights of corporate persons.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — historical transformation that generated large-scale rail, industrial, and financial conglomerates requiring judicial recognition.
- [[first-amendment]] — constitutional guarantee of free speech and religious exercise extended to corporate advocacy in landmark twentieth- and twenty-first-century cases.
- [[campaign-finance]] — electoral regulation field profoundly transformed by Supreme Court rulings upholding corporate independent campaign outlays.
