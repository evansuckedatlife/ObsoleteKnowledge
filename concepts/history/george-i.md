## summary

**George I** was the first Hanoverian King of Great Britain and Ireland, reigning from 1714 until his death in 1727. A German-born prince who spoke little English and showed minimal interest in British affairs, his reign marked a crucial transition toward constitutional monarchy and parliamentary supremacy, as power gradually shifted from the crown to the Prime Minister.

## you gotta know

- Born **George Louis** in Hanover, 1660; became king of Britain through the *Act of Settlement* of 1701, which barred Catholic heirs and chose him as the nearest Protestant relative.
- Aged 54 when he ascended the British throne, making him the oldest first-time reigning monarch in English history to that point.
- Spoke minimal English, relied on German and French, and maintained strong ties to Hanover, often visiting his German principality and delegating British governance to ministers.
- His court became known for using German and Hanover customs; English nobles resented his preference for German courtiers and his apparent indifference to British affairs.
- Relied heavily on *Robert Walpole*, who became the first Prime Minister and skillfully managed both the king and Parliament through the monarch's disinterest.
- Walpole's influence grew because George I delegated power so thoroughly; this partnership established the precedent of the Prime Minister as the chief executive subordinate to the monarch.
- His reign solidified parliamentary control over taxation, legislation, and foreign policy, transforming the monarchy from an active decision-maker to a figurehead.
- Never learned to trust British nobles or institutions, surrounding himself with a small German court clique and maintaining distance from London society.
- Faced political instability early in his reign, including the *Jacobite* rising of 1715 among Scottish and Irish supporters of the deposed *Stuart* line.
- The Jacobite threat reinforced George I's caution and made him dependent on reliable Whig politicians like Walpole who could manage Parliament and suppress dissent.
- His distance from governance paradoxically strengthened constitutional development: Parliament flourished in the power vacuum he left, developing procedures, precedent, and institutional confidence.
- Left England with a stronger Parliament, a weak but secure monarchy, and a Hanoverian succession that would define the British monarchy for centuries.
