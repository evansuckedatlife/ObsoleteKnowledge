## summary

**Négritude** (also rendered as **Negritude**) was an influential literary, cultural, and ideological movement founded in 1930s *[[france]]* by Francophone Black intellectuals from Africa and the Caribbean. Developed in direct opposition to French *[[colonialism]]* and the psychological alienation wrought by imperial assimilation policies, the movement sought to reclaim pride in Black identity, celebrate African heritage, and affirm shared pan-African values. It shaped twentieth-century anti-colonial consciousness, catalyzed modern African literature, and provided the intellectual foundation for the political and cultural emergence of newly independent African nations.

## you gotta know

- Co-founded in Paris during the 1930s by three prominent Black students: the Senegalese poet *[[leopold-senghor]]*, Martinican poet *[[aime-cesaire]]*, and French Guianese poet *Léon Damas*, who united across geographical divides to reject French assimilation.
- Coined by *[[aime-cesaire]]* in the student periodical *L'Étudiant noir* in 1935, defiantly transforming the French racial slur *nègre* into a badge of collective pride, cultural dignity, and active political self-determination.
- Deeply influenced by the *[[harlem-renaissance]]* in the *[[united-states]]*, drawing profound creative inspiration from the writings of Langston Hughes and Claude McKay as well as the vibrant *[[jazz]]* culture thriving in interwar Parisian cafés.
- Championed and theoretically developed by *[[leopold-senghor]]*, who defined it as the totality of cultural values of the Black world, contrasting intuitive African emotion with European Cartesian rationalism and using it to guide national culture in independent *[[senegal]]*.
- Received prominent intellectual support in *[[france]]* from *[[existentialism]]* philosopher Jean-Paul Sartre, whose 1948 essay *"Orphée noir"* (*Black Orpheus*) served as the preface to Senghor's landmark anthology of Black *[[poetry]]* and framed the movement as an anti-racist racism.
- Catalyzed major international gatherings, including the historic 1956 First Congress of Black Writers and Artists at the Sorbonne and the 1966 World Festival of Negro Arts in Dakar, establishing lasting global forums for African and diasporic creative expression.
- Drew notable criticism from subsequent generations of African and Caribbean thinkers, such as Nigerian writer Wole Soyinka—who famously remarked that "a tiger does not proclaim its tigritude, it pounces"—and Martinican psychiatrist Frantz Fanon, who cautioned against static racial essentialism.

## connections

- [[leopold-senghor]] — Senegalese poet, statesman, and first president of *[[senegal]]* who served as the primary philosophical theorist of African cultural identity.
- [[aime-cesaire]] — Martinican poet and politician who originally coined the term and authored the defining masterpiece *Cahier d'un retour au pays natal*.
- [[colonialism]] — the oppressive system of imperial subjugation and forced assimilation that the intellectual movement sought to dismantle.
- [[harlem-renaissance]] — the African American cultural flowering that provided essential aesthetic and political models for Francophone Black writers.
- [[france]] — the imperial metropolis where colonial students met, founded pioneering journals, and organized anti-colonial intellectual resistance.
- [[existentialism]] — the philosophical framework through which European intellectuals engaged with the movement as a dialectical stage in liberation.
- [[french-west-africa]] — the colonial federation whose artists and emerging political leaders drew upon the movement's ideals during decolonization.
- [[poetry]] — the primary literary vehicle through which movement thinkers expressed cultural reclamation, linguistic innovation, and anti-imperial dissent.
