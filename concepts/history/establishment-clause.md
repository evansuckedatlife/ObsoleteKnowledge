## summary

The **Establishment Clause** is the first part of the *First Amendment*, prohibiting Congress from enacting laws that establish an official religion. Drafted as part of the *Bill of Rights*, it enshrines the principle that government should remain neutral on religious matters, protecting both religious freedom and secular governance.

## you gotta know

- Reads: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion" — the opening words of the First Amendment, ratified in 1791 as part of the *Bill of Rights*.
- Rooted in *Enlightenment* philosophy and *James Madison*'s *Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom* (1786), which inspired its language and reflected colonial experiences with religious persecution.
- Madison drafted the clause to prevent the creation of a national church or the preferential treatment of any single religion, breaking from European traditions of state-sponsored churches.
- Interpreted broadly by the Supreme Court to mean government must not favor one religion over another, nor religion over non-religion, in laws, policies, or public funding.
- Uses the word "establishment" to refer not just to direct funding but to preferential governmental support or endorsement of religious institutions or beliefs.
- Works in tandem with the *Free Exercise Clause*, which protects individuals' right to practice religion; together they define religious liberty as a two-sided protection.
- Has spawned ongoing litigation, from *Engel v. Vitale* (school prayer cases) to *Lemon v. Kurtzman* (parochial school funding) to modern debates over tax-exempt status for religious organizations.
- Establishes the principle of "separation of church and state," though those exact words do not appear in the Constitution; the phrase emerged from *Jefferson*'s writings and *Madison*'s legislative history.
- Fundamentally shapes American public policy in education, healthcare, and military service, affecting everything from public school curricula to military chaplaincy funding.
