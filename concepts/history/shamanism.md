## summary

**Shamanism** across the northern Japanese archipelago and surrounding islands—specifically **Ainu shamanism**—refers to the ecstatic healing, divinatory, and mediumistic traditions historically practiced by the indigenous Ainu people of Hokkaido, Sakhalin, and the Kuril chain. Deeply interwoven with a comprehensive animist worldview, these practices operated on the conviction that the physical world (*aynu mosir*) is continuously visited by spiritual beings known as *kamuy*, who embody animals, natural phenomena, and domestic hearth fires. Unlike public community rituals conducted exclusively by male village elders, shamanic seances were predominantly the domain of female practitioners who entered altered states of consciousness to diagnose sickness, divine the future, and exorcise malevolent spirits. This rich tradition serves as a cornerstone of indigenous circumpolar history, illuminating how northern hunting and gathering communities maintained ecological balance and spiritual sovereignty under intensifying geopolitical pressures.

## you gotta know

- Led predominantly by female spiritual mediums known as *tusukur*, who inherited their callings through spontaneous spirit possession or visionary dreams rather than institutionalized hereditary lineages.
- Operated within an animist ontology centered on *kamuy*, spiritual beings embodied in animals such as the brown bear and Blakiston's fish owl, natural elements, and crafted implements who visit the human world.
- Regarded *Kamuy Fuchi*, the sacred goddess of the household hearth fire, as the primary mediator and judge who supervised all séances and provided direct authorization for shamans to commune with external spirits.
- Emphasized therapeutic healing and soul-recovery, attributing serious physical illness, misfortune, and mental distress to soul loss or to attacks by malevolent demonic entities known collectively as *wenkamuy*.
- Depended on passive spirit possession termed *tusu*, wherein a spirit directly displaced the medium's consciousness to speak and prophesy, contrasting sharply with the active, out-of-body soul flights typical of mainland Siberian shamanism.
- Utilized whittled willow wands called *inaw*, intricately carved with curling wood shavings to function as divine communication channels, protective spiritual armor, and appeasement offerings during rituals.
- Maintained a strict gender division with patriarchal communal liturgies, standing apart from male-led prayers like *kamuynomi* and the elaborate *iyomante* bear-sending ceremony by focusing on spontaneous, domestic crisis management.
- Faced systematic prohibition and cultural disruption under imperial [[japan]], particularly following the 1899 Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act, which criminalized traditional shamanic practices and accelerated assimilation through [[colonialism]].

## connections

- [[japan]] — the imperial state whose northern territorial consolidation annexed Hokkaido and enforced strict cultural assimilation policies that suppressed indigenous spiritual ceremonies.
- [[colonialism]] — the structural apparatus of legal dispossession, forced agrarian settlement, and language suppression that banned traditional ritual tattoos and mediumistic healing practices.
- [[russian-empire]] — the rival northern empire contesting Sakhalin and the Kuril chain, where exiled ethnographers like *Bronisław Piłsudski* conducted pioneering field recordings of native shamans.
- [[buddhism]] — the institutional religious apparatus employed by Japanese authorities during the Edo and Meiji periods to register households and replace indigenous mortuary and spiritual customs.
- [[linguistics]] — the scientific discipline through which scholars study the endangered Ainu language isolate, which preserves the specialized poetic vocabulary, chants, and epics of shamanic seances.
- [[silk-road]] — the extensive trans-Eurasian trade network whose maritime northern branches brought Chinese bronze mirrors, glass beads, and Manchurian silks into Ainu ceremonial and shamanic life.
