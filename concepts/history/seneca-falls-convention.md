## summary

The **Seneca Falls Convention** was the foundational gathering of the American women's rights movement, held in Seneca Falls, New York in July 1848. Organized by [[Elizabeth Cady Stanton]] and [[Lucretia Mott]], the convention produced the Declaration of Sentiments, which explicitly demanded political equality and voting rights for women. The meeting marked the beginning of organized feminism in America and launched a struggle that would not achieve [[voting rights]] for women for over seventy years.

## you gotta know

- Held July 19–20, 1848 in Seneca Falls, New York, drawing about 300 people including men who supported women's equality.
- Organized by [[Elizabeth Cady Stanton]] and [[Lucretia Mott]], two activists who had been excluded from speaking at the 1840 World Anti-Slavery Convention in London.
- Produced the Declaration of Sentiments, modeled on the Declaration of Independence, asserting that "all men and women are created equal" with inalienable rights.
- Explicitly demanded woman suffrage—the right to vote—a radical claim at the time that even many sympathetic observers thought too extreme or impossible.
- Articulated grievances against laws that made women civilly and legally inferior: marriage laws, property restrictions, education denial, and political voicelessness.
- Addressed issues of women's bodily autonomy, employment, divorce rights, and education that extended far beyond voting rights, showing the breadth of feminist demands.
- [[Susan B. Anthony]] did not attend but quickly became a leading organizer of the subsequent women's rights movement, partnering with Stanton for decades.
- The convention established women's suffrage as the central goal of American feminism for the next seven decades until the [[Nineteenth Amendment]] (1920) finally granted voting rights.
