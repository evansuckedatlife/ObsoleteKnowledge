## summary

The **Commerce Clause** (Article I, Section 8 of the *United States Constitution*) grants Congress power to regulate interstate commerce. A seemingly narrow provision granting federal authority to manage trade between states, it became the constitutional engine for vast federal regulatory power over labor, civil rights, environmental protection, and consumer safety. Through judicial interpretation, the *Commerce Clause* transformed a limited federal government into a regulatory superpower.

## you gotta know

- The Commerce Clause grants Congress power to "regulate Commerce among the several States"; the Framers intended it to prevent states from erecting trade barriers and maintain a unified national market.
- Early interpretation was narrow: *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824) and *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) affirmed federal supremacy in interstate matters but still recognized limits to federal authority.
- The *New Deal* (1933–39) pushed the boundaries: *Wickard v. Filburn* (1942) upheld federal regulation of wheat production on a single farm because it affects interstate commerce, even if indirectly; this marked the effective end of judicially enforced limits on federal power.
- *Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States* (1964) upheld the *Civil Rights Act of 1964*'s prohibition of discrimination in public accommodations—a civil rights law—on *Commerce Clause* grounds, reasoning that segregation affected interstate travel and commerce.
- The Commerce Clause became the constitutional hook for federal regulation of everything from labor standards to environmental protection to gun sales; its sweep seemed unlimited until recent decisions like *United States v. Lopez* (1995) resurrected limits.
- Today, the *Commerce Clause* remains the primary source of federal legislative power, though the Supreme Court's current conservative majority has suggested some activities lack sufficient nexus to interstate commerce.
