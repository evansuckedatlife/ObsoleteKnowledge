## summary

The **Second Bank of the United States**, commonly known as the **Second Bank** or the **BUS**, was a federally chartered central banking corporation that managed the public credit, currency stabilization, and monetary policy of the [[united-states]] between 1816 and 1836. Headquartered in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, the powerful financial institution acted as the primary depository for federal tax revenue and restrained excessive paper money issuance by private state-chartered banks. Its disputed constitutional legitimacy and ultimate destruction during the bitter *Bank War* redefined American [[federalism]], vastly expanded executive power under [[jacksonian-democracy]], and fundamentally altered nineteenth-century national finance.

## you gotta know

- Vetoed and systematically dismantled by President *Andrew Jackson*, who vehemently denounced the institution as an unconstitutional aristocratic monopoly, subsequently removing federal deposits and distributing them among favored state-chartered "pet banks" during the dramatic *Bank War*.
- Formed the constitutional center of the landmark Supreme Court ruling *McCulloch v. Maryland* in 1819, in which Chief Justice *John Marshall* affirmed that the [[united-states-constitution]] granted Congress [[implied-powers]] under the Necessary and Proper Clause to establish a national bank, famously declaring that "the power to tax involves the power to destroy."
- Managed aggressively by its aristocratic third president, *Nicholas Biddle*, who retaliated against presidential hostility by deliberately contracting commercial credit and calling in bank loans to artificially induce an economic contraction, attempting to coerce public support for a recharter.
- Chartered in 1816 under Democratic-Republican President *James Madison*, who reversed his party's traditional strict-constructionist opposition to central banking in order to restore monetary order and service heavy sovereign debt following the War of 1812.
- Became the central campaign battleground of the presidential election of 1832, when National Republican candidate *Henry Clay* and Senator *Daniel Webster* pushed an early recharter bill through Congress to corner the president politically, triggering a popular backlash that secured *Jackson's* sweeping reelection.
- Incurred lasting regional resentment for exacerbating the devastating *Panic of 1819*, when early bank directors under *William Jones* and successor *Langdon Cheves* drastically restricted credit, called in state bank notes, and foreclosed on extensive western real estate.
- Housed in a celebrated Greek Revival landmark in Philadelphia designed by prominent architect *William Strickland*, featuring monumental Doric porticos consciously modeled after the Parthenon of [[ancient-greece]].

## connections

- [[united-states]] — the sovereign republic whose public finances, trade duties, and national circulating currency the bank was chartered to regulate.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — the foundational document whose Necessary and Proper Clause was authoritatively interpreted by the Supreme Court to validate federal corporations.
- [[jacksonian-democracy]] — the egalitarian political coalition that rallied behind *Andrew Jackson's* crusade against perceived financial privilege and corporate corruption.
- [[federalism]] — the constitutional doctrine governing state and federal authority tested when Maryland asserted its power to tax a federally chartered branch.
- [[implied-powers]] — the constitutional principle established in *McCulloch v. Maryland* establishing that congressional authority extends beyond explicitly enumerated text.
- [[ancient-greece]] — the classical civilization whose fifth-century BCE Athenian architecture directly provided the stylistic model for the bank's Philadelphia temple.
- [[election-of-1824]] — the contentious presidential election that fractured the Democratic-Republican party and set the stage for the partisan realignment of the bank conflict.
