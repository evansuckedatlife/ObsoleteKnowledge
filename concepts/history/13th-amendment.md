## summary

The *13th Amendment* to the *United States Constitution*, ratified in December 1865, abolished slavery throughout the United States and its territories. Authored by *President Abraham Lincoln* and championed through Congress during the final year of the *American Civil War*, the amendment represented the constitutional culmination of the anti-slavery movement and formally eradicated the institution that had defined American society for nearly three centuries.

## you gotta know

- *Abraham Lincoln* introduced the amendment as a war measure and moral imperative, framing abolition not merely as military necessity but as constitutional principle; its passage required a two-thirds majority in both houses of Congress.
- The amendment's language is stark: "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction."
- This exception clause—permitting involuntary servitude as criminal punishment—has had enormous consequences: it enabled convict leasing, chain gangs, and mass incarceration, which many scholars argue represents slavery's continuation under a different name.
- The amendment was ratified by 27 of the then-36 states, with ratification completed in December 1865, just months after *General Robert E. Lee*'s surrender at Appomattox; Southern states were coerced into ratifying as a condition of *Reconstruction*.
- The 13th Amendment was the first constitutional amendment to restrict individual liberty (rather than enlarging rights); it established the federal government's power to regulate labor and social conditions nationwide.
- The amendment's ratification did not end racial oppression; the subsequent *14th* and *15th Amendments*, *Reconstruction* legislation, and later *Civil Rights Act* were necessary to address the legal and de facto discrimination that persisted despite formal abolition.
