## summary

**Vaccination** is the medical practice of introducing a weakened or inactive form of a disease agent into the body to stimulate immunity without causing the disease itself. Pioneered by *Edward Jenner* in 1796 using *cowpox* to protect against *smallpox*, vaccination became the most effective tool in modern public health, preventing millions of deaths and enabling the eradication of diseases. The practice transformed from empirical observation to scientific principle when *germ theory* revealed that vaccines work by training the immune system to recognize and defeat pathogens.

## you gotta know

- *Edward Jenner* deliberately exposed an 8-year-old boy to *cowpox*, then *smallpox*, demonstrating immunity—a risky experiment that founded modern vaccination. His willingness to experiment on a child, though ethically troubling by modern standards, produced the first documented proof that vaccination worked.
- Vaccination differs from **Inoculation** (the earlier practice of deliberately infecting someone with mild disease); vaccination uses weakened or dead material instead. **Inoculation** was common in the Ottoman Empire and China centuries before Jenner and represented humanity's first attempts to control epidemic disease.
- *Smallpox* vaccination campaigns in the 19th and 20th centuries transformed public health, eventually eradicating the disease entirely by 1980.
- *Louis Pasteur* developed vaccines for *cholera* and *rabies*, proving that Jenner's principle applied to other diseases and validating vaccination as a general strategy.
- Vaccine types include *live attenuated* (weakened virus), *inactivated* (dead pathogen), *toxoid* (inactivated toxin), and *subunit* (isolated antigen)—each with different safety-efficacy profiles.
- Vaccination is population-level medicine: "herd immunity" occurs when enough people are vaccinated that disease cannot spread, protecting even the unvaccinated and immunocompromised.
- Opposition to vaccination emerged almost immediately after Jenner—religious objections, fears of contamination, and resistance to state mandates persist to the present day.
