## summary

**Colorado Amendment 2** was a contentious 1992 statewide ballot initiative that amended the Colorado State Constitution to prohibit all state and municipal protections against discrimination based on sexual orientation. Approved by fifty-three percent of voters, the measure invalidated existing anti-bias ordinances in Denver, Aspen, and Boulder and ignited nationwide boycotts. In the landmark 1996 ruling *Romer v. Evans*, the Supreme Court of the [[united-states]] struck down the amendment under the [[14th-amendment]], establishing foundational jurisprudence for gay civil rights.

## you gotta know

- Drafted and championed by the conservative group *Colorado for Family Values*, led by activist *Will Perkins*, in response to municipal ordinances protecting gay, lesbian, and bisexual individuals.
- Approved by Colorado voters in November 1992 with approximately 53.4 percent of the vote, altering the state constitution to preclude any branch of government from adopting protective civil classifications for sexual orientation.
- Earned Colorado the nationwide moniker *The Hate State*, triggering extensive travel, cultural, and economic boycotts from municipal governments, conventions, and prominent celebrities.
- Prompted a legal challenge filed by plaintiffs including public school teacher *Richard Evans* and tennis legend *Martina Navratilova*, naming Governor *Roy Romer* as the primary defendant.
- Struck down in May 1996 by the Supreme Court of the [[united-states]] in the landmark 6–3 decision *Romer v. Evans*, authored by Justice *Anthony Kennedy*.
- Grounded its constitutional invalidation on the *Equal Protection Clause* of the [[14th-amendment]], with the Court holding that the measure lacked even a rational basis and was born of animus.
- Established the vital legal principle that a state cannot classify a singled-out class of citizens to make it harder for them than for anyone else to seek protection from the normal political process.
- Featured a fierce dissenting opinion by Justice *Antonin Scalia*, who argued the measure represented a permissible and traditional democratic expression in a cultural dispute.
