## summary

**Atlantic trade** (also known as **transatlantic trade**) refers to the complex commercial and maritime network that spanned the Atlantic Ocean from the late fifteenth century into the nineteenth century, linking Europe, West Africa, and the Americas in the wake of the [[age-of-exploration]]. Often functioning through the **triangular trade**, this system facilitated the continuous exchange of European manufactured goods, millions of enslaved African laborers, and American agricultural commodities. The enterprise generated unprecedented wealth for European colonial powers, transformed global demographics and ecology, and established the institutional foundations for modern global capitalism and [[colonialism]].

## you gotta know

- The classic *triangular trade* network operated across three distinct legs: European merchant ships carried manufactured goods such as firearms, alcohol, and textiles to West Africa; the brutal *Middle Passage* transported millions of enslaved Africans across the ocean under horrific conditions; and plantation-grown raw materials were returned to European ports.
- Sugar formed the economic backbone and initial catalyst of the entire oceanic system, creating exceptionally lethal, labor-intensive plantation complexes in Portuguese Brazil, Spanish territories under [[spain]], and Caribbean colonies held by [[england]] and [[france]].
- The transatlantic slave trade caused immense demographic and institutional devastation across Western and Central Africa, leading to the forced displacement of more than twelve million human beings, destabilizing indigenous polities, and reshaping African regional warfare around captive procurement.
- European imperial powers managed these shipping lanes through *mercantilism*, implementing strict commercial barriers like the English *Navigation Acts* to ensure that high-value colonial commodities, including tobacco, sugar, rice, cotton, and dyestuffs, directly enriched the home metropolis while barring foreign competitors.
- Operating in tandem with the *Columbian Exchange*, these maritime voyages permanently altered global ecosystems and diets by circulating American cultigens such as maize, cassava, potatoes, and cacao throughout Europe and Africa, while introducing Eurasian livestock, sugarcane, and lethal epidemic diseases to the New World.
- Enormous capital accumulated from unfree plantation labor and commercial monopolies directly catalyzed modern financial capitalism, fostering the rise of modern chartered banks, commodity bourses, and marine insurance institutions such as *Lloyd's of London*, which later provided capital for the [[industrial-revolution]].
- Major European conflicts and treaties often pivoted on trading rights like the *asiento*, an exclusive royal monopoly license granted by the Spanish monarchy to supply enslaved African laborers to Spanish America, which the [[british-empire]] famously acquired following the 1713 *Treaty of Utrecht*.

## connections

- [[age-of-exploration]] — pioneered the oceanic navigation routes and cartographic advances that integrated the Atlantic basin into a unified global economy.
- [[colonialism]] — provided the ideological, military, and political framework used by European powers to extract resources and establish settler and plantation regimes.
- [[british-empire]] — emerged as the dominant maritime force in Atlantic shipping, leveraging commercial dominance and chartered monopolies to expand its imperial reach.
- [[spain]] — established early transatlantic circuits transporting silver from the Americas and pioneered initial colonial plantation models in the Caribbean.
- [[france]] — accumulated massive imperial revenues through its sugar-producing Caribbean colonies, particularly *Saint-Domingue*, before the Haitian Revolution disrupted its empire.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — profoundly stimulated by the abundant raw materials, colonial captive consumer markets, and mercantile capital produced by Atlantic commerce.
- [[silk-road]] — the ancient overland network whose commercial prominence was eclipsed as maritime Atlantic commerce reoriented world trade patterns toward the ocean.
