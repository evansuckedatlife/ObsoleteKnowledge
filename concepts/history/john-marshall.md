# John Marshall

## summary

**John Marshall** (1755–1835) was the fourth Chief Justice of the United States and the most influential jurist in American history, whose interpretation of the Constitution established judicial review, expanded federal power, and shaped American constitutional law for two centuries. His landmark decisions transformed the Supreme Court from a minor institution into a co-equal branch of government capable of striking down legislative and executive acts.

## you gotta know

- Marshall was born in Virginia and served as a soldier in the *Continental Army*, later practicing law; he was appointed to Congress, served briefly as Secretary of State under *John Adams*, and then appointed Chief Justice in 1801—a position he held for 34 years until his death.
- *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) was his most consequential decision: the Court invalidated a minor provision of the *Judiciary Act of 1789*, establishing the principle of *judicial review*—the power of courts to declare laws unconstitutional and void.
- His expansive interpretation of federal power in *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) upheld the constitutionality of the *Bank of the United States* and asserted that the Constitution's "necessary and proper" clause gave Congress broad powers; he rejected state claims to nullify federal law.
- *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824) established a broad interpretation of Congress's *commerce clause* authority, empowering federal government to regulate interstate commerce and preempt state regulations; this decision undergirded federal regulatory power for centuries.
- Marshall's jurisprudence consistently favored national over state power, contractual property rights over populist claims, and judicial authority over executive and legislative prerogatives; these principles aligned with Federalist ideology and influenced American constitutional development.
- His long tenure (1801–35) allowed him to shape the Court during crucial decades; his consensus-building and moderate tone (despite bold constitutional innovations) gave his decisions credibility and durability.
