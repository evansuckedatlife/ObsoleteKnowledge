## summary

**Russian nihilism** was an influential philosophical and cultural movement that swept the [[russian-empire]] during the 1860s and 1870s, characterized by the wholesale rejection of established social institutions, traditional morality, Orthodox [[christianity]], and tsarist autocracy. Rooted in philosophical materialism, radical utilitarianism, and reverence for natural sciences, the movement demanded total intellectual liberation from patriarchal customs and aristocratic privilege. It profoundly shaped the generation of radical intelligentsia, serving as an intellectual precursor to revolutionary [[populism]] and the clandestine political violence that culminated in the assassination of Tsar *Alexander II*.

## you gotta know

- *Ivan Turgenev* famously popularized the term in his 1862 novel *Fathers and Sons*, crystallizing the movement through the medical student *Bazarov*, who declares that a nihilist is a man who bows to no authority and accepts no principle on faith alone.
- *Nikolay Chernyshevsky* provided the movement's ideological manifesto with his 1863 prison-written novel *What Is to Be Done?*, depicting ascetic revolutionaries guided by rational egoism and communal labor, which later became the defining blueprint for generations of Russian radicals, including *Vladimir Lenin*.
- Unlike Western philosophical nihilism, which often drifted toward existential despair or moral skepticism, Russian nihilists were fiercely optimistic materialists who viewed empirical science, utilitarianism, and physiological study as tools for universal human emancipation.
- *Fyodor Dostoevsky* vehemently opposed the nihilists, writing *Crime and Punishment* to critique rationalist morality and *Demons* to expose the destructive, amoral fanaticism of clandestine political conspirators.
- *Dmitry Pisarev*, writing for the radical journal *Russkoye Slovo*, became the leading real-world spokesman for the movement, arguing that aesthetic art and poetry were useless bourgeois distractions compared to scientific advancement and practical boots.
- The movement marked a sharp generational rupture between the aristocratic "fathers" of the 1840s—idealistic romantic liberals influenced by [[germany]] and the [[enlightenment]]—and the plebeian "children" (*raznochintsy*) of the 1860s, who embraced hard materialism and severed social decorum.
- Nihilist fashion and lifestyle functioned as active public rebellion: male nihilists grew unkempt long hair, wore blue-tinted spectacles, and threw heavy plaid shawls over their shoulders, while female nihilists cropped their hair short, wore unadorned black wool dresses, and entered sham marriages (*fiktivnye braki*) to escape patriarchal households and pursue higher education abroad.
- The movement radicalized from intellectual nonconformity into violent conspiratorial action under figures like *Sergey Nechayev*, whose chilling 1869 tract *Catechism of a Revolutionary* argued that the true radical must sever all personal bonds and embrace ruthless terror, foreshadowing organizations like *[[narodnaya-volya]]*.

## connections

- [[russian-empire]] — the imperial autocracy whose rigid social hierarchy and censorship provoked radical revolt.
- [[existentialism]] — the broader philosophical tradition addressing meaninglessness, which later examined similar questions of moral foundation.
- [[enlightenment]] — the intellectual source of rationalism and secular empiricism that nihilists adapted into absolute materialism.
- [[french-revolution]] — the historical archetype of revolutionary upheaval that inspired radical Russian political aspirations.
- [[christianity]] — the state-enforced Russian Orthodox faith whose dogma and moral doctrines nihilists systematically rejected.
- [[germany]] — the philosophical origin of the Left Hegelian and materialist ideas that fueled radical student societies.
- [[soviet-union]] — the subsequent socialist state shaped by radical traditions originating in Chernyshevsky's ideological lineage.
