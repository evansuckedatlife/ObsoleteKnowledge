## summary

**Endurance**, originally christened *Polaris*, was the three-masted wooden barquentine that carried *Sir Ernest Shackleton* and his twenty-seven-man crew on the ill-fated *Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition* of 1914–1917. Trapped and slowly crushed by pack ice within the treacherous Weddell Sea, the vessel's destruction catalyzed one of the most celebrated epics of survival and human leadership in maritime history. After camping on shifting floes for months, the crew reached remote Elephant Island, enabling Shackleton's desperate open-boat voyage aboard the *James Caird* to secure the rescue of every single crew member.

## you gotta know

- Constructed in Norway at the Framnæs shipyard under master shipwright *Christian Christensen*, featuring exceptionally dense oak, fir, and greenheart hull planking engineered to withstand polar ice packs.
- Renamed by *Ernest Shackleton* after his ancestral family motto, *Fortitudine vincimus* ("By endurance we conquer").
- Departed South Georgia Island in December 1914 with twenty-eight men and sixty-nine sled dogs aboard, aiming to complete the first overland crossing of the Antarctic continent.
- Became completely beset by dense sea ice in January 1915 at coordinates 76°58′S, drifting helplessly northwestward with the Weddell Sea pack ice for over ten months.
- Sustained fatal structural damage under catastrophic compressive ice pressures, forcing Shackleton to order the crew to abandon ship in October 1915 before the vessel sank beneath the ice in November 1915.
- Provided timber, canvas, stores, and three lifeboats—including the twenty-two-foot *James Caird*—that proved essential for the expedition's miraculous overland and sea survival.
- Allowed the crew to survive over a year of drifting ice floes, brutal blizzards, and starvation rations, culminating in a desperate 800-mile open-sea voyage to South Georgia without a single loss of life.
- Located in an extraordinary state of preservation in March 2022 by the *Endurance22* expedition at a depth of nearly 10,000 feet, resting upright on the Antarctic seabed with its stern lettering intact.
