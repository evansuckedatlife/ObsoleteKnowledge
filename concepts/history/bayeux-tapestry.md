## summary

The **Bayeux Tapestry** is a monumental embroidered linen cloth measuring nearly seventy meters long, created in the late eleventh century to depict the events leading up to the Norman Conquest of [[england]]. Commissioned shortly after 1066, likely in southern [[england]] for display in Bayeux Cathedral in Normandy, [[france]], it visually chronicles the political intrigues and military campaigns that culminated in the decisive [[battle-of-hastings]]. It remains one of the most celebrated surviving masterpieces of Romanesque narrative art, providing historians with an extraordinary pictorial window into medieval warfare, maritime technology, and Anglo-Norman material culture.

## you gotta know

- Depicts the dynastic succession crisis and cross-Channel invasion of 1066, culminating in the [[battle-of-hastings]] where [[william-the-conqueror]], Duke of Normandy, defeated King *Harold Godwinson* to seize the throne of [[england]].
- Despite its traditional English name, the artifact is technically an embroidery rather than a true woven tapestry, expertly stitched with colored wool yarns in stem and laid-and-couching stitches onto nine joined panels of flax linen.
- Contains one of the earliest and most famous artistic representations of [[halley-s-comet]], shown blazing across the upper frieze under the Latin caption *Isti Mirant Stella* as terrified courtiers interpret it as an omen of doom.
- Commissioned by *Bishop Odo of Bayeux*, the maternal half-brother of [[william-the-conqueror]], and executed by skilled Anglo-Saxon needleworkers in Kent rather than stitched by William's wife *Queen Matilda* as local folklore claimed.
- Illustrates the legendary death of King *Harold Godwinson* under the embroidered titulus *Harold Rex Interfectus Est*, showing a figure with an arrow in his eye beside a falling soldier trampled by Norman cavalry.
- Requisitioned in 1803 by [[napoleon-bonaparte]], who put the textile on public display in Paris to inspire anti-British sentiment and drum up national support for his planned invasion of [[england]].
- Flanked by continuous upper and lower margins featuring mythological beasts, pastoral labors, fables from *Aesop*, and in the final battle sequences, graphic depictions of stripped corpses and severed limbs.
- Features enigmatic and unresolved historical vignettes, such as an unnamed tonsured clerk reaching out to strike or touch the cheek of a woman labeled *Ælfgyva*, an allusion to an eleventh-century scandal whose exact context is lost.

## connections

- [[battle-of-hastings]] — the decisive 1066 confrontation that forms the narrative climax and conclusion of the embroidered scroll.
- [[william-the-conqueror]] — the Norman duke whose claim to the English crown is legitimized and celebrated throughout the composition.
- [[england]] — the kingdom conquered in the depicted campaign, where English artisans in Canterbury almost certainly stitched the textile.
- [[france]] — the modern nation that preserves the artifact in Normandy, where it was originally consecrated in Bayeux Cathedral.
- [[napoleon-bonaparte]] — the French ruler who exhibited the embroidery in Paris in 1803 to promote his planned cross-Channel invasion.
- [[halley-s-comet]] — the celestial apparition depicted in panel thirty-two as an omen foretelling dynastic overthrow.
- [[christianity]] — the religious institution represented by *Bishop Odo*, who sponsored the work to decorate his newly built cathedral.
