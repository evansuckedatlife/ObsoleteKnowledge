## summary

**Self-incrimination** is the act of exposing oneself to criminal prosecution, accusation, or legal liability through one's own testimony, a vulnerability counterbalanced in common-law legal systems by the **privilege against self-incrimination**. Most famously enshrined in the *Self-Incrimination Clause* of the [[fifth-amendment]] to the [[united-states-constitution]], this fundamental legal doctrine provides that no person may be compelled by the government to serve as a witness against themselves in a criminal proceeding. Originating from seventeenth-century resistance to inquisitorial oaths in *[[england]]* and refined by thinkers of the *[[enlightenment]]*, the **right against self-incrimination** serves as a vital cornerstone of modern adversarial criminal procedure and constitutional liberty across the [[united-states]].

## you gotta know

- Colloquially known as "pleading the Fifth," the protection allows criminal defendants to decline to testify at trial and permits witnesses in any legal proceeding to refuse answers that could subject them to prosecution.
- In the landmark 1966 decision *[[miranda-v-arizona]]*, the *Supreme Court* under Chief Justice *Earl Warren* established that custodial police interrogation is inherently coercive, requiring law enforcement to advise detained suspects of their right to remain silent.
- The constitutional privilege extends strictly to testimonial and communicative evidence; the government may legally compel physical evidence, including blood samples, fingerprints, voice exemplars, and handwriting specimens.
- Originally restricting only federal proceedings under the *[[bill-of-rights]]*, the protection was incorporated against state governments via the Due Process Clause of the [[14th-amendment]] in the 1964 case *Malloy v. Hogan*.
- Under the precedent of *Griffin v. California*, judges and prosecutors are strictly prohibited from commenting on a defendant's refusal to testify or encouraging jurors to draw an adverse inference of guilt from their silence.
- The government can overcome the privilege by granting formal immunity; *Kastigar v. United States* held that "use and derivative use" immunity is constitutionally sufficient to compel testimony because it prevents any compelled statements or resulting evidence from being used against the witness.
- Under the collective entity doctrine, the privilege is personal to natural persons and cannot be asserted by corporations, labor unions, or partnerships to shield institutional records from subpoenas.
- The common-law privilege crystallized from the Latin maxim *nemo tenetur seipsum accusare* ("no man is bound to accuse himself"), galvanized in seventeenth-century *[[england]]* by Leveller *John Lilburne*'s refusal to take the inquisitorial *ex officio* oath before the *Court of Star Chamber*.

## connections

- [[fifth-amendment]] — constitutional provision containing the explicit clause that no person shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.
- [[miranda-v-arizona]] — landmark ruling establishing mandatory procedural warnings to safeguard suspects against coercive police interrogation.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — supreme legal framework of the [[united-states]] that enshrines the privilege among core procedural protections.
- [[14th-amendment]] — constitutional amendment whose Due Process Clause incorporated the self-incrimination guarantee to bind state courts.
- [[due-process]] — constitutional guarantee of fundamental procedural fairness, historically and doctrinally intertwined with protections against coerced testimony.
- [[bill-of-rights]] — foundational charter of ten amendments ratified in 1791 to protect individual citizens from governmental overreach.
- [[enlightenment]] — intellectual movement whose ideals regarding individual liberty and limits on state coercion shaped modern criminal jurisprudence.
- [[england]] — birthplace of the common-law privilege, established in reaction against inquisitorial practices of the Star Chamber.
