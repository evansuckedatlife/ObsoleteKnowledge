## summary

**Free Silver** was a late-19th-century political movement advocating unlimited coinage of silver into money alongside gold, aiming to expand the money supply and aid agrarian and working-class voters. Championed by populists and progressives, it became a central issue in the 1896 presidential election, ultimately defeated by the McKinley administration's commitment to gold-only currency.

## you gotta know

- Emerged as a response to agricultural depression and the *Panic of 1893*, when farmers sought currency expansion to raise commodity prices and ease debt burdens caused by deflation.
- The Panic of 1893 crushed agricultural prices and threw millions of Americans out of work, creating urgent political pressure for monetary reform and relief.
- Argued that unlimited coinage of silver into money would inflate the money supply, raise agricultural prices, and redistribute wealth from creditors to debtors, helping farmers pay debts.
- The movement appealed especially to farming regions of the South and West, where agricultural depression was most severe and resentment of eastern financial interests was highest.
- Met fierce opposition from eastern bankers and gold-standard advocates who feared inflation would destabilize the currency and harm financial interests, making investments risky.
- The debate pitted rural agricultural interests against urban financial interests, creating a sharp sectional divide between America's regions.
- Backed by the *Populist Party* and adopted by *William Jennings Bryan* as the centerpiece of his 1896 Democratic presidential campaign, uniting Democrats and Populists.
- *Bryan*'s famous *Cross of Gold* speech (1896) electrified the convention: "You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold!" became the iconic phrase of the movement.
- Soundly defeated when *William McKinley* won the 1896 election and committed the nation firmly to the gold standard, ending serious debate over the issue for decades.
- Symbolized the defeat of agrarian interests in national politics and represented the last major 19th-century challenge to gold-standard orthodoxy.
