## summary

**Interstate Commerce** refers to trade and economic transactions between and across the states of the *United States*, a domain explicitly placed under federal authority by the *Commerce Clause* of the Constitution. The power to regulate interstate commerce became one of the most important and expansive federal powers, allowing Congress to address economic matters spanning state lines. This constitutional authority has been the basis for major legislation and judicial decisions that shaped American federalism and expanded federal power.

## you gotta know

- The Commerce Clause (Article I, Section 8) grants Congress power "to regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States, and with the Indian Tribes"—one of the most expansively interpreted clauses in the Constitution.
- Intended to prevent states from enacting protectionist policies that would balkanize the national economy and harm interstate trade; the Framers had witnessed how economic warfare between states threatened the union under the Articles of Confederation.
- [[Gibbons v. Ogden]] (1824) established that interstate commerce included more than just goods—it encompassed navigation, transportation, and commercial activity affecting interstate trade; Chief Justice Marshall rejected the idea that commerce power was narrowly limited.
- [[Wickard v. Filburn]] (1942) dramatically expanded federal power by holding that even local wheat production (consumed on the farmer's own farm) affecting national grain markets could be regulated; this allowed federal control of agricultural production.
- Used to justify civil rights legislation: [[Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States]] (1964) upheld the [[Civil Rights Act of 1964]] as a valid exercise of the commerce power because motels affect interstate commerce and segregation burdens it.
- Provides constitutional basis for federal regulation of railroads, airlines, telecommunications, environmental protection, labor standards, and countless industries spanning state boundaries; this makes it the foundation for most modern federal regulatory authority.
- Demonstrates how constitutional interpretation evolves: the clause's scope has expanded from narrow commercial regulation (early 1800s) to encompassing broad economic and social policy (1900s onward); some argue it has expanded so much that limits are now purely political, not constitutional.
