## summary

**Free Speech** is the right to express ideas without government censorship or punishment, enshrined in the U.S. Constitution's *First Amendment* and now regarded as foundational to democratic society. Though the Framers embraced the principle, American law has repeatedly strained against it—from the *Alien and Sedition Acts* of 1798 to prosecutions during wartime. The meaning of free speech has evolved through court cases that balance expression against national security, defamation, and public order.

## you gotta know

- Protected by the *First Amendment* as part of the Bill of Rights, adopted in 1791 as a check on federal power.
- The *Alien and Sedition Acts* of 1798 criminalized seditious speech, demonstrating early tension between free speech and governmental security concerns.
- *Schenck v. United States* (1919) established the *"clear and present danger"* test, permitting suppression of speech that poses immediate risk.
- *Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.* became the jurisprudential champion of free-speech protections, writing eloquent opinions defending robust public debate.
- Courts distinguish between categories of speech: political speech receives the strongest protection; commercial speech and obscenity less protection.
- Remains contested—modern debates center on hate speech, incitement, online content, and the power of platforms to regulate expression.
- Not absolute: exceptions include direct incitement to violence, true threats, defamation, and national security concerns, though courts have gradually narrowed these exceptions over time.
- The Pentagon Papers case (1971) reaffirmed strong protection for press freedom even when publishing classified government documents about past government wrongdoing and deceptions.
- Free speech remains contested: societies continue debating where boundaries lie between protecting expression and preventing harm, particularly regarding social media, corporate speech, and speech targeting vulnerable groups.
