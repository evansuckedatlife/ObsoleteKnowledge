## summary

The **Fugitive Slave Act** refers most famously to the federal statute enacted by the *United States Congress* in September 1850 as an essential concession to the South within the *Compromise of 1850*. Building upon the earlier **Fugitive Slave Act of 1793**, this revised **Fugitive Slave Law** mandated that federal marshals, local authorities, and private citizens across free northern states assist in the capture and rendition of escaped enslaved persons. By stripping accused African Americans of due process, establishing federal commissioners with financial incentives for rendition, and penalizing northern noncompliance, the act radicalized anti-slavery sentiment across the northern *United States* and accelerated the nation's descent into the [[american-civil-war]].

## you gotta know

- Part of the *Compromise of 1850*, engineered by Kentucky Senator *[[henry-clay]]* and Illinois Senator *Stephen A. Douglas* to resolve territorial disputes arising from the Mexican-American War.
- Created specially appointed federal commissioners who received ten dollars when ruling in favor of a slaveholder claimant, but only five dollars if they found in favor of the alleged fugitive.
- Denied accused runaways the right to testify in their own defense and prohibited the use of a jury trial, requiring only an ex parte affidavit from a claimant to authorize rendition.
- Compelled ordinary citizens in free states under penalty of heavy fines and imprisonment to join federal posses to capture suspected fugitives, enraging northern public opinion.
- Sparked aggressive northern resistance, prompting several state legislatures to pass nullifying "personal liberty laws" and inciting urban crowds to orchestrate the armed rescues of *Anthony Burns* in Boston and *Joshua Glover* in Milwaukee.
- Directly prompted *Harriet Beecher Stowe* to write her epochal anti-slavery novel *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, which dramatized the cruelty of northern slave catchers and galvanized abolitionist activism.
- Upheld by Chief Justice *Roger B. Taney* in the landmark Supreme Court decision *Ableman v. Booth* (1859), which rebuked Wisconsin's attempt to annul federal authority over fugitive recovery.
- Replaced the earlier **Fugitive Slave Act of 1793**, which had derived from the Fugitive Slave Clause of the [[united-states-constitution]] but had been rendered toothless by the 1842 Supreme Court ruling in *Prigg v. Pennsylvania*.

## connections

- [[united-states-constitution]] — derived its legal authority from the Fugitive Slave Clause located in Article IV, Section 2.
- [[american-civil-war]] — intensified northern moral outrage and southern resentment, serving as a direct catalyst for secession.
- [[reconstruction-era]] — overturned when Congress repealed all fugitive slave statutes in 1864, paving the way for postwar civil rights legislation.
- [[united-states]] — transformed federal power by overriding northern state autonomy and compelling local citizens to enforce slaveholding property rights.
- [[american-literature]] — inspired abolitionist masterworks, including *Uncle Tom's Cabin* and the defiant essays of *Ralph Waldo Emerson*.
- [[henry-clay]] — crafted the *Compromise of 1850* to avert southern secession, viewing the statute as an indispensable concession to slave states.
- [[daniel-webster]] — damaged his Massachusetts political career by delivering the "Seventh of March" speech defending the enforcement of the act to preserve the Union.
- [[fur-trade]] — expanded American territorial claims in the West, which repeatedly reignited congressional crises over the territorial status of slavery.
