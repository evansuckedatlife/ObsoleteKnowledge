## summary

The **1990s prosperity**, frequently termed the **1990s economic boom** or the **Clinton boom**, was an extended era of rapid macroeconomic expansion, record job creation, and historic fiscal turnaround in the [[united-states]]. Spanning from the post-recession recovery in March 1991 until the market contraction in March 2001, this 120-month period stood as the longest continuous peacetime expansion in American economic history. The decade was fueled by tremendous productivity surges from personal computers and the commercial internet, the realization of a post-[[cold-war]] peace dividend, and the emergence of consecutive federal budget surpluses under President *Bill Clinton*. Its combination of robust gross domestic product growth, tumbling poverty rates, and low inflation redefined modern expectations for monetary and fiscal policy.

## you gotta know

- Marked the longest continuous economic expansion in modern American history at the time, enduring for 120 consecutive months from the trough of the 1990–1991 recession to the onset of the 2001 contraction.
- Driven by the rapid proliferation of personal computing, fiber-optic telecommunications, and the commercial internet, which triggered speculative investment and immense asset valuation during the *dot-com boom*.
- Produced four consecutive federal budget surpluses between fiscal years 1998 and 2001—the first since 1969—accelerated by the tax reforms of the *Deficit Reduction Act of 1993* and the *Balanced Budget Act of 1997*.
- Navigated by *Federal Reserve* Chairman *Alan Greenspan*, who accommodated technology-driven productivity growth with measured interest rates while famously coining the phrase *irrational exuberance* during a 1996 dinner speech.
- Shaped by the economic philosophy known as *Rubinomics*, named for Treasury Secretary *Robert Rubin*, which held that lowering the federal deficit would reduce long-term bond yields, stimulate private business investment, and sustain growth.
- Witnessed unemployment fall to 3.8 percent in April 2000 while inflation remained unexpectedly subdued, challenging long-standing macroeconomic theories concerning the trade-off modeled by the *Phillips curve*.
- Embraced globalization and market liberalization through the passage of major trade pacts, including the contentious ratification of [[nafta]] in 1993 and the formal accession of new partners into the *World Trade Organization*.
- Dissolved in early 2001 following the precipitous crash of the tech-heavy *NASDAQ* index, an overabundance of speculative capital, and the revelation of high-profile accounting scandals at firms like *Enron*.

## connections

- [[united-states]] — the country that served as the primary epicenter of the technological surge, financial market gains, and macroeconomic expansion.
- [[cold-war]] — the dissolution of the Soviet bloc allowed the federal government to reduce military outlays, releasing a peace dividend that aided deficit elimination.
- [[nafta]] — the signature North American trade pact ratified under Clinton that lowered commercial barriers and came to symbolize the era's free-trade consensus.
- [[welfare-reform]] — the landmark 1996 legislation that replaced open-ended entitlement programs with work requirements during a time of peak labor market absorption.
- [[the-lewinsky-scandal]] — the high-profile White House controversy whose political consequences were heavily blunted by public satisfaction with ongoing financial stability.
- [[impeachment-of-bill-clinton]] — the 1998–1999 impeachment effort that failed to secure a conviction partly because the president presided over historic peace and prosperity.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — the historical era frequently cited as a predecessor to the 1990s information-age transformation in workplace automation and communication networks.
