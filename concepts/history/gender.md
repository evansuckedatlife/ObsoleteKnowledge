## summary

**Gender** is understood in modern philosophy and social theory as a socially constructed system of roles, behaviors, and identities rather than as a direct biological fact. This conceptual shift—crystallized by *Simone de Beauvoir* and later theorized by *Judith Butler*—fundamentally transformed how scholars, activists, and societies understand the relationship between sex, identity, and power.

## you gotta know

- *Simone de Beauvoir's* 1949 declaration "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" in *The Second Sex* established that gender is constructed through lived experience and social expectation, not determined by biology alone.
- The distinction between *sex* (biological) and *gender* (social/cultural) became a foundational concept in feminism, enabling analysis of how social systems shape identity beyond anatomy.
- Early second-wave feminists used this distinction to argue that women's historical subordination was not inevitable but rather a product of changeable social structures.
- *Judith Butler's* theory of **gender performativity** (articulated in *Gender Trouble*, 1990) argued that gender is constituted through repeated acts and performances, not an innate essence—one *does* gender rather than *is* gender.
- Butler's work radically challenged the idea of a stable "female" or "male" subject, suggesting instead that gender categories are themselves performed into existence.
- **Gender roles** have historically been enforced through legal, economic, and cultural systems that assign different rights, responsibilities, and possibilities to different genders.
- Western societies enforced gender divisions through property law (coverture), education exclusions, labor market segregation, and family structure requirements tied to reproduction.
- The recognition of gender as constructed enabled critique of patriarchal systems as mutable rather than natural, opening space for liberation movements and identity diversity.
- Contemporary usage distinguishes gender identity (one's internal sense of gender) from gender expression (outward presentation) from biological sex, recognizing that these need not align.
- **Gender performativity** has implications for transgender and non-binary identities, suggesting that gender can be iteratively revised through performance and social recognition.
- Gender theory has become central to understanding power relations, labor division, violence, and identity in modern societies; it extends beyond binary categories to encompass non-binary and genderqueer experiences.

## connections

- [[simone-de-beauvoir]] — the existentialist philosopher who established gender as constructed.
- [[judith-butler]] — the theorist who articulated gender performativity.
- [[existentialism]] — the philosophical tradition emphasizing human freedom and construction of identity.
- [[queer-theory]] — the theoretical framework that builds on gender as performative and fluid.
- [[second-wave-feminism]] — the movement that weaponized gender theory for liberation.
- [[sexuality]] — closely related but distinct category of identity and desire.
