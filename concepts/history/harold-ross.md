## summary

**Harold Ross** was an influential American journalist and editor who co-founded *The New Yorker* magazine in 1925 alongside his wife *Jane Grant*. Ross served as the publication's editor-in-chief from its fragile inception until his death in 1951, forging an unmistakable tone of urban wit, exacting prose, factual rigor, and satirical sophistication. Under his eccentric leadership, the weekly established lasting benchmarks for 20th-century literary journalism, biographical profiles, and contemporary humor.

## you gotta know

- Co-founded *The New Yorker* in February 1925 with financial backing from yeast heir *Raoul Fleischmann*, famously declaring in its prospectus that it was "not edited for the old lady in Dubuque."
- Edited the military trench newspaper *Stars and Stripes* in France during World War I, where he forged lifelong relationships with writers *Alexander Woollcott* and *Franklin P. Adams*.
- Participated actively in the witty literary gatherings of the *Algonquin Round Table*, drawing early contributors and columnists directly from the circle.
- Established legendary editorial standards that pioneered exhaustive fact-checking departments, obsessive grammatical precision, and relentless stylistic queries on manuscripts.
- Mentored and championed major 20th-century writers and illustrators, including *E.B. White*, *James Thurber*, *Dorothy Parker*, *Peter Arno*, and *Charles Addams*.
- Created enduring editorial departments and structural hallmarks of the magazine, such as "The Talk of the Town", full-page biographical Profiles, and single-panel cartoon gags.
- Published *John Hersey*'s groundbreaking, entire-issue reportage *Hiroshima* in August 1946, demonstrating the magazine's capacity for profound moral journalism.
