## summary

The **Conservation Movement** was a Progressive Era initiative (roughly 1900–1920) to protect America's natural resources, forests, and wilderness from unchecked exploitation by logging companies, mining interests, and development. Led by *Theodore Roosevelt* and his chief forester *Gifford Pinchot*, conservationists advocated for scientific management of public lands, the establishment of national parks and forests, and federal regulation of resource extraction. Though distinct from modern environmentalism (which emphasizes preservation for its own sake), conservation established the principle that natural resources belong to the public and should be managed for long-term national benefit rather than immediate private profit.

## you gotta know

- Emerged during the Progressive Era as part of broader reform efforts to challenge corporate monopolies and assert public interest against unregulated business exploitation.
- Focused on the American West, where vast public lands and forests faced rapid deforestation, overgrazing, and resource depletion from private corporations; conservationists sought to reverse destructive practices and preserve resources for future generations.
- *Theodore Roosevelt*, serving as President (1901–1909), was the movement's most prominent political champion; he dramatically expanded federal land protection through executive action, protected 230 million acres through national forests, parks, and reserves.
- *Gifford Pinchot*, Roosevelt's chief forester and head of the U.S. Forest Service, championed "scientific forestry"—the principle that forests should be managed rationally for sustained yield rather than clearcut and abandoned.
- Established the U.S. Forest Service (1905) to manage national forests under sustainable principles; created national parks (including the Grand Canyon) and wildlife reserves to protect endangered species and scenic areas.
- Distinguished "conservation" (sustainable use of resources) from "preservation" (setting aside lands untouched); this distinction sometimes divided conservationists, particularly in disputes over dam-building and mining on public lands.
- Implemented regulations on hunting and fishing to prevent species extinction; recognized that wildlife required protected habitats and laws limiting harvest.
- Faced opposition from mining, logging, and ranching interests who viewed conservation restrictions as government overreach and economic burden; tensions between resource extraction and conservation persist today.
- Influenced later environmental movements and established precedent for federal stewardship of public lands; Roosevelt's Conservation Movement laid groundwork for the Environmental Movement of the 1960s onward.
