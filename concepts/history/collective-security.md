## summary

**Collective security** is the principle that an attack on one nation constitutes a threat to all, binding countries to defend each other through collective action rather than unilateral force or alliances. Developed after *World War I* as an alternative to the balance-of-power politics that produced imperial wars, it was enshrined in the *League of Nations* and later *NATO*. Though elegant in theory, collective security repeatedly failed—most catastrophically in the 1930s when aggressors exploited the *League*'s lack of enforcement power.

## you gotta know

- Collective security emerged from the *Paris Peace Conference* (1919) and the *League of Nations Covenant* (1920), intended to prevent future wars by making aggression illegitimate and requiring member nations to enforce peace collectively.
- The League's fundamental weakness was lack of enforcement: it had no standing army and member nations could opt out of military action; when *Japan* invaded *Manchuria* (1931) and *Italy* invaded *Ethiopia* (1935), the League imposed only ineffectual sanctions.
- *Nazi Germany* exploited this paralysis: the *Munich Conference* (1938) exemplified appeasement, where France and Britain sacrificed *Czechoslovakia* to Hitler without invoking collective security, hoping to avoid war rather than enforce the system.
- *World War II* discredited the League and prompted redesign of collective security in the *United Nations Charter* (1945), which created the *Security Council* with permanent members (veto power) and enforcement authority.
- *NATO* (1949) represented a regional variant of collective security against *Soviet Union*, binding North Atlantic democracies to common defense under *Article 5*: an attack on one is an attack on all.
- The concept remains aspirational: the *United Nations* lacks enforcement power; regional alliances succeed irregularly; major powers continue pursuing national interest over collective obligation.
