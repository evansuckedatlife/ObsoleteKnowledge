# Darius III

## summary

**Darius III** (ruled 336–330 BCE) was the last king of the *Achaemenid Persian Empire*, facing the unprecedented challenge of *Alexander the Great's* invasion. Though he inherited a vast and wealthy empire, Darius III was unable to marshal effective resistance to Alexander's military genius and was ultimately defeated in three decisive battles, ending over two centuries of Persian imperial supremacy and reshaping the ancient world.

## you gotta know

- Darius III ascended to the Persian throne in 336 BCE as a junior member of the Achaemenid dynasty, a period when internal political instability had weakened the empire's cohesion and military readiness.
- *Alexander the Great* invaded Persia in 334 BCE with roughly 35,000 troops; Darius III commanded vastly larger armies but faced strategic challenges in coordinating his empire's forces and countering Alexander's tactical innovations.
- The three major battles between Darius and Alexander defined the campaign: the *Granicus River* (334 BCE), *Issus* (333 BCE), and *Gaugamela* (331 BCE), each a decisive defeat that progressively weakened Persian resistance.
- At *Gaugamela*, the final and most decisive battle, Darius commanded perhaps 1 million troops (likely an exaggeration by ancient sources) against Alexander's roughly 47,000; Alexander's maneuvering routed the Persian forces despite their numerical superiority.
- Darius fled the battlefield at Gaugamela and was assassinated by *Bessus*, a satrap (provincial governor), in 330 BCE while attempting to raise new forces; his death marked the effective end of Achaemenid rule.
- The swift collapse of the Persian Empire was shocking to contemporaries: an empire that had dominated the ancient world for two centuries fell to a young Macedonian general and his army within four years.
- Darius III is often portrayed as an ineffectual ruler in Greek and Macedonian sources (which are our primary sources); modern historians note that he faced an unprecedented military challenge and that his defeat reflects Alexander's exceptional generalship rather than Darius's incompetence.
