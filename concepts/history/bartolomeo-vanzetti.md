## summary

**Bartolomeo Vanzetti** was an Italian-born anarchist, labour activist, and fish peddler whose arrest, trial, and execution in the [[united-states]] became one of the most polarizing legal controversies of the twentieth century. Convicted alongside shoemaker *Nicola Sacco* for a deadly 1920 payroll robbery in South Braintree, Massachusetts, he maintained his innocence through seven years of failed appeals. His case unfolded against the backdrop of the First Red Scare following [[world-war-i]], exposing deep xenophobic hostility toward immigrant radicals and transforming him into an enduring international martyr for civil liberties and the American labour movement.

## you gotta know

- Convicted alongside fellow Italian anarchist *Nicola Sacco* for the April 15, 1920 armed robbery and murder of paymaster *Frederick Parmenter* and security guard *Alessandro Berardelli* outside the *Slater and Morrill* shoe factory in South Braintree, Massachusetts.
- Executed in the electric chair at Charlestown State Prison on August 23, 1927, triggering massive general strikes, riots, and demonstrations across major world cities including London, Paris, Berlin, and Buenos Aires.
- Tried before Judge *Webster Thayer*, whose blatant anti-radical bias and derogatory private remarks about the defendants prompted prominent legal scholars, including future Supreme Court Justice *Felix Frankfurter*, to publish influential critiques in *The Atlantic Monthly*.
- Denied clemency by Massachusetts Governor *Alvan T. Fuller*, who rejected widespread appeals after the *Lowell Committee*, an advisory board chaired by *Harvard University* president *Abbott Lawrence Lowell*, endorsed the original verdicts.
- Adhered to the militant Galleanist circle of anarchists following *Luigi Galleani*, having fled to Mexico in 1917 to avoid conscription during [[world-war-i]] before returning to work as a fish peddler in Plymouth.
- Separately convicted in 1920 of an earlier attempted armed robbery in Bridgewater, Massachusetts, a disputed trial that severely prejudiced the subsequent murder proceedings in Dedham.
- Delivered an impassioned statement upon sentencing asserting that their persecution elevated their cause, famously predicting that their execution would achieve far more for human solidarity than an ordinary life spent talking on street corners.
- Vindicated symbolically on the fiftieth anniversary of his death in 1977, when Massachusetts Governor *Michael Dukakis* issued an official proclamation declaring that the defendants had been denied a fair and impartial trial.

## connections

- [[nicola-sacco]] — fellow Italian anarchist and co-defendant whose life, trial, and execution were inextricably linked to his own.
- [[united-states]] — the adopted country whose post-war Red Scare, nativist climate, and legal system framed the historic prosecution.
- [[italy]] — his homeland in Piedmont, which he left in 1908 and whose government unsuccessfully intervened during the appeals.
- [[world-war-i]] — the global conflict whose wartime tensions, draft resistance, and anti-radical suppression precipitated the arrests.
- [[14th-amendment]] — the constitutional guarantee of due process invoked by defence attorneys arguing that courtroom prejudice fatally tainted the proceedings.
- [[american-literature]] — the cultural movement profoundly shaped by the case, inspiring impassioned works by *Upton Sinclair*, *John Dos Passos*, and *Edna St. Vincent Millay*.
- [[new-york-city]] — the hub of radical immigrant organizing where protest rallies and bomb scares frequently erupted during the trial.
