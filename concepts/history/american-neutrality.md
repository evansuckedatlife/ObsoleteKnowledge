## summary

**American neutrality** was the official diplomatic stance and foreign policy doctrine maintained by the [[united-states]] to avoid direct military entanglement in overseas conflicts, most prominently during the first three years of [[world-war-i]] and throughout the turbulent 1930s lead-up to [[world-war-ii]]. Rooted in foundational eighteenth-century precedents established by [[george-washington]], the policy aimed to safeguard domestic political stability, protect commercial shipping rights, and shield the Western Hemisphere from destructive European rivalries. Under President *Woodrow Wilson*, this precarious non-intervention was repeatedly challenged by British naval blockades and German submarine warfare before finally collapsing in April 1917, permanently altering America's diplomatic standing on the world stage.

## you gotta know

- President *Woodrow Wilson* issued an official proclamation in August 1914 urging the nation to remain "impartial in thought as well as in action," warning that choosing sides would fracture an ethnically diverse American population.
- The torpedoing of the British ocean liner *RMS Lusitania* by a German U-boat in May 1915 claimed the lives of 128 American citizens, igniting intense national fury and escalating calls for direct military retaliation.
- *Woodrow Wilson* secured his re-election to the presidency in 1916 largely on the memorable campaign slogan "He Kept Us Out of War," which captured widespread public reluctance to send troops into European trenches.
- Imperial Germany's fateful resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare in February 1917, combined with the sensational intercepted *Zimmermann Telegram* proposing an alliance with Mexico, effectively dismantled diplomatic relations and precipitated the US entry into war.
- Former president [[theodore-roosevelt]] fiercely criticized Wilson's neutral stance as unprincipled and weak, actively mobilizing public support for the *Preparedness Movement* to dramatically strengthen the nation's military defenses.
- The philosophical precedent for the doctrine originated with the landmark 1793 Proclamation of Neutrality issued by [[george-washington]], who subsequently warned against the perils of permanent foreign alliances in his 1796 Farewell Address.
- Statutory isolationism returned with force during the Great Depression when Congress passed the Neutrality Acts of 1935 through 1937, legally barring arms exports and financial credits to warring nations before [[world-war-ii]].
- Economic impartiality proved profoundly unequal in practice, as powerful Wall Street banking syndicates headed by *J.P. Morgan* extended billions of dollars in commercial loans to the [[british-empire]] and [[france]] while the Royal Navy blockaded trade with [[germany]].

## connections

- [[world-war-i]] — the catastrophic European conflagration whose naval battles and ideological divides tested and eventually shattered American neutrality.
- [[united-states]] — the North American republic whose geographic distance, democratic principles, and commercial ambitions shaped its non-interventionist stance.
- [[theodore-roosevelt]] — the former president who vehemently opposed Wilson's non-belligerency, championing military readiness and national honor.
- [[germany]] — the Central Powers empire whose unrestricted commerce raiding and covert diplomacy provoked the American decision to declare war.
- [[british-empire]] — the dominant maritime belligerent whose naval blockade of Europe and massive war purchases tied American prosperity to an Allied victory.
- [[george-washington]] — the founding president whose eighteenth-century proclamations established the enduring tradition of avoiding European diplomatic entanglements.
- [[france]] — the embattled Allied power that relied heavily upon American agricultural products, industrial supplies, and private financial loans.
- [[world-war-ii]] — the subsequent global conflict preceded by rigid congressional neutrality legislation that was ultimately superseded by Lend-Lease.
