## summary

**Abortion** in the [[united-states]] has historically functioned as one of the nation's most contentious legal, cultural, and political battlegrounds regarding bodily autonomy, healthcare, and the scope of individual liberty. Long governed under traditional English common law before widespread nineteenth-century criminalization, the procedure emerged in the late twentieth century as a defining test of constitutional interpretation under the [[united-states-constitution]]. Through landmark rulings rooted in the [[14th-amendment]], the debate transformed electoral coalitions, judicial nominations, and the legal definition of substantive due process.

## you gotta know

- *Roe v. Wade* (1973) established a constitutional right to terminate pregnancy prior to fetal viability, with Justice *Harry Blackmun* authoring a 7–2 majority opinion finding that privacy guarantees in the [[14th-amendment]] Due Process Clause protect reproductive decisions under a rigid trimester framework.
- *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) overturned both *Roe* and *Casey* in an opinion by Justice *Samuel Alito*, holding that the [[united-states-constitution]] contains no right to abortion because it is neither explicitly mentioned nor deeply rooted in the nation's history and traditions.
- *Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey* (1992) reaffirmed the essential holding of *Roe* through a plurality opinion by Justices *Sandra Day O'Connor*, *Anthony Kennedy*, and *David Souter*, replacing the trimester framework with the "undue burden" standard for pre-viability regulations while abandoning strict scrutiny.
- The *Hyde Amendment*, an annual federal budget rider first enacted in 1976 and named for Illinois Republican Representative *Henry Hyde*, prohibited federal Medicaid funds from covering pregnancy termination except in narrow cases of life endangerment, rape, or incest, a restriction later upheld in *Harris v. McRae* (1980).
- In early American history, termination before "quickening"—the threshold when fetal movement is first detected, typically around the fourth month—was widely permitted under received English common law, until an aggressive mid-nineteenth-century campaign led by physician *Horatio Storer* and the *American Medical Association* spurred nationwide statutory bans.
- Clandestine mutual aid networks arose to secure healthcare amid state bans, most prominently Chicago's underground *Jane Collective* (the Abortion Counseling Service of Women's Liberation), which trained lay women to perform roughly eleven thousand safe, illegal procedures between 1969 and 1973.
- Opposition organized into a formidable political force after 1973 through groups like the *National Right to Life Committee* and the *Moral Majority*, establishing mass annual rallies like the *March for Life* and dramatically aligning social conservatives with the *Republican Party*.

## connections

- [[14th-amendment]] — provided the Due Process Clause foundation for substantive privacy rights that anchored challenges against state prohibitions.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — the foundational charter whose interpretation regarding unenumerated rights and federalism framed decades of federal court battles.
- [[united-states]] — the federal republic whose electoral politics, party platforms, and judicial appointments were reshaped by the debate.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — provided legal strategies, street demonstration precedents, and rights-based rhetoric adapted by reproductive freedom advocates.
- [[christianity]] — the faith tradition whose evangelical and Catholic bodies mobilized theological opposition, anti-abortion lobbying, and pregnancy crisis centers.
- [[privacy]] — the constitutional doctrine extended from marital contraception precedents to encompass personal reproductive and medical decisions.
- [[reproductive-rights]] — the overarching human rights framework demanding universal access to contraception, abortion, and maternal healthcare.
