## summary

**American politics** refers to the systems, actors, and ideological struggles that shape governance in the United States—a republic founded on principles of democracy, constitutional checks and balances, and popular sovereignty, yet perpetually contested by regional factions, class interests, and competing visions of national purpose. From the founding conflicts between federalists and anti-federalists through Jacksonian democracy, the Civil War, Gilded Age politics, and 20th-century partisan realignments, American political history reveals how constitutional structures interact with mass mobilization, economic change, and social movements to shape the nation's direction.

## you gotta know

- The American two-party system (Democrats and Republicans) is not mandated by the *Constitution* but evolved from competing coalitions—rivals since the early republic.
- The presidency, Congress, and courts operate under *separation-of-powers*: no single branch can dominate, creating perpetual friction and negotiation.
- Suffrage expansion—from property-owning white men to women, African Americans, and 18-year-olds—fundamentally transformed electoral politics and party coalitions.
- The *Civil War* realigned American politics: *Reconstruction* briefly empowered African American voters in the South, then *Jim Crow* disenfranchised them for a century.
- Political *parties* are mass organizations competing for votes; they coalesce around regional interests (South vs. North, rural vs. urban), class, and ideology.
- Campaign finance and media—from newspapers to television to social media—shape electoral competition and who can be heard; money in politics has grown exponentially.
- American exceptionalism—the idea that U.S. democracy is unique and superior—has shaped domestic politics and foreign policy, for better and worse. This mythology has justified both democratic idealism and imperial ambitions, making it a powerful and contested force in American political discourse.
