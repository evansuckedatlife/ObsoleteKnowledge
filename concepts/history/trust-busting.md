## summary

**Trust-busting** was the Progressive Era practice of using federal antitrust laws to dissolve large corporate monopolies and cartels that dominated American industry. Beginning in earnest under Theodore Roosevelt in the early 1900s, it represented a fundamental shift in government's role—from passive laissez-faire to active regulation of corporate power. The movement aimed to restore competitive markets, lower consumer prices, and preserve the opportunity for small business.

## you gotta know

- Emerged in response to the rise of industrial *trusts*—monopolistic combinations like Standard Oil and the railroad cartels that controlled entire industries.
- Grounded in the *Sherman Antitrust Act* (1890), which banned monopolistic practices, though early enforcement was weak until Roosevelt took office in 1901.
- Theodore Roosevelt's administration filed 44 antitrust suits; the landmark *Northern Securities* case (1904) dissolved a major railroad monopoly and emboldened trust-busters.
- Led to the break-up of *Standard Oil* (1911) into 34 competing companies, and challenged *U.S. Steel* and the *American Tobacco Company*.
- Involved epic courtroom battles between government lawyers and corporate counsel, dramatizing the struggle between public interest and private monopoly.
- Populist and progressive politicians embraced trust-busting as a solution to inequality; it unified agrarian and urban reform coalitions.
- Had mixed long-term results: some monopolies reformed, but many reconsolidated after a generation; the practice waned after World War I as wartime industry was seen as too important to disrupt.
- The legal legacy of trust-busting endured through the *Clayton Antitrust Act* (1914) and the *Federal Trade Commission Act*, which strengthened and clarified antitrust law beyond the Sherman Act.
