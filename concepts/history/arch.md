## summary

An **arch** is a curved symmetrical structure spanning an opening and supporting weight above it primarily through compressive forces. Though corbelled precursors existed in ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt, the systematic perfection of the masonry **true arch** by engineers in the [[roman-empire]] revolutionized monumental architecture and civil infrastructure. By channeling lateral and downward thrust onto vertical supports, this structural innovation enabled builders across [[rome]] and later civilizations to construct expansive aqueducts, bridges, triumphal monuments, and towering vaulted basilicas.

## you gotta know

- The central wedge-shaped stone at the apex of a masonry arch is the *keystone*, which locks the adjacent tapered blocks—termed *voussoirs*—into compression and allows the assembly to stand once temporary wooden *centering* frameworks are dismantled.
- Roman builders capitalized on semicircular arches to erect multi-tiered civil infrastructure across Europe, creating famous surviving water conduits such as the *Pont du Gard* in southern [[france]] and the *Aqueduct of Segovia* in central [[spain]].
- Expanding the two-dimensional profile of an arch into three dimensions forms the basis of vaulted architecture, as an arch rotated around its central vertical axis generates a dome, while an arch extruded horizontally produces a barrel vault.
- The intersection of two identical barrel vaults at right angles created the groin vault, an engineering leap that allowed Roman builders to open expansive interior spaces and admit clerestory lighting in monumental public bath complexes.
- Earlier Mediterranean civilizations like [[ancient-greece]] largely avoided true radial arches in monumental public temples, favoring post-and-lintel trabeation or the *corbel arch*, in which successive horizontal courses of stone cantilever inward until meeting at the peak.
- Medieval builders adopted the pointed arch from the [[middle-east]], distributing weight more directly downward to diminish outward thrust and facilitating the dramatic heights and stained-glass expanses of Gothic cathedrals across [[france]] and [[england]].
- The mathematically ideal shape of an arch supporting solely its own dead weight without bending moments is an inverted *catenary*, a physical principle first noted by *Robert Hooke* during the [[enlightenment]] and later applied in [[spain]] by *Antoni Gaudí*.

## connections

- [[roman-empire]] — standardized the semicircular voussoir arch as the foundation of imperial civil engineering and infrastructure across three continents.
- [[rome]] — showcases monumental arches throughout the capital, including the *Colosseum* arcades and the imperial triumphal *Arch of Constantine*.
- [[ancient-greece]] — retained post-and-beam construction for temples, using the arch sparingly despite possessing the technical knowledge to build it.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — replaced traditional stone voussoirs with cast iron and structural steel, allowing railway viaducts to span unprecedented distances.
- [[renaissance]] — embraced classical semicircular Roman arches over medieval pointed Gothic forms in urban basilicas, loggias, and palazzi across [[italy]].
- [[france]] — preserves monumental Roman arched aqueducts like the *Pont du Gard* and hosted the development of Gothic pointed arch systems.
- [[spain]] — contains the grand Roman *Aqueduct of Segovia* and the famous catenary arches designed for modern basilicas by *Antoni Gaudí*.
