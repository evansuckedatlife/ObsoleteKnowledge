## summary

**Consumer culture** is the social and economic orientation toward acquiring material goods as a primary source of identity, status, and happiness—a phenomenon that emerged in the early 20th century as industrial *mass-production*, cheap credit, and sophisticated *advertising* made consumption accessible and desirable to ordinary people. Before industrialization, consumption was constrained by scarcity and marked status primarily through luxury goods for the elite; consumer culture democratized desire, making shopping and spending central activities for all social classes. This shift transformed not just economics but psychology, leisure, cities, and politics—subordinating civic engagement and durability to novelty, disposability, and the perpetual chasing of new products.

## you gotta know

- Accelerated during the *Roaring Twenties* (1920s) in America: department stores, mail-order catalogs, and easy credit made shopping a mass leisure activity.
- *Advertising* transformed from product information to psychological manipulation, deploying imagery, desire, and aspiration to sell not goods but lifestyles and identities.
- The *Ladies' Home Journal* and similar magazines pioneered consumer advertising aimed at women, who became primary targets for products and advertisers' persuasion.
- Department stores (like Macy's and Marshall Field) became palaces of consumption, creating leisure shopping as spectacle and social experience.
- Credit (installment plans, credit cards) made consumption possible without savings; debt became normalized, enabling working-class participation in consumption.
- Planned obsolescence emerged: manufacturers designed products to fail or fall out of fashion, forcing continuous replacement—a departure from durability-focused production.
- Transformed urban space: shopping districts, suburban malls, and highways oriented around retail commerce reshaped geography and community life.
