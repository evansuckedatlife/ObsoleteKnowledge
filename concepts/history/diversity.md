## summary

**diversity** in American constitutional law refers to the compelling state interest in obtaining the educational benefits of a heterogeneous student body in higher education. First articulated by Justice *Lewis F. Powell Jr.* in *Regents of the University of California v. Bakke* (1978), this legal doctrine permitted universities to consider race as one factor among many in holistic admissions. For forty-five years, the pursuit of student body diversity served as the principal constitutional defense for race-conscious affirmative action under the Equal Protection Clause before being heavily curtailed in 2023.

## you gotta know

- Articulated as a viable constitutional justification by Justice *Lewis F. Powell Jr.* in the fractured 1978 landmark decision *Regents of the University of California v. Bakke*.
- Served as a doctrinal compromise by rejecting rigid quotas while allowing race to function as a flexible "plus factor" to evaluate individual applicants holistically.
- Grounded theoretically in *First Amendment* academic freedom, positing that universities have a compelling interest in creating an atmosphere of speculation, debate, and mutual understanding.
- Reaffirmed and solidified in 2003 by Justice *Sandra Day O'Connor* in *Grutter v. Bollinger*, which upheld the *University of Michigan Law School*'s race-conscious admissions policy while predicting that racial preferences would no longer be necessary in twenty-five years.
- Contrasted with the companion case *Gratz v. Bollinger* (2003), where the Supreme Court struck down Michigan's undergraduate point system because giving automatic points for race lacked individualized review.
- Upheld again in *Fisher v. University of Texas* (2016), holding that universities owe substantial deference to defining educational benefits flowing from student body diversity under strict scrutiny.
- Effectively dismantled as a legal justification for race-conscious admissions by Chief Justice *John Roberts* in the consolidated 2023 decision *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* (*SFFA*), which held that such goals lacked sufficiently coherent, measurable standards under the *14th-amendment*.
- Sparked nationwide revisions across American higher education admissions policies, prompting institutions to pivot toward socioeconomic metrics and race-neutral recruitment initiatives.
