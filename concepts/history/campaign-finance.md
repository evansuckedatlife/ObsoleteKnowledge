## summary

**Campaign finance** (often addressed in reform legislation as **campaign finance reform** or broadly termed **political financing**) denotes the mechanisms, statutes, and regulatory regimes governing how money is raised and spent in political campaigns. In the [[united-states]], this framework has developed through ongoing friction between legislative efforts to curb electoral corruption and judicial protections safeguarding political speech under the [[first-amendment]]. The field remains among the most contentious arenas in American constitutional law, fundamentally shaping candidate selection, party influence, and the balance of democratic representation.

## you gotta know

- The Tillman Act of 1907 established the initial federal ban prohibiting corporations and national banks from making monetary contributions directly to federal political candidates.
- The Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971, dramatically overhauled in 1974 following political corruption revelations, created mandatory contribution disclosures, spending limitations, and the bipartisan Federal Election Commission.
- The United States Supreme Court fundamentally reshaped electoral regulation in *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976), ruling that spending money on political campaigns constitutes protected speech under the [[first-amendment]], thereby striking down limits on independent expenditures and candidate personal spending.
- The Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002, commonly identified as the McCain-Feingold Act, outlawed unregulated "soft money" contributions to national political parties and restricted corporate and union broadcast advertisements shortly before elections.
- In the landmark ruling *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission* (2010), the Supreme Court invalidated statutory restrictions on corporate and labor union independent political expenditures, ruling that political speech rights extend to associations and corporations.
- The subsequent federal appeals decision in *SpeechNow.org v. FEC* cleared the path for independent-expenditure-only political committees, colloquially known as Super PACs, which are permitted to accept unlimited individual and organizational donations.
- "Dark money" spending has proliferated significantly through politically active 501(c)(4) social welfare organizations and trade associations that participate in election advocacy without being required to reveal the identities of their donors.
- Reform proposals continue to center on public campaign financing, small-donor matching funds, enhanced digital advertisement disclosure, and constitutional amendments aimed at overturning judicial spending precedents.

## connections

- [[united-states-constitution]] — the supreme legal text whose provisions on speech, due process, and equal protection govern campaign jurisprudence.
- [[united-states]] — the federal republic where private electoral spending has reached unmatched financial dimensions.
- [[first-amendment]] — the constitutional guarantee shielding freedom of expression, under which limits on independent expenditures have consistently been invalidated.
- [[corporate-personhood]] — the controversial legal doctrine treating corporate entities as legal persons endowed with specific constitutional speech rights.
- [[watergate]] — the executive political scandal that prompted Congress to implement comprehensive federal campaign disclosure rules and spending caps in 1974.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — the historic activist movement whose legal victories protecting donor anonymity in *NAACP v. Alabama* now influence modern donor disclosure litigation.
