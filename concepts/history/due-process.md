# Due Process

## summary

**Due Process** is the constitutional guarantee that the government cannot deprive individuals of life, liberty, or property without following established legal procedures and rules. Enshrined in both the *Fifth Amendment* (limiting federal power) and the *Fourteenth Amendment* (limiting state power), due process is a foundational principle of American law that protects individuals from arbitrary state action and requires fair legal procedures.

## you gotta know

- The Fifth Amendment's *Due Process Clause* ("nor be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law") applied originally to the federal government but was considered the law of the land before the Civil War.
- The *Fourteenth Amendment* (1868) extended due process protections against *state governments*, a crucial expansion that allowed federal courts to strike down state laws violating individual rights.
- Due process has two dimensions: *procedural* (the government must follow fair legal procedures—notice, hearing, right to counsel) and *substantive* (the government cannot enact arbitrary laws, even if procedures are followed).
- *Lochner era* jurisprudence (1905–1937) invoked substantive due process to strike down *progressive* labor and economic regulations, protecting business rights against reform; this approach was largely abandoned after the *New Deal*.
- Modern due process protections extend to diverse contexts: criminal procedure (*Miranda* rights, *exclusionary rule*), freedom of conscience, privacy rights, and protection against discrimination.
- Due process creates *strict scrutiny* and *rational basis* review—different levels of judicial review depending on whether a law affects fundamental rights or suspect classifications.
- The *incorporation doctrine* uses the Fourteenth Amendment's due process clause to apply most *Bill of Rights* protections to the states, making it one of the most expansive constitutional powers in American jurisprudence.
