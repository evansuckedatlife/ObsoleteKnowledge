## summary

**compelled speech** is an American constitutional doctrine under the First Amendment establishing that the government cannot force individuals or private entities to articulate, support, or adhere to specific ideological viewpoints. Originating in Landmark Supreme Court jurisprudence regarding mandatory patriotic exercises in public schools, the doctrine holds that freedom of speech inherently encompasses both the right to speak and the right to refrain from speaking at all. It serves as a foundational limit against state-mandated orthodoxy in civil liberties, political discourse, and commercial regulation.

## you gotta know

- Established in the landmark 1943 case *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, which held that public school students could not be forced to salute the American flag or recite the Pledge of Allegiance.
- Justice *Robert H. Jackson* authored the celebrated majority opinion in *Barnette*, declaring: "If there is any fixed star in our constitutional constellation, it is that no official, high or petty, shall prescribe what shall be orthodox in politics, nationalism, religion, or other matters of opinion."
- *Barnette* directly overturned *Minersville School District v. Gobitis* (1940), an earlier ruling that had upheld mandatory flag salutes for *Jehovah's Witnesses*.
- Extended to state license plates in *Wooley v. Maynard* (1977), where the Court ruled that New Hampshire could not prosecute a driver for covering the state motto "Live Free or Die."
- Applied to private parade organizers in *Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian and Bisexual Group of Boston* (1995), affirming that private parade organizers cannot be compelled to include messages they oppose.
- Distinguishes between ideological coercion and permissible factual disclosures, such as mandatory health warnings or consumer protection disclosures on commercial products.
- Modern jurisprudence, including *303 Creative LLC v. Elenis* (2023) and *National Institute of Family and Life Advocates v. Becerra* (2018), frequently invokes the doctrine in clashes between anti-discrimination statutes and expressive liberties.
