## summary

The **spice trade** was the vast maritime commerce in high-value spices—pepper, cloves, nutmeg, cinnamon, and mace—from the Indian Ocean and Southeast Asian islands to European markets from the 15th century onward. Driven by European demand for flavoring and food preservation, the trade became the primary economic engine of early European exploration and colonialism. Control of spice routes determined wealth and imperial power, shaping the geopolitics of the Age of Exploration.

## you gotta know

- Medieval Europe relied on long-distance spice routes through Islamic intermediaries; spices were luxury goods worth fortunes, used for preserving meat and signaling wealth.
- Overland routes through the Middle East (the *Silk Road*) were slow and expensive; European merchants sought direct sea routes to the source in the East Indies.
- Portuguese explorer *Vasco da Gama* reached India by sea in 1498, establishing the first direct European maritime link to spice-producing regions.
- Portugal established a trading empire in the Indian Ocean, controlling key ports and eliminating Arab and Italian intermediaries; Portuguese merchants reaped enormous profits.
- The Dutch East India Company (*VOC*, founded 1602) rivaled and eventually surpassed Portuguese dominance; Dutch control of the *Moluccas* (Spice Islands) gave them a near-monopoly on cloves and nutmeg.
- The spice trade sparked ruthless competition, warfare, and colonization; European powers fought for monopolies on specific crops in specific islands.
- Globalization's precursor: the spice trade connected continents, enriched merchants, financed exploration, and laid the foundation for European colonial empires in Asia and the Pacific.
