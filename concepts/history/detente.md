## summary

**Détente** (from French, meaning "relaxation of tension") was the policy of reducing Cold War hostilities between the United States and Soviet Union in the 1970s. Pursued vigorously by President *Richard Nixon* and his advisor *Henry Kissinger*, it sought to manage superpower competition through diplomacy and trade rather than military confrontation. Détente represented a significant shift from the ideological rigidity of the earlier Cold War.

## you gotta know

- Emerged in the late 1960s and early 1970s as both superpowers recognized the costs of nuclear arms racing and proxy conflicts.
- Grounded in *realpolitik*—the concept that national interests should guide foreign policy rather than ideological crusades.
- Produced major diplomatic breakthroughs: the *Strategic Arms Limitation Talks* (SALT I, 1972) restricted nuclear weapons; the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty limited defensive systems.
- Nixon's opening to *China* (1971–1972) exemplified détente strategy: playing rivals against each other to reduce total Soviet advantage.
- Trade and cultural exchange increased between the superpowers, including grain sales to the USSR and scientific cooperation.
- Sparked fierce criticism from American conservatives who saw it as appeasement of communism and abandonment of Cold War vigilance.
- Declined rapidly after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979), which Americans viewed as betrayal of détente's spirit of mutual restraint.
- The Helsinki Accords (1975) symbolized détente's high point, though they proved controversial by legitimizing Soviet control over Eastern Europe while nominal committing the USSR to human rights.
- Kissinger's shuttle diplomacy in the Middle East tried to manage superpower competition through personal negotiation rather than confrontational posturing.
- By the early 1980s, the Cold War's ideological intensity resurged under *Ronald Reagan*, who rejected détente as naive and weak.
