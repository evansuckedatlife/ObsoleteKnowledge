## summary

**Sacagawea** (also rendered **Sakakawea** or **Sacajawea**) was a Lemhi Shoshone woman who served as an indispensable interpreter, guide, and symbol of peaceful intent for the *Corps of Discovery* led by *Meriwether Lewis* and *William Clark* between 1804 and 1806. Captured as an adolescent by the Hidatsa and subsequently sold into marriage with French-Canadian fur trapper *Toussaint Charbonneau*, she accompanied the exploratory mission across the northern plains and Rocky Mountains to the Pacific Ocean following the [[louisiana-purchase]] authorized by *President* [[thomas-jefferson]]. Her knowledge of indigenous geography, edible plants, and regional languages proved indispensable to the survival of the expedition, establishing her as an enduring icon of early frontier history and exploration within the [[united-states]].

## you gotta know

- Accompanied the *Corps of Discovery* from Fort Mandan in modern North Dakota to the Pacific Coast and back, serving as the expedition's sole female member alongside her newborn infant son, *Jean Baptiste Charbonneau*, whose presence signaled to indigenous nations that the military party came in peace rather than on a war raid.
- Facilitated the crucial acquisition of pack horses across the Continental Divide when she discovered that the Shoshone leader *Cameahwait*, from whom the expedition desperately needed mounts to traverse the Bitterroot Mountains, was her own brother whom she had not seen since her childhood capture.
- Rescued irreplaceable expedition journals, scientific records, navigational instruments, and medicines when a sudden squall nearly capsized her husband *Toussaint Charbonneau's* pirogue on the Missouri River in May 1805, prompting *William Clark* to name the Sacagawea River in her honor.
- Provided essential botanical sustenance and medical relief to expedition members by foraging wild licorice, prairie turnips (*Psoralea esculenta*), Jerusalem artichokes, currants, and wild fennel, mitigating chronic food shortages and preventing nutritional deficiencies during harsh northern travel.
- Participated alongside the enslaved African American *York* in the historic November 1805 democratic vote determining where the expedition would construct Fort Clatsop near the mouth of the Columbia River, marking an exceptionally rare instance of a Native American woman casting a ballot in early frontier records.
- Fostered an enduring relationship with *William Clark*, who nicknamed her infant son "Pompey" or "Pomp," later named the prominent geological formation Pompeys Pillar in Montana after the boy, and assumed legal responsibility for *Jean Baptiste's* classical education in St. Louis.
- Officially recorded by clerk *John C. Luttig* as having died of "putrid fever" in December 1812 at Fort Manuel Lisa along the upper Missouri River at approximately age twenty-four, though oral traditions among the Eastern Shoshone maintained that she lived under the name *Porivo* until 1884.

## connections

- [[thomas-jefferson]] — the third president whose commissioning of the transcontinental expedition and completion of the [[louisiana-purchase]] initiated her journey.
- [[united-states]] — the expanding republic whose western cartography and territorial reconnaissance were substantially advanced by her guidance.
- [[colonialism]] — the broader geopolitical framework of Euro-American territorial expansion and displacement that followed the trails she helped map.
- [[age-of-exploration]] — the historical era of state-directed geographic reconnaissance and scientific survey reflected in her transcontinental trek.
- [[louisiana-purchase]] — the 1803 land acquisition from [[france]] that prompted the federal mission across the northern plains to the Pacific.
- [[william-clark]] — the co-leader who praised her resourcefulness, named landmarks after her family, and later provided schooling for her son.
