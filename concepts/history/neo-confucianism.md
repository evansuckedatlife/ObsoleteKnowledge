## summary

**Neo-Confucianism** is a major philosophical, ethical, and metaphysical movement that originated during the Tang and Song dynasties in imperial [[china]] as a revitalization of classical Confucian thought. Formulated partly in response to the pervasive cultural and spiritual influence of [[buddhism]] and Daoism, the school synthesized traditional ethical humanism with a cosmological framework centered on universal principle and material energy. Under the definitive 12th-century synthesis of *Zhu Xi*, it was adopted as state orthodoxy, providing the mandatory curriculum for the imperial civil service examinations for over seven centuries and fundamentally structuring moral, social, and political life across East Asia, including [[japan]], Korea, and Vietnam.

## you gotta know

- *Zhu Xi* codified the tradition into state orthodoxy during the Southern Song dynasty by compiling and annotating the *Four Books*—the *Analects*, *Mencius*, *Great Learning*, and *Doctrine of the Mean*—which superseded the older *Five Classics* as the basis of the imperial civil service examinations until their abolition in 1905.
- Core metaphysics centers on the dualism of *li* (underlying cosmic principle or order) and *qi* (vital force or psycho-physical matter), holding that all entities share the same inherently pure principle but differ in virtue and clarity according to the purity or turbidity of their physical endowment.
- The movement emerged as an intellectual defense of social order against [[buddhism]] and Daoism, adopting Buddhist metaphysical depth and quiet contemplation while fiercely rejecting monastic celibacy, ascetic withdrawal, and the abandonment of filial piety and civic responsibility.
- The dominant Cheng-Zhu school advocated the epistemological doctrine of *gewu* (the "investigation of things"), asserting that moral self-cultivation requires actively studying external phenomena, natural order, and classical literature to apprehend the latent universal principle residing in the world.
- During the Ming dynasty, the statesman and philosopher *Wang Yangming* founded the rival School of Mind (*Xinxue*), arguing against external investigation by asserting that the mind is principle, knowledge and action are fundamentally unified (*zhixing heyi*), and every person possesses innate moral awareness (*liangzhi*).
- Tang dynasty precursors *Han Yu* and *Li Ao* spearheaded the intellectual revival centuries earlier by writing polemical essays against Buddhist institutions, lamenting the decline of ancient sage traditions, and articulating the concept of the transmission of the Way (*daotong*).
- Northern Song cosmologists *Zhou Dunyi*, *Zhang Zai*, and the brothers *Cheng Hao* and *Cheng Yi* formulated foundational concepts, such as Zhou's diagram of the Supreme Ultimate (*Taijitu Shuo*) and Zhang's egalitarian *Western Inscription*, which envisioned humanity and the cosmos as a single moral organism.

## connections

- [[china]] — the imperial civilization where the tradition evolved across multiple dynasties to serve as the dominant administrative and ethical orthodoxy.
- [[buddhism]] — the rival Indian-derived tradition whose metaphysical depth stimulated Confucian thinkers while drawing severe criticism for its world-denying monasticism.
- [[japan]] — the neighboring archipelago where Tokugawa shoguns and samurai scholars adapted Neo-Confucian ethics to anchor social order and feudal loyalty.
- [[renaissance]] — a parallel cultural movement in Europe characterized by an intellectual return to classical antiquity to revitalize moral philosophy, education, and civil governance.
- [[silk-road]] — the transcontinental trade network that brought diverse religious currents across Eurasian frontiers, prompting Chinese scholars to reaffirm their indigenous heritage.
- [[enlightenment]] — the European intellectual era whose leading thinkers admired translated Jesuit accounts of Chinese governance grounded in rational, secular Confucian morality.
