## summary

The **Compromise of 1850** was a series of legislative measures that temporarily defused the crisis over slavery's expansion into western territories acquired from the *Mexican-American War*. Crafted by *Henry Clay* and negotiated through bitter sectional debate, it admitted *California* as a free state, organized new territories without slavery restrictions, and included a draconian *Fugitive Slave Act* compelling northern compliance with slavery enforcement. Though hailed as a triumph of compromise, it intensified sectional hostility and postponed rather than resolved the conflict that would erupt in civil war eleven years later.

## you gotta know

- California's admission as a free state disrupted the sectional balance: the Senate had maintained equal numbers of free and slave states since the *Missouri Compromise* (1820); California's entry threatened Southern dominance.
- *Henry Clay*, the "Great Compromiser," orchestrated the package: California free; New Mexico and Utah organized as territories with popular sovereignty (voters would decide slavery); the slave trade (not slavery) abolished in *Washington, D.C.*; and a harsh *Fugitive Slave Act* (1850) passed.
- The *Fugitive Slave Act* required northern citizens to assist in capturing enslaved people; it overrode state laws and denied fugitives trial by jury; its brutality shocked northerners and radicalized abolitionists who saw it as proof of slavery's tyranny.
- *Daniel Webster*, a northern senator, surprised many by supporting the Compromise, declaring "I wish to make myself heard, not as a New England man, but as an American"; his *"Seventh of March" speech* endorsed the package but damaged his reputation among abolitionists.
- Southern secessionists like *John C. Calhoun* rejected the Compromise as insufficient; Calhoun opposed California's admission and any curb on slavery expansion, foreshadowing Southern grievances that would justify secession in 1861.
- The Compromise briefly calmed sectional tensions but created new flashpoints: enforcement of the *Fugitive Slave Act* sparked resistance in northern states; the Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854) undid the territorial provisions, reigniting conflict.
