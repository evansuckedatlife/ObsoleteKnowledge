## summary

The **Sedition Act of 1918** was an act of the *United States Congress* enacted during [[world-war-i]] as an expansive amendment to the *Espionage Act of 1917*. Promoted by the administration of President *Woodrow Wilson*, the statute criminalized a sweeping range of expression, prohibiting any "disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language" regarding the form of government of the [[united-states]], the [[united-states-constitution]], the military flag, or service uniforms. The legislation triggered aggressive federal suppression of pacifists, socialist organizers, and labor activists, prompting landmark Supreme Court cases that reshaped constitutional interpretations of [[free-speech]] and [[civil-liberties]].

## you gotta know

- Passed in May 1918 to dramatically expand Title I of the *Espionage Act of 1917*, granting federal prosecutors unprecedented statutory power to penalize antiwar sentiment, draft evasion advocacy, and industrial strikes during [[world-war-i]].
- Provided the legal crucible for defining American [[free-speech]] jurisprudence, leading Justice *Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.* to articulate the famous [[clear-and-present-danger]] test in the landmark ruling [[schenck-v-united-states]].
- Prompted the arrest and high-profile prosecution of Socialist Party leader *Eugene V. Debs*, who was sentenced to ten years of imprisonment after delivering an anti-militarist speech in Canton, Ohio, that criticized the capitalist motives behind the conflict.
- Catalyzed the landmark Supreme Court decision in *Abrams v. United States*, where the majority upheld convictions against Russian Jewish immigrant anarchists, drawing an influential dissenting opinion from Justice *Holmes* championing the uninhibited "free trade in ideas."
- Outlawed verbal and written attacks that brought the [[united-states-constitution]], the American military flag, or armed forces uniforms into contempt, scorn, or disrepute, punishable by fines reaching ten thousand dollars and prison terms up to twenty years.
- Empowered Postmaster General *Albert S. Burleson* to exercise sweeping administrative censorship by denying access to the postal mails for radical publications, socialist newspapers, and antiwar journals such as *The Masses*.
- Fueled a pervasive climate of wartime conformity and vigilantism led by groups like the *American Protective League*, targeting the *Industrial Workers of the World* and laying institutional groundwork for the subsequent *Palmer Raids*.
- Faced mounting domestic criticism following the 1918 armistice, leading Congress to officially repeal the amendment in March 1921 while preserving the underlying espionage framework.

- [[clear-and-present-danger]] — foundational judicial doctrine formulated during challenges to wartime speech restrictions to demarcate the boundaries of protected expression.
