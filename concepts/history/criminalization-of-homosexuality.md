## summary

The **criminalization of homosexuality**, historically codified primarily through **sodomy laws**, **anti-sodomy laws**, and statutes against the **criminalization of same-sex sexual activity**, denotes the institutional penalization of non-heterosexual conduct by state and religious authorities. Arising from ecclesiastical traditions and secularized across Europe, these punitive regimes were globally exported by European empires and vigorously enforced through imprisonment, forced labor, corporal punishment, and execution. Over the course of the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, sustained civil rights activism dismantled these statutes across numerous jurisdictions, establishing fundamental rights to privacy and equal protection under the law.

## you gotta know

- Rooted in English statutory law via the Buggery Act 1533 enacted under King *Henry VIII*, which shifted jurisdiction over unnatural sexual offenses from ecclesiastical courts to the royal state and made conviction punishable by hanging.
- Exported globally by the [[british-empire]] through Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, drafted by *Thomas Babington Macaulay* in 1860, which served as the template for anti-sodomy statutes across dozens of colonies throughout Asia, Africa, and the Caribbean.
- Enforced in Victorian Britain under Section 11 of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885 (the *Labouchere Amendment*), which banned "gross indecency" between men and was notoriously used in 1895 to convict and sentence playwright *Oscar Wilde* to two years of hard labor at Reading Gaol.
- Codified in Imperial Germany under Paragraph 175 (*Paragraph einhundertfünfundsiebzig*) of the criminal code, which was dramatically expanded by the Nazi regime in 1935, resulting in the deportation of tens of thousands of gay men to concentration camps marked by pink triangles (*Rosa Winkel*).
- Used against mathematician and codebreaker *Alan Turing* in 1952, whose conviction for gross indecency forced him to submit to chemical castration via estrogen injections to avoid imprisonment, leading to his suicide in 1954.
- Challenged in the United States Supreme Court case *Bowers v. Hardwick* (1986), where a 5–4 conservative majority led by Justice *Byron White* upheld Georgia's sodomy statute, explicitly rejecting the assertion of a constitutional right to engage in homosexual sodomy.
- Decisively overturned in the landmark Supreme Court decision *Lawrence v. Texas* (2003), where Justice *Anthony Kennedy*'s 6–3 majority opinion struck down Texas's "Homosexual Conduct" law, ruling that consensual adult intimacy is protected under the Due Process Clause of the *Fourteenth Amendment*.
- Replicated in post-revolutionary Latin America, notably under the *Cuban Revolution*, which institutionalized state persecution by detaining thousands of gay men and dissidents in military-administered agricultural labor camps during the mid-1960s.

## connections

- [[united-states-constitution]] — served as the foundational legal arena where guarantees of substantive due process and personal liberty challenged state surveillance of private intimacy.
- [[14th-amendment]] — provided the crucial Due Process and Equal Protection clauses utilized in *Lawrence v. Texas* to invalidate state anti-sodomy prohibitions.
- [[british-empire]] — weaponized colonial penal codes like Section 377 to impose Victorian sexual morality upon colonized societies across the globe.
- [[united-states]] — maintained state-level criminal codes used for decades to justify police entrapment, employment disqualification, and tavern raids like Stonewall.
- [[christianity]] — provided early theological rationales and canon law frameworks that European monarchs translated into secular capital criminal statutes.
- [[cuban-revolution]] — demonstrated how mid-twentieth-century authoritarian socialist regimes also criminalized nonconforming sexual orientation under the guise of revolutionary hygiene.
