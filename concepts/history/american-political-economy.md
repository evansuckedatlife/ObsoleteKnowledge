## summary

**American political economy** describes the dynamic institutional relationship between federal governance, legal frameworks, and market capitalism in the [[united-states]]. Emerging from early constitutional debates between agrarian decentralization and industrial central banking, the system underwent its most defining transformation during the [[great-depression]], when *Franklin D. Roosevelt*'s New Deal constructed the modern regulatory and welfare state. Grasping this institutional evolution is essential for understanding how the American nation has historically navigated market failures, labor struggles, corporate consolidation, and federal macroeconomic management.

## you gotta know

- The *New Deal* enacted under [[franklin-d-roosevelt]] fundamentally restructured American capitalism in response to the [[great-depression]], establishing federal regulatory oversight of capital markets and institutionalizing the modern social safety net through the *Social Security Act* of 1935.
- Federal relief agencies such as the [[works-progress-administration]] and the *Civilian Conservation Corps* directly employed millions on public infrastructure projects, marking a revolutionary break from prevailing laissez-faire doctrines of non-intervention.
- The constitutional foundation of federal economic regulation rests primarily on the *Commerce Clause* and the *Taxing and Spending Clause* of the [[united-states-constitution]], which broadened substantially after the Supreme Court's pivotal 1937 jurisprudential shift.
- Foundational conflicts over national development began in the 1790s between *Alexander Hamilton*'s program of national debt assumption, protective tariffs, and central banking under [[george-washington]], and [[thomas-jefferson]]'s agrarian philosophy of decentralized states' rights.
- Unprecedented corporate consolidation following the [[american-civil-war]] and [[reconstruction-era]] fostered the Gilded Age regulatory state, producing landmark federal antitrust measures including the *Interstate Commerce Act* of 1887 and the *Sherman Antitrust Act* of 1890.
- Wartime industrial mobilization during [[world-war-ii]] cemented a permanent partnership among the federal government, private corporations, and organized labor, which underpinned sustained economic expansion throughout the [[cold-war]].
- The postwar Keynesian economic consensus was institutionalized by the *Employment Act of 1946*, which committed the federal government to maintaining maximum employment, production, and purchasing power through the [[united-states-congress]].
- The onset of stagflation in the 1970s dismantled the postwar Keynesian synthesis, prompting a decisive ideological turn in the 1980s toward financial deregulation, monetarism, and supply-side tax cuts.

## connections

- [[franklin-d-roosevelt]] — thirty-second president whose New Deal administration permanently enlarged executive power and regulatory control over the national economy.
- [[great-depression]] — catastrophic economic crisis whose devastating unemployment and financial collapse prompted the abandonment of strict laissez-faire policies.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — foundational legal document whose Commerce Clause provides the principal constitutional basis for federal economic regulation and statutory oversight.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — era of mechanized manufacturing and continental railroad building that transformed the nation from an agrarian economy into an industrial juggernaut.
- [[works-progress-administration]] — landmark New Deal agency that exemplified direct federal employment and Keynesian public works expenditure during peacetime depression.
- [[thomas-jefferson]] — early republican leader whose agrarian economic vision stood in lasting opposition to Hamiltonian financial centralization and manufacturing subsidies.
- [[cold-war]] — prolonged international competition that entrenched an enduring military-industrial complex and spurred sustained federal investment in scientific research and aerospace.
- [[united-states]] — federal republic whose legal traditions, dual sovereignty, and commercial markets fostered a distinctively decentralized model of capitalism.
