## summary

**American mass media** encompasses the centralized systems of print journalism, mass-circulation periodicals, radio, and television networks that developed across the [[united-states]] from the nineteenth century onward. Catalyzed by the [[industrial-revolution]] and funded primarily by commercial advertising, these communication channels replaced localized news with a synchronized national public sphere. By delivering shared information, entertainment, and consumer imagery to millions simultaneously, this infrastructure became the defining engine of American [[popular-culture]] and democratic discourse.

## you gotta know

- The golden age of network broadcasting saw *NBC*, *CBS*, and *ABC* dominate the airwaves, creating shared cultural rituals for tens of millions of simultaneous viewers.
- Mass-market illustrated magazines such as *The Saturday Evening Post*, *Life*, and *Time* pioneered photojournalism and national advertising, reaching broader and more diverse demographics than any competing print publications.
- President *Franklin D. Roosevelt* revolutionized political communication during the Great Depression through his radio *Fireside Chats*, bypassing hostile press barons to speak directly to citizens in their homes.
- Television news coverage transformed public opinion during the [[civil-rights-movement]] and the [[cold-war]], as televised footage of police brutality in the South galvanized nationwide support for civil rights reform.
- The nineteenth-century penny press democratized journalism by selling newspapers like the *New York Sun* for one cent, shifting editorial focus from partisan mercantile essays toward urban human-interest reporting and crime.
- Sensationalist yellow journalism in the 1890s, driven by a fierce circulation war between *Joseph Pulitzer* and *William Randolph Hearst*, illustrated the political influence of press barons prior to the Spanish-American War.
- The *Communications Act of 1934* created the *Federal Communications Commission*, codifying the regulatory doctrine that the broadcast airwaves are public property requiring commercial stations to serve the public interest.
- Federal postal subsidies and rural free delivery in the late nineteenth century drastically lowered distribution costs, allowing national periodicals to achieve unprecedented rural and small-town circulation.

## connections

- [[popular-culture]] — established the national distribution channels and advertising imagery that standardized consumer trends and mass leisure.
- [[magazine-publishing]] — developed the general-interest illustrated formats that connected geographically separated readers into a unified market.
- [[united-states]] — provided the constitutional protections of a free press and private corporate framework that shaped commercial broadcasting.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — supplied the rotary printing presses, paper manufacturing, and telegraph lines essential for rapid nationwide distribution.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — utilized evening television news broadcasts to confront a nationwide audience with the realities of racial oppression.
- [[cold-war]] — mobilized radio and television programming as ideological tools of democratic messaging at home and abroad against the [[soviet-union]].
- [[silent-film]] — established the visual culture and star system that print magazines and newspapers popularized across the country.
- [[new-york-city]] — functioned as the financial and creative epicenter for leading newspaper syndicates, major magazines, and network broadcast headquarters.
