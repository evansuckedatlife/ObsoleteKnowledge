## summary

**Betts v. Brady** was a landmark *United States Supreme Court* ruling decided in 1942 that established that indigent defendants in state criminal prosecutions did not possess an automatic constitutional right to appointed legal counsel under the Due Process Clause of the [[14th-amendment]]. The court determined that counsel was constitutionally mandated only in capital crimes or where special circumstances demonstrated that the absence of a lawyer resulted in fundamental unfairness. This controversial precedent governed state criminal jurisprudence across the [[united-states]] for over two decades until it was formally repudiated and overturned by [[gideon-v-wainwright]] in 1963.

## you gotta know

- Famously overturned twenty-one years later by the unanimous 1963 landmark ruling in [[gideon-v-wainwright]], which established that the Sixth Amendment right to assistance of counsel is fundamental and applies to all state felony trials.
- Refused to incorporate the Sixth Amendment right to counsel against state governments through the [[14th-amendment]], holding that states were not constitutionally compelled to finance legal defense for all impoverished accused citizens.
- Began when *Smith Betts*, an unemployed farm worker and indicted robbery suspect in *Maryland*, requested that the trial court appoint counsel on his behalf because he was too poor to hire an attorney, a request flatly denied by the judge.
- Authored by Justice *Owen Roberts* as a 6–3 decision, which reasoned that providing appointed counsel was not a universal requirement of fundamental fairness under the [[united-states-constitution]].
- Established the unpredictable "special circumstances" doctrine, which placed the burden on unrepresented defendants to prove that extreme factors like illiteracy, youth, mental incapacity, or trial complexity rendered their conviction fundamentally unfair.
- Drew an influential and stinging dissent from Justice *Hugo Black*, joined by Justices *William O. Douglas* and *Frank Murphy*, arguing that denying an attorney to a person solely because of poverty was an intolerable injustice.
- Distinguished and limited the reach of the 1932 *Scottsboro Boys* decision in [[powell-v-alabama]], confining mandatory counsel to capital prosecutions where defendants faced execution.
- Generated two decades of arbitrary judicial administration and systemic unfairness, prompting extensive criticism from legal scholars and civil liberties advocates prior to the [[civil-rights-movement]].

## connections

- [[14th-amendment]] — constitutional vehicle through which petitioners argued that due process required states to provide defense counsel.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — supreme organic charter establishing the balance between federal constitutional rights and state criminal procedure.
- [[united-states]] — nation whose highest judicial body heard the case and managed federalist principles during wartime.
- [[world-war-ii]] — historical era of global conflict during which the court prioritized judicial restraint and state autonomy.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — era of expanding constitutional protections and equal justice initiatives that helped hasten the demise of the ruling.
- [[gideon-v-wainwright]] — landmark 1963 decision that completely overruled the precedent and guaranteed counsel for all indigent state felony defendants.
- [[powell-v-alabama]] — earlier 1932 ruling granting counsel in capital cases that this decision refused to extend to non-capital offenses.
