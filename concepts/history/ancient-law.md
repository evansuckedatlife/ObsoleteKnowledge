## summary

**Ancient law** encompasses the codified statutory frameworks, customary edicts, and judicial traditions developed by the earliest literate civilizations of the [[middle-east]], the Mediterranean basin, and East Asia. Beginning with third-millennium BCE Mesopotamian cuneiform tablets, these legal systems transitioned justice from arbitrary royal decrees and private blood feuds into public, formalized rules governing liability, commercial property, family relations, and civic order. Their foundational concepts—ranging from the presumption of innocence and retaliatory justice to civic statutes in [[ancient-greece]] and the civil jurisprudence of [[rome]]—provided the enduring structural bedrock for modern statutory law and constitutional governance across the world.

## you gotta know

- Promulgated by the Babylonian monarch around 1750 BCE, the *[[code-of-hammurabi]]* carved 282 case-law edicts into a diorite stele, establishing strict liability, social-class-based penalties, and the enduring doctrine of *lex talionis* across the ancient [[middle-east]].
- The *Twelve Tables* of [[rome]], posted publicly in the Roman Forum around 450 BCE following the political Struggle of the Orders, established essential procedural rights, private property protections, and formal equality before the magistrate for plebeian citizens.
- Athenian jurisprudence transformed dramatically from the legendary, excessively harsh capital statutes of *Draco* to the sweeping constitutional reforms of *Solon*, who abolished debt bondage and created popular citizen juries in [[ancient-greece]].
- The legal tradition of [[judaism]], codified within the Mosaic Torah, merged religious piety with statutory liability, providing moral covenants like the *Decalogue* alongside extensive civil regulations on contracts, dietary observance, debt forgiveness, and social welfare.
- Commissioned by Emperor *Justinian I* in the sixth century CE, the *Corpus Juris Civilis* consolidated centuries of classical jurisprudence from the [[roman-empire]] into a systematic civil code that profoundly shaped modern continental European law.
- The *Code of Ur-Nammu*, composed in the Sumerian language around 2100 BCE, represents the earliest known surviving statutory text, notable for prescribing monetary compensation for physical injuries rather than corporal retribution.
- Ancient [[china]] developed rival jurisprudential traditions during the Warring States period, where the philosophy of *Legalism* advocated strict, universally applied statutory punishments and institutional rewards to centralize bureaucratic state authority.

## connections

- [[code-of-hammurabi]] — the defining Mesopotamian legal stele whose punitive formulas influenced Near Eastern statutory traditions.
- [[rome]] — the civilization whose *Twelve Tables* and praetorian edicts laid the groundwork for Western civil and procedural jurisprudence.
- [[roman-empire]] — the imperial power whose sixth-century *Corpus Juris Civilis* organized Classical jurisprudence into the basis of European civil law.
- [[ancient-greece]] — the cradle of trial by jury and constitutional codifications instituted under the reforms of *Draco* and *Solon*.
- [[middle-east]] — the geographic and cultural heartland where the earliest cuneiform statutory traditions arose in Sumer and [[babylon]].
- [[judaism]] — the religious and cultural tradition that integrated divine covenant with comprehensive statutory, civic, and moral laws.
- [[china]] — the imperial society where ancient debates between *Confucian* moral virtue and *Legalist* statutory codification governed statecraft.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — the modern constitutional charter that drew upon ancient Roman and Greek ideals of codified statutory limits on power.
