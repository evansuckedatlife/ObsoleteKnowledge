## summary

**Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard** (commonly abbreviated **SFFA v. Harvard**) was a landmark 2023 decision by the Supreme Court of the [[united-states]] declaring race-conscious admissions policies in higher education unconstitutional under the Equal Protection Clause of the [[14th-amendment]]. Decided alongside a companion case against the University of North Carolina, the ruling effectively eliminated affirmative action in American collegiate admissions by overturning precedents established in *Regents of the University of California v. Bakke* and *Grutter v. Bollinger*. Authored by Chief Justice *John Roberts*, the majority opinion held that university admissions programs must evaluate applicants strictly on an individual basis rather than on race, fundamentally transforming higher education policy and civil rights jurisprudence.

## you gotta know

- Struck down affirmative action in university admissions across the [[united-states]], holding that race-conscious selection procedures at Harvard College and the University of North Carolina violated the Equal Protection Clause of the [[14th-amendment]] and Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964.
- Effectively overruled the 2003 precedent *Grutter v. Bollinger*, which had previously permitted universities to treat an applicant's race as a "plus factor" in holistic evaluations to achieve the educational benefits of a diverse student body.
- Delivered by Chief Justice *John Roberts* on behalf of a 6–3 conservative majority, concluding that university programs lacked sufficiently measurable diversity goals and improperly used race as a negative factor and racial stereotype.
- Initiated by conservative legal activist *Edward Blum* through his legal advocacy organization *Students for Fair Admissions*, which alleged that Harvard systematically depressed admissions for Asian American applicants by using subjective personal ratings to cap their enrollment numbers.
- Produced sharp ideological exchanges between conservative Justice *Clarence Thomas*, who wrote a concurrence advocating for a colorblind interpretation of the [[united-states-constitution]], and liberal Justices *Sonia Sotomayor* and *Ketanji Brown Jackson*, who authored dissents defending race-conscious remedies as vital to fulfilling the goals of the [[civil-rights-movement]].
- Centered on conflicting historical interpretations of the [[reconstruction-era]], with opposing opinions debating whether the original public meaning and early federal race-conscious measures intended to allow remediation for historical oppression.
- Provided an explicit exception allowing universities to consider an applicant's discussion of how race has impacted their character, perseverance, or unique lived experience, provided the student is evaluated on individual merits rather than as a racial category.
- Exempted United States military service academies in Footnote 4 of the majority opinion, leaving open whether distinctive national security interests might justify race-conscious selection outside civilian academia.

## connections

- [[14th-amendment]] — provides the Equal Protection Clause that formed the constitutional foundation of the Court's ruling against race-conscious admissions.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — the supreme legal charter interpreted by the justices to assess the limits of state-sponsored racial classifications.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — the historic campaign for racial equality whose statutory achievements, notably Title VI of the Civil Rights Act, were applied to private universities receiving federal funds.
- [[reconstruction-era]] — the post-Civil War period in which the Fourteenth Amendment was drafted and ratified, generating intense judicial debate over its historical scope.
- [[united-states]] — the federal republic whose nationwide university systems were compelled to alter admissions processes in the wake of the decision.
- [[american-civil-war]] — the conflict that prompted the destruction of slavery and spurred the adoption of the constitutional guarantees at issue.
