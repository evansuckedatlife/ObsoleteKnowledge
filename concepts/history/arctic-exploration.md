## summary

**Arctic exploration** refers to the centuries-long European and later international quest to navigate, map, and establish dominance over the Arctic regions, particularly seeking the elusive **Northwest Passage**—a sea route through northern Canada connecting the Atlantic and Pacific. Explorers like *Henry Hudson*, searching for the passage from the sixteenth century onward, faced deadly ice, scurvy, mutiny, and indigenous resistance, often perishing in the attempt. Later polar expeditions combined geographic ambition with nationalist competition and scientific inquiry, culminating in the attainment of both the Arctic and Antarctic poles by the early twentieth century.

## you gotta know

- The *Northwest Passage* became the obsession of centuries of explorers seeking a shortcut to Asian trade, competing European empires to establish territorial claims.
- *Henry Hudson*, English explorer and navigator, made multiple Arctic voyages (1607–1611) seeking the passage; his final voyage ended in mutiny and death when trapped by ice.
- Arctic exploration linked to British colonial ambitions—successful mapping claims and overwintering established presumed sovereignty over vast Arctic territories and indigenous lands.
- *Roald Amundsen* (Norwegian) successfully navigated the Northwest Passage (1903–1906), proving it existed and demonstrating advanced techniques in polar navigation and survival.
- The search for the North Pole drove expeditions that became nationalistic competitions in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, culminating in Robert Peary's disputed 1909 claim.
- Arctic exploration revealed the region's hostile climate, indigenous peoples' deep knowledge and adaptation, and later environmental vulnerabilities that made it strategically important during the *Cold War*.
