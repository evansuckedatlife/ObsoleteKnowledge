## summary

**Black Feminism** is an intellectual tradition and political movement originating in the [[united-states]] that analyzes how race, gender, class, and sexuality operate simultaneously rather than as separate systems of oppression. Prefigured in the nineteenth century by activists such as *Sojourner Truth* and *Anna Julia Cooper*, it coalesced during the 1960s and 1970s to address the marginalization of Black women within both mainstream white feminism and the male-dominated [[civil-rights-movement]]. By introducing foundational frameworks such as *intersectionality* and *identity politics*, **Black Feminist Thought** revolutionized modern social theory, legal studies, and global human rights activism.

## you gotta know

- Coined the paradigm of *intersectionality*, introduced by legal scholar *Kimberlé Crenshaw* in her 1989 paper *Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex* to demonstrate how anti-discrimination legal frameworks erased Black women by treating race and gender as mutually exclusive categories.
- Articulated the earliest explicit definition of *identity politics* in the 1977 *Combahee River Collective Statement*, authored by a Boston group named after *Harriet Tubman*'s 1863 raid, which asserted that the liberation of Black women would necessitate the destruction of all systems of oppression.
- Critiqued the bourgeois and exclusionary assumptions of second-wave white feminist theory through foundational texts such as *bell hooks*'s 1981 study *Ain't I a Woman?* and *Audre Lorde*'s 1979 address reminding scholars that "the master's tools will never dismantle the master's house."
- Grounded its modern theoretical framework in nineteenth-century abolitionism and suffragism, drawing directly on *Sojourner Truth*'s 1851 speech at Akron, *Ida B. Wells*'s investigative crusades against lynching, and *Anna Julia Cooper*'s 1892 book *A Voice from the South*.
- Formulated the alternative concept of *womanism*, coined by author *Alice Walker* in 1979 and expanded in *In Search of Our Mothers' Gardens*, to describe a commitment to the survival and wholeness of an entire community, embracing both women and men.
- Developed systemic sociological concepts including the *matrix of domination* and *standpoint epistemology*, expounded by *Patricia Hill Collins* in her landmark 1990 volume *Black Feminist Thought: Knowledge, Consciousness, and the Politics of Empowerment*.
- Mobilized grassroots organizations such as the *National Black Feminist Organization* in [[new-york-city]] in 1973, building upon *Frances Beale*'s influential 1969 pamphlet *Double Jeopardy: To Be Black and Female* that warned against subsuming women's liberation into Black nationalist patriarchy.

## connections

- [[civil-rights-movement]] — the mass struggle whose patriarchal leadership structures spurred Black women to establish autonomous theoretical and activist organizations.
- [[united-states]] — the primary historical setting where the specific legal, political, and institutional critiques of Black feminist theorists emerged.
- [[reconstruction-era]] — the pivotal era of emancipation and backlash during which Black women organized mutual aid clubs and fought systemic disenfranchisement.
- [[harlem-renaissance]] — the cultural renaissance whose women writers and performers challenged male-centric racial politics and articulated Black female desire.
- [[american-literature]] — the literary tradition profoundly transformed by Black feminist writers who recovered forgotten historical voices and dismantled racial caricatures.
- [[poetry]] — the essential creative medium championed by theorists like *Audre Lorde* to synthesize emotional vulnerability, radical politics, and collective liberation.
- [[cold-war]] — the geopolitical environment in which Black radical feminists connected domestic anti-racism with global anti-colonial movements despite intense state surveillance.
