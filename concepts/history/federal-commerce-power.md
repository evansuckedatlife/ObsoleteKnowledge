## summary

The **federal commerce power** is the constitutional authority granted to the Congress of the [[united-states]] to regulate commerce with foreign nations, among the several states, and with Indian tribes. Conferred by Article I, Section 8, Clause 3 of the [[united-states-constitution]], this power represents the primary textual foundation for national economic regulation by the [[federal-government-united-states]]. Through two centuries of Supreme Court interpretation, its scope has expanded from early nineteenth-century navigation conflicts into an expansive jurisdictional instrument governing industrial labor, corporate monopolies, financial institutions, and civil rights.

## you gotta know

- Derived from the Commerce Clause, drafted during the Philadelphia Convention of 1787 to eliminate the destructive interstate trade barriers and protectionist tariffs that characterized the *Articles of Confederation*.
- First comprehensively defined in the 1824 landmark Supreme Court decision *Gibbons v. Ogden*, in which Chief Justice *John Marshall* established that commerce includes navigation and struck down a state-granted monopoly over interstate steamboat transit.
- Narrowed during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries by formalistic judicial distinctions between manufacturing, agriculture, and commerce, as seen in *United States v. E. C. Knight Co.* and *Hammer v. Dagenhart*.
- Fundamentally transformed during the New Deal era following the 1937 constitutional shift, with rulings in *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.* and *United States v. Darby* establishing that Congress can regulate local activities that exert a substantial effect on interstate trade.
- Reached its peak doctrinal breadth in *Wickard v. Filburn* (1942), where the Supreme Court held that an individual farmer's home-grown wheat for personal consumption could be federally regulated due to its cumulative aggregate impact on national commerce.
- Utilized by the national legislature as the primary constitutional vehicle to enact Title II of the *Civil Rights Act of 1964*, upheld in *Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States* and *Katzenbach v. McClung* on grounds that racial discrimination burdened interstate travel and commerce.
- Constrained in modern jurisprudence by decisions like *United States v. Lopez* (1995) and *United States v. Morrison* (2000), which invalidated non-economic federal statutes that lacked an explicit economic nexus.
- Furnishes the constitutional authority enabling national regulation of monetary transfers, credit instruments, and commercial banking systems overseen by the [[federal-reserve]].

## connections

- [[united-states-constitution]] — supreme organic charter containing the Commerce Clause in Article I, Section 8, Clause 3.
- [[federal-government-united-states]] — national apparatus whose expansive domestic legislative programs rely primarily on the commerce clause for legitimacy.
- [[federal-reserve]] — central banking authority that oversees interstate transactions, commercial rediscount markets, and national banking stability under federal commerce mandates.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — twentieth-century liberation campaign whose statutory desegregation triumphs in public accommodations were legally anchored in the commerce power.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — era of rapid rail and factory expansion that forced judicial abandonment of narrow commercial definitions in favor of nationwide economic regulation.
