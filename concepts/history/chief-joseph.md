## summary

**Chief Joseph**, known to his people as *Hin-mah-too-yah-lat-kekt* ("Thunder Rolling Down the Mountain"), was a prominent leader of the Wallowa band of the Nez Perce people in the Pacific Northwest. Facing forced eviction by the *United States* government from their ancestral lands in Oregon, he helped lead his people on a legendary 1,170-mile fighting retreat toward sanctuary in Canada during the Nez Perce War of 1877. Renowned as a skilled diplomat, orator, and principled resistance figure, his tragic surrender and eloquent speeches made him an enduring symbol of Indigenous resilience against Western expansion.

## you gotta know

- Succeeded his father, *Old Joseph*, as leader of the Wallowa Nez Perce in 1871, steadfastly refusing to recognize an illegitimate 1863 "thief treaty" that ceded their fertile homeland.
- Co-led the epic 1877 retreat alongside war chiefs *Looking Glass*, *White Bird*, and his brother *Ollokot*, skillfully outmaneuvering pursuing U.S. Army units commanded by General *Oliver O. Howard*.
- The Nez Perce defeated or held off superior American military forces in numerous engagements across Oregon, Idaho, and Montana, including the Battle of the Big Hole and Canyon Creek.
- Surrounded just forty miles south of the Canadian border in the Bear Paw Mountains by troops under Colonel *Nelson A. Miles*, he surrendered on October 5, 1877, to spare the freezing women and children.
- Delivered one of the most famous surrender speeches in American history, recorded by Lieutenant *Charles Erskine Wood*, concluding with the immortal words: "Hear me, my chiefs! I am tired; my heart is sick and sad. From where the sun now stands I will fight no more forever."
- Traveled to Washington, D.C., in 1879 to advocate for his displaced tribe before President *Rutherford B. Hayes*, delivering his impassioned "An Indian's View of Indian Affairs" address in *New York City*.
- Spent his remaining years in exile in Oklahoma and the Colville Reservation in Washington state, dying in 1904 of what his attending physician poignantly diagnosed as "a broken heart."
