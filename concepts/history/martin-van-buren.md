## summary

**Martin Van Buren** (1782–1862) was an American politician who served as the eighth President of the *United States* (1837–1841) and was a dominant figure in Democratic Party politics during the Jacksonian era. Rising from humble origins in rural New York, Van Buren became a master of political organization, earning the nickname "the Magician" for his skill in navigating party factions and electoral coalitions. Though his presidency was marked by the devastating Panic of 1837 and subsequent economic depression, Van Buren's political career exemplified the emerging professional politician and the rise of mass political parties in 19th-century America.

## you gotta know

- Born in Kinderhook, New York, Van Buren rose through New York state politics as a member of the *Democratic-Republican Party*, then the Democratic Party after its reorganization; his skill in building coalitions and managing party interests made him indispensable to political figures above him.
- Served as *Andrew Jackson*'s Secretary of State (1829–1831) and Vice President (1833–1837); he was instrumental in Jackson's political victories and became Jackson's preferred successor for the presidency.
- Gained prominence during the *Petticoat Affair*, when he sided with *Peggy Eaton* (a woman of questionable reputation married to Secretary of War John Eaton) against the wives of other cabinet members; this stance earned him Jackson's favor and elevated him politically.
- Elected President in 1836, Van Buren inherited the economic chaos of the Panic of 1837, a severe financial crisis that devastated the economy during his tenure; the downturn blamed on his policies ensured his defeat in 1840 despite his having little to do with its causes.
- Refused to use federal funds to relieve the economic depression, adhering to laissez-faire principles and limited government; this principled stance, though economically sound, made him deeply unpopular as ordinary Americans suffered.
- Ran for re-election in 1840 and lost decisively to *William Henry Harrison*, ending the Democratic Party's dominance; his defeat represented a shift in electoral politics toward popular enthusiasm and mass campaigning.
- Later pursued the presidential nomination on the *Free Soil Party* ticket in 1848, opposing the expansion of slavery into new territories; his 1848 candidacy split the Democratic vote and helped elect *Zachary Taylor*, a slave-holding Whig.
- Represented the old-style political operative—expert in party mechanics and elite coalition-building—in an era when popular appeal and charisma were increasingly essential to electoral success.
