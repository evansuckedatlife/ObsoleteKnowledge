## summary

**Criminal procedure** comprises the rules and protections governing how the state conducts criminal prosecution—the rights of the accused and the obligations of law enforcement and courts. Rooted in English common law and American constitutional protections (particularly the *Fifth* and *Sixth Amendments*), criminal procedure underwent revolutionary expansion in the 1960s through landmark Supreme Court decisions (*Gideon v. Wainwright*, *Miranda v. Arizona*) that required counsel for the poor, protected against self-incrimination, and imposed due-process constraints on police. These reforms transformed criminal justice from an aristocratic privilege into a formal system with ostensible protections for all.

## you gotta know

- Criminal procedure emerged from common-law tradition and *Magna Carta* (1215), establishing that due process—notice, hearing, and trial—precedes punishment; American constitutional drafters enshrined these protections in the *Bill of Rights* (1791).
- The *Fifth Amendment* protects against self-incrimination ("I take the Fifth") and requires the state to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt; the *Sixth Amendment* guarantees the right to counsel, public trial, and confrontation of witnesses.
- For centuries, the right to counsel existed only for those wealthy enough to hire lawyers; indigent defendants faced trial unrepresented, at profound disadvantage; *Gideon v. Wainwright* (1963) established that states must provide counsel for the poor.
- *Miranda v. Arizona* (1966) required police to inform suspects of their rights before custodial interrogation—the famous "you have the right to remain silent"; the ruling aimed to protect against coercive confessions obtained through psychological manipulation.
- The 1960s criminal procedure revolution, led by Chief Justice *Earl Warren*'s Supreme Court, extended due-process protections to states and imposed federal constraints on local police practices; conservatives resented the restrictions; progressives saw them as essential protection against tyranny.
- Modern criminal procedure balances state interest in effective law enforcement against individual protection against arbitrary arrest and unjust conviction; the balance remains contested, with mass incarceration and guilty pleas (95% of convictions) suggesting procedure protects fewer than theoretically.
