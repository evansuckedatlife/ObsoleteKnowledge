## summary

**The Lost Generation**, also referred to simply as the **Lost Generation**, denotes the post-war demographic cohort and literary circle that reached maturity during [[world-war-i]]. Coined by salonnière *Gertrude Stein* and popularized by *Ernest Hemingway*, the term came to define the restless community of American expatriate artists and intellectuals who gathered in [[france]] during the 1920s. Reeling from the unprecedented devastation of mechanized combat and alienated by Victorian puritanism, this cohort catalyzed [[modernism]], radically revolutionizing transatlantic literature and capturing the psychological fracture of twentieth-century life.

## you gotta know

- *Gertrude Stein* famously coined the moniker when quoting a Parisian garage master who scolded his young mechanic with the words *"Une génération perdue"*, a remark *Ernest Hemingway* later immortalized as an epigraph to his 1926 masterpiece *The Sun Also Rises*.
- The core circle included prominent American expatriates such as *Hemingway*, *F. Scott Fitzgerald*, *John Dos Passos*, *T. S. Eliot*, and *E. E. Cummings*, whose landmark texts explored spiritual aimlessness, emotional numbness, and fractured romantic relationships.
- Grounded in the horrific devastation of [[world-war-i]], members experienced severe psychological disorientation and shell shock, leading them to repudiate nineteenth-century romanticism, bourgeois patriotism, and naive faith in historical progress.
- Expatriate cultural life centered on *Parisian* hubs on the Left Bank, particularly *Stein*'s weekly salon at *27 rue de Fleurus* and *Sylvia Beach*'s independent English-language bookshop *Shakespeare and Company*, which provided loans, mail services, and intellectual refuge.
- Across the Atlantic in the [[united-states]], the cohort's disillusionment coincided with the roaring prosperity and rebellious hedonism of [[jazz]] culture, fueled by defiance of *Prohibition* and immortalized in *Fitzgerald*'s *The Great Gatsby*.
- Stylistically, their writing spearheaded literary [[modernism]] by rejecting ornate rhetoric in favor of *Hemingway*'s influential "iceberg theory," a minimalist technique utilizing direct syntax, understated dialogue, and omitted context to convey underlying emotional truth.
- Before military enlistment or due to defective eyesight, several future authors volunteered as wartime ambulance or Red Cross drivers in [[france]] and [[italy]], an experience that directly subjected *Hemingway*, *Dos Passos*, and *Cummings* to immediate battlefield carnage.

## connections

- [[world-war-i]] — the catastrophic trench warfare that shattered nineteenth-century certainty and produced the physical and spiritual devastation haunting the authors.
- [[modernism]] — the broader twentieth-century artistic and literary revolution that embraced narrative fragmentation, direct imagery, and unconventional structure.
- [[france]] — the adopted European sanctuary where favorable exchange rates, cafe society, and artistic tolerance nurtured the American expatriate community.
- [[united-states]] — the homeland whose conservative postwar politics, commercialism, and *Prohibition* alienated young creative intellectuals.
- [[jazz]] — the revolutionary musical movement emerging from African American traditions that defined the decade's frenetic tempo and bohemian soundtrack.
- [[existentialism]] — the burgeoning philosophical stance sharing the generation's preoccupation with meaninglessness, personal authenticity, and moral ambiguity.
- [[harlem-renaissance]] — the concurrent literary awakening that similarly dismantled traditional racial and aesthetic paradigms in post-war urban America.
- [[italy]] — the southern European front where *Hemingway* was wounded while driving an ambulance, providing raw material for *A Farewell to Arms*.
