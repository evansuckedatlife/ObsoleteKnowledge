## summary

**Agriculture** as an economic sector encompasses crop cultivation and livestock raising, the foundation of human civilization and the dominant occupation for most of history. In the modern era, agriculture shifted from subsistence farming to commercial production, becoming subject to tariffs, commodity markets, and industrial mechanization. Understanding agriculture's role in history illuminates conflicts over land use, labor exploitation, trade policy, and environmental transformation—from the *Corn Laws* protecting British landowners to the mechanization of the Great Plains.

## you gotta know

- Until the late 19th century, agriculture employed the vast majority of the population in most countries; industrialization gradually reduced this proportion dramatically.
- The *Corn Laws* in Britain (1815–1846) protected domestic grain prices by taxing imports, keeping food expensive and sparking fierce political conflict between industrial and agricultural interests.
- Mechanization transformed farming: the *McCormick reaper*, *steam threshers*, and later tractors reduced labor requirements and enabled large-scale monoculture.
- Land tenure systems shaped agricultural societies: *feudalism*, *serfdom*, peasant tenancy, and eventually industrial agriculture each had different labor and power dynamics. Controlling land was the basis of wealth and political power for millennia, and struggles over land ownership, tenant rights, and redistribution remain central to political conflict worldwide.
- The *Great Plains* of North America represented a new agricultural frontier—vast acreages of wheat and corn made possible by rail transport and mechanical innovation.
- Agricultural surplus enabled civilization itself: cities, armies, and specialized classes all depended on farmers' ability to produce more than subsistence.
- Modern agriculture is defined by commodity markets, price volatility, government subsidies, and environmental externalities—monoculture depletes soil and pollutes water.
