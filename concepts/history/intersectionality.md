## summary

**Intersectionality** is the analytical framework recognizing that social identities and oppressions (race, gender, class, sexuality, disability) do not operate in isolation but interact and reinforce one another. Emerging from Black feminist theory and articulated forcefully by thinkers like *bell hooks*, intersectionality reveals how power structures compound, requiring analysis and activism that addresses multiple overlapping forms of marginalization. This concept has become foundational to contemporary social justice movements worldwide.

## you gotta know

- Rooted in the recognition that Black women and women of color face distinct oppressions that cannot be separated into isolated categories; their experiences are qualitatively different from white women's *or* Black men's experiences.
- Articulated through the famous testimony "Ain't I a Woman?" by *Sojourner Truth* (1851), which interrogated assumptions about womanhood across racial lines by pointing out that enslaved Black women were denied the protections supposedly granted to all women—decades before the framework had a name.
- Formalized as an academic framework in the late 20th century by scholars including *Kimberlé Crenshaw* (who coined the term in 1989), *Patricia Hill Collins*, and *bell hooks*, drawing on decades of Black feminist thought in the civil rights and Black Power eras.
- Demonstrates that a Black woman's experience is not simply the sum of racism plus sexism, but a unique intersection producing distinct realities; she faces discrimination and barriers that white women don't face and that Black men don't face.
- Applied to understanding discrimination and privilege: an individual may hold power in one dimension (class, sexuality) while experiencing marginalization in another (race, disability); power is not distributed uniformly across social locations.
- Central to modern feminism, activism, and policy-making: intersectional analysis reveals why one-size-fits-all solutions fail, why mainstream feminism excluding women of color has limitations, and why movements must address interconnected oppressions simultaneously.
- Extends beyond gender and race to encompass disability, sexuality, immigration status, age, religion, and other dimensions of identity and power; intersectionality recognizes that oppression is multidimensional and that liberation requires addressing all systems of domination.

## connections

- [[bell-hooks]] — the theorist who articulated how oppressions interlock in contemporary consciousness.
- [[sojourner-truth]] — whose "Ain't I a Woman?" speech embodied intersectional analysis before the term existed.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — the broader movement for racial justice that informed intersectional feminist theory.
- [[feminism]] — the movement whose focus on gender was complicated and enriched by intersectional analysis.
- [[race]] — one of the fundamental categories whose interaction with other systems of power intersectionality examines.
- [[class]] — another structural dimension whose relationship to race and gender intersectionality analyzes.
