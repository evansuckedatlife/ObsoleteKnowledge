# Suffrage

## summary

**Suffrage** is the right to vote in political elections, a fundamental aspect of democracy. The expansion of suffrage—from property-owning white men to all adult citizens regardless of race or gender—was one of the central struggles of American and world history, achieved through centuries of activism, reform, and violence in the United States.

## you gotta know

- In the *United States*, voting was initially restricted to white male property owners; successive expansions included nonproperty-owning men (early 19th century), *African Americans* (15th Amendment, 1870), and *women* (19th Amendment, 1920).
- The *Civil Rights Movement* of the 1950s–1960s challenged racial disenfranchisement through tactics including *sit-ins*, *Freedom Rides*, and the *Voting Rights Act of 1965*, which outlawed literacy tests and other mechanisms that suppressed Black voter registration.
- *Women's suffrage* took nearly a century of activism (from the 1848 *Seneca Falls Convention* to the 1920 ratification of the 19th Amendment) and included figures like *Susan B. Anthony*, *Elizabeth Cady Stanton*, and *Emmeline Pankhurst* in Britain.
- Disenfranchisement of Black voters in the South was systematic after *Reconstruction*: literacy tests, poll taxes, grandfather clauses, and violence (lynching, intimidation) kept Black citizens from voting until the *Voting Rights Act of 1965*.
- The *Fifteenth Amendment* (1870) prohibited racial discrimination in voting; the *Nineteenth Amendment* (1920) prohibited gender discrimination; the *Voting Rights Act* (1965) abolished literacy tests and authorized federal oversight of elections.
- *Suffragettes* (women advocating for voting rights) employed confrontational tactics including property destruction and hunger strikes, generating controversy while advancing the cause.
- Modern voting rights battles continue: disputes over voter ID laws, gerrymandering, and ballot access reflect ongoing tensions over who gets to vote and whose votes count.
