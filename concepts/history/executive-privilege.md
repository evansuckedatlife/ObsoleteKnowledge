## summary

The principle of **executive privilege** is the implied legal authority claimed by the President of the United States and other executive branch officials to withhold confidential communications, military secrets, or sensitive deliberative materials from legislative and judicial inquiries. Grounded in the separation of powers doctrine implicit in the Constitution, it exists to ensure candid advice within the presidency. It was definitively recognized as a qualified rather than absolute power during the Watergate crisis, cementing judicial review over executive secrecy.

## you gotta know

- First invoked in practice by *George Washington* in 1792 during a congressional inquiry into the military defeat of General *Arthur St. Clair*, and later regarding the *Jay Treaty*.
- Formally tested during the 1807 treason trial of *Aaron Burr*, where Chief Justice *John Marshall* ruled that President *Thomas Jefferson* could be issued a subpoena for documents.
- Received its definitive Supreme Court assessment in *United States v. Nixon* (1974), which held that executive privilege is qualified, not absolute, and must yield to judicial demands for evidence in criminal proceedings.
- Involves two primary recognized branches: the "presidential communications privilege," which protects direct presidential deliberations, and the "deliberative process privilege," which shields broader executive agency policymaking.
- Reaffirmed and analyzed in congressional subpoena contexts in *Trump v. Mazars USA, LLP* (2020), which established a four-part balancing test for legislative document requests.
- Invoked frequently throughout twentieth-century national security controversies and cold war defense programs regarding the state secrets doctrine.

## connections

- [[george-washington]] — first president, who established the initial precedent for withholding diplomatic and military documents.
- [[thomas-jefferson]] — president whose refusal to provide documents in the Burr treason case prompted early judicial limits on the doctrine.

- [[fourth-amendment]] — constitutional limit on government intrusion that interacts with questions of presidential oversight and warrants.
