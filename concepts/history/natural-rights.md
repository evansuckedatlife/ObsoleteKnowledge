## summary

**Natural rights**, often identified as **inalienable rights**, are fundamental moral and political entitlements held to belong inherently to all human beings by virtue of human existence rather than by legal statute or governmental grant. Formalized during the European [[enlightenment]], most influentially by English philosopher *John Locke* in his 1689 *Two Treatises of Government*, the doctrine posits that individuals in a primordial *state of nature* possess innate claims to life, liberty, and estate. By asserting that political rulers are instituted solely to protect these preexisting liberties and may be legitimately overthrown if they fail to do so, the concept provided the bedrock philosophical justification for both the [[american-revolution]] and the [[french-revolution]], permanently transforming constitutional governance across [[england]], the [[united-states]], and [[france]].

## you gotta know

- *John Locke* articulated the definitive modern formulation in his 1689 *Two Treatises of Government*, arguing that humans possess inherent rights to life, liberty, and estate that predate civil society and cannot be legitimately surrendered to an arbitrary ruler.
- *Thomas Jefferson* directly adapted Locke's framework when drafting the 1776 United States *Declaration of Independence*, famously recasting the triad as the self-evident and unalienable rights to "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness."
- In [[france]], the National Constituent Assembly enshrined the doctrine in the August 1789 *Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen*, affirming that the aim of all political associations is the preservation of the natural and imprescriptible rights of liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression.
- *Thomas Hobbes* introduced a contrasting, earlier formulation in *Leviathan* (1651), defining the right of nature (*jus naturale*) as the desperate individual liberty of self-preservation in a brutal state of nature, which subjects surrender to an absolute sovereign to escape perpetual war.
- Utilitarian legal philosopher *Jeremy Bentham* vehemently attacked the concept in *Anarchical Fallacies*, dismissing natural and imprescriptible rights as "simple nonsense: natural and imprescriptible rights, rhetorical nonsense,—nonsense upon stilts."
- English jurist *Sir William Blackstone* incorporated natural rights into common law theory in his *Commentaries on the Laws of England*, classifying primary absolute rights into personal security, personal liberty, and private property.
- The principle deeply informed the framing of the [[united-states-constitution]] and the American *Bill of Rights*, operating on the presumption that constitutions do not bestow fundamental liberties, but rather erect legal boundaries to prevent government infringement upon preexisting rights.
- Modern international human rights law traces its intellectual lineage to natural rights theory, culminating in the 1948 *Universal Declaration of Human Rights*, which translated the philosophy into a secular, globalized framework.

## connections

- [[enlightenment]] — the intellectual movement that elevated reason, empiricism, and natural rights against absolutism and divine-right monarchy.
- [[thomas-jefferson]] — American statesman who adapted Locke's philosophy into the foundational justification of American independence.
- [[american-revolution]] — the anti-colonial struggle waged on the conviction that imperial governance had violated fundamental inalienable rights.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — constitutional framework crafted to safeguard preexisting individual liberties from state tyranny.
- [[french-revolution]] — continental upheaval that radicalized rights theory through the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen.
- [[england]] — nation where John Locke, the Glorious Revolution, and common law traditions first developed modern rights discourse.
- [[united-states]] — republic established upon the formal constitutional recognition of inherent rights.
