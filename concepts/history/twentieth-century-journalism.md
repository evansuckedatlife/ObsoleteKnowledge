## summary

**Twentieth-century journalism** encompasses the reporting practices, editorial innovations, and mass-media institutions that shaped global public life throughout the twentieth century, particularly within the [[united-states]]. Defined by the transition from sensationalist turn-of-the-century yellow press toward professional standards of objectivity, the era saw the rise of influential newsmagazines, photojournalism, and network broadcasting. It reached peak authority as an indispensable civic institution during major geopolitical conflicts, serving as both an instrument of mass information and a critical watchdog over state power.

## you gotta know

- Exposed the *Watergate* scandal through investigative reporting by *The Washington Post* journalists *Bob Woodward* and *Carl Bernstein*, whose reliance on confidential source *Deep Throat* prompted congressional inquiries and the resignation of President *Richard Nixon*.
- Reached millions of American living rooms through broadcast network television during the [[cold-war]], establishing anchors such as *CBS News* veteran *Walter Cronkite* as trusted arbiters of national events from the *John F. Kennedy* assassination to the Vietnam War.
- Transformed print media through the weekly newsmagazine format pioneered by *Henry Luce*, whose [[new-york-city]] launches of [[time-magazine]] in 1923 and [[life-magazine]] in 1936 popularized concise departmental reporting and mass-circulation photojournalism.
- Brought front-line combat to domestic audiences during [[world-war-ii]], as radio correspondents like *Edward R. Murrow* delivered live rooftop reports during the London Blitz while combat photographers captured harrowing frontline images.
- Galvanized national support for the [[civil-rights-movement]] by broadcasting stark visual records of racial violence, police brutality, and peaceful demonstrations across the segregated South directly to a horrified national public.
- Pioneered the *New Journalism* movement during the 1960s and 1970s, as writers including *Tom Wolfe*, *Hunter S. Thompson*, *Joan Didion*, and *Gay Talese* integrated literary techniques, subjective immersion, and stylistic tropes from [[modernism]] into nonfiction reportage.
- Institutionalized professional codes of ethics and editorial objectivity in the early decades, spurred by *Walter Lippmann*'s philosophical critique in *Public Opinion* and the 1917 founding of the *Pulitzer Prize* to counter sensationalist yellow journalism.

## connections

- [[henry-luce]] — publishing executive who revolutionized the industry by creating weekly news and photojournalism magazines.
- [[cold-war]] — international ideological conflict that shaped press censorship debates, foreign reporting, and major investigative leaks.
- [[world-war-ii]] — global conflict that accelerated the development of live international radio reporting and field photojournalism.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — freedom struggle whose televised coverage proved decisive in turning public opinion against segregation.
- [[modernism]] — aesthetic movement whose narrative experimentation influenced the literary techniques of New Journalism.
- [[united-states]] — nation where commercial news networks, corporate publishing empires, and First Amendment protections flourished.
- [[new-york-city]] — primary publishing capital housing the corporate headquarters of leading networks, magazines, and wire services.
