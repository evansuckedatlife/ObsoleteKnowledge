## summary

**Nuclear deterrence** is the Cold War strategy of preventing war through the threat of mutually destructive nuclear retaliation, formalized as Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD). Based on rational-actor assumptions that both superpowers would avoid war if both faced total annihilation, it relied on maintaining credible second-strike capability—the assured ability to retaliate even after a nuclear first strike. Nuclear deterrence paradoxically became the principal mechanism preventing superpower conflict while accumulating tens of thousands of weapons capable of ending civilization.

## you gotta know

- Emerged from Soviet acquisition of atomic weapons (1949) and the subsequent realization that neither superpower could achieve nuclear superiority without triggering its own destruction.
- Dependence on "second-strike capability"—the ability to absorb a first strike and still retaliate with devastating force—created pressure to deploy missiles, bombers, and submarines constantly and globally.
- The doctrine assumed rational leaders would never initiate nuclear war knowing the outcome; it became less stabilizing when crises (Cuban Missile Crisis, various alerts) demonstrated how miscalculation could trigger accidental war.
- Required sophisticated early-warning systems, communication redundancy, and launch authority dispersal to prevent enemy decapitation strikes, creating complex bureaucracies of nuclear command and control.
- Generated technological arms races: ballistic missiles, submarine-launched weapons, multiple independently targeted reentry vehicles (MIRVs), and precision guidance systems all pursued to maintain deterrent credibility.
- Critics argued MAD was psychologically destabilizing and morally indefensible; proponents credited it with preventing direct U.S.-Soviet war despite decades of proxy conflicts and crises.
