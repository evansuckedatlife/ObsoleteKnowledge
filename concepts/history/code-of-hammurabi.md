## summary

The **Code of Hammurabi** is one of the oldest written legal codes, inscribed by King *Hammurabi* of Babylon around 1750 BCE. Comprising 282 laws governing everything from commercial transactions to criminal punishment, the code established principles of justice, property rights, and social hierarchy that influenced legal systems across the ancient Near East and beyond. Famous for its principle of "an eye for an eye" (*lex talionis*), the Code of Hammurabi represents a crucial development in human civilization: the transition from arbitrary power to written, publicly accessible law.

## you gotta know

- Created during the reign of King Hammurabi of Babylon (r. 1792–1750 BCE), inscribed on a stone stele (standing slab) in cuneiform script so that it could be publicly read and referenced.
- Covers diverse legal matters: marriage and divorce, business contracts, property disputes, wage disputes, construction liability, slavery, and criminal penalties ranging from fines to mutilation to death.
- Establishes a hierarchical system of justice in which punishments varied depending on the social status of the victim and perpetrator—a noble, a free commoner, and an enslaved person received different legal protections.
- Incorporates the principle of *lex talionis* ("law of retaliation"): "If a man destroys the eye of another man, his eye shall be destroyed"; though often invoked symbolically, retaliation was a genuine legal concept in the code.
- Addresses commercial practices in detail, including regulations on merchants, innkeepers, laborers, and craftspeople; establishes wage standards and penalties for fraud and breach of contract.
- Sets out military regulations, conscription obligations, and compensation for military service—reflecting Babylon's importance as a military and commercial power.
- Provides evidence of advanced urban civilization in ancient Mesopotamia and demonstrates that written law predates alphabetic writing, using cuneiform symbols on imperishable stone.
- Was rediscovered by French archaeologists in 1901 in Susa (ancient Elam, modern Iran), where Elamite conquerors had carried the stele; its survival intact is exceptional.
