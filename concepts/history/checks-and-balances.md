# Checks and Balances

## summary

**Checks and balances** is the constitutional principle that the three branches of government—executive, legislative, and judicial—hold reciprocal powers to constrain one another, preventing tyranny and the concentration of power. Enshrined in the U.S. *Constitution* (1787), this doctrine distributes authority so that no single branch can unilaterally dominate, requiring cooperation and compromise for major decisions.

## you gotta know

- The Framers of the Constitution drew on *Montesquieu*'s theory of *separation of powers*, which posited that tyranny arises when executive, legislative, and judicial authority concentrate in one body; distribution prevents abuse.
- The *Executive* (President) nominates judges, commands the military, directs foreign policy, and can veto legislation—but the *Legislative* (Congress) confirms judges, controls spending, declares war, and can override a veto with a two-thirds majority.
- The *Judiciary* (courts, ultimately the *Supreme Court*) interprets the Constitution and laws, can void unconstitutional acts (via *judicial review*), and tries impeachments—but cannot initiate action, depending on cases brought to it.
- Congress must approve the President's budget, can impeach and remove the President and judges, ratifies treaties (Senate), and declares war; the President can veto bills, propose legislation, and appoint federal judges.
- The *Senate* confirms or rejects presidential appointments (judges, cabinet officers, ambassadors); the *House* initiates impeachment; both chambers must pass legislation for it to become law.
- *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) established that courts can strike down unconstitutional laws, a critical check against legislative overreach and a keystone of the system.
- The system requires compromise and consensus, making unilateral action difficult; critics note it can also create gridlock and slow responses to crises.
