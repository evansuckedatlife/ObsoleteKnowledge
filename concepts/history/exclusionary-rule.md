## summary

The **exclusionary rule** is a legal principle in United States constitutional law that prohibits evidence obtained through unreasonable searches and seizures from being introduced in criminal trials. Established federally in the early twentieth century and later extended to state courts, it functions as the primary judicial mechanism enforcing protections against unlawful government overreach. It is one of the most frequently contested procedural doctrines in American jurisprudence due to its balance between protecting constitutional rights and excluding probative guilt evidence.

## you gotta know

- Fully incorporated against state prosecutions in the landmark 1961 Supreme Court decision *Mapp v. Ohio*, which overruled the earlier doctrine of *Wolf v. Colorado*.
- First formally adopted for federal criminal trials in the 1914 Supreme Court ruling *Weeks v. United States*, written by Justice *William R. Day*.
- Spawned the doctrine known as the "fruit of the poisonous tree," coined by Justice *Felix Frankfurter* in *Nardone v. United States* (1939), expanding exclusion to secondary evidence derived from initial illegality.
- Subject to the notable "good-faith exception" established in *United States v. Leon* (1984), which allows evidence if police reasonably relied on a defective warrant issued by a neutral magistrate.
- Governed by the "inevitable discovery" doctrine established in *Nix v. Williams* (1984) and the "independent source" doctrine articulated in *Silverthorne Lumber Co. v. United States* (1920).
- Curtailed in modern jurisprudence such as *Herring v. United States* (2009) and *Hudson v. Michigan* (2006), which limited the rule's application in cases of isolated negligence and knock-and-announce violations.
