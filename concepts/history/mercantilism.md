## summary

**Mercantilism** was the dominant economic doctrine from the 16th through 18th centuries, holding that a nation's power derived from the accumulation of precious metals and maintaining a trade surplus. Mercantilist states pursued colonies, monopolized trade routes, and enacted protective tariffs to hoard bullion and prevent rival nations from accumulating wealth. The theory justified aggressive economic nationalism and colonialism until [[Adam Smith]] and later economists dismantled its logic.

## you gotta know

- Based on the conviction that the world's wealth was finite; one nation's gain was another's loss, making economic competition inherently zero-sum.
- Equated national wealth with bullion reserves—gold and silver—leading governments to obsess over trade balances and the accumulation of precious metals hoarded in royal treasuries.
- Justified colonial expansion: colonies existed to provide raw materials to the mother country and serve as markets for manufactured goods, enriching the metropole at colonial expense.
- Inspired the Navigation Acts (especially in Britain), which reserved colonial trade for the metropole and prohibited direct trade between colonies and rival nations.
- Prompted mercantilist wars and conflicts over trade routes, colonial territories, and commercial monopolies among European powers, making economics a matter of military strategy.
- Led to the creation of chartered trading companies like the Dutch East India Company and the British East India Company to enforce monopolies and project state power.
- Assumed that government intervention—tariffs, subsidies, monopolies, colonialism—was necessary to achieve national prosperity and that state wealth directly equaled national power and security.
- Undermined by [[Adam Smith]]'s *Wealth of Nations* (1776), which argued persuasively that free trade and comparative advantage created mutual benefit, not zero-sum competition, revolutionizing economic thought worldwide.
