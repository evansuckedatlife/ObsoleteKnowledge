# Second-Wave Feminism

## summary

**Second-wave feminism** (1960s–1980s) was a broad social movement for gender equality that emerged in the United States and spread internationally, addressing workplace discrimination, reproductive rights, domestic violence, and political representation. Led by figures like *Betty Friedan* and *Gloria Steinem*, the movement challenged systemic sexism across institutions and culture, fundamentally transforming gender relations and women's participation in public life.

## you gotta know

- Second-wave feminism emerged from the limited gains of first-wave feminism (suffrage, legal rights) and the backlash of the 1950s "domesticity" ideology; women educated and radicalized by the *civil-rights-movement* and anti-war activism turned attention to their own oppression.
- *Betty Friedan's* *The Feminine Mystique* (1963) diagnosed "the problem that has no name"—the dissatisfaction of educated women confined to homemaking—and catalyzed organized resistance; she founded the *National Organization for Women* (NOW) in 1966.
- The movement fought for equal pay, workplace opportunities, and professional advancement; it challenged occupational segregation, discriminatory hiring, and the "double shift" of women working and managing households.
- Reproductive rights became central: access to contraception (the birth-control pill, legalized by *Griswold v. Connecticut*, 1965), abortion rights (secured by *Roe v. Wade*, 1973), and freedom from compulsory motherhood.
- The movement exposed domestic violence, sexual assault, and harassment as political issues rather than private problems; consciousness-raising groups became methodology for understanding systemic oppression and building solidarity.
- Second-wave feminism faced critiques from within: women of color and working-class women argued that the movement was dominated by white, middle-class perspectives; Black feminism, Chicana feminism, and lesbian feminism emerged as distinct strands addressing specific oppression intersecting with gender.
