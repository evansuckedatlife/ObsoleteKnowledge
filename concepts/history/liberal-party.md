## summary

The **British Liberal Party** emerged in the mid-19th century as a political force advocating for individual liberty, free trade, democratic reform, and gradual social progress. It dominated British politics from the 1860s through the early 20th century, serving as the primary opposition to the *Conservative Party* and driving major electoral reforms, trade liberalization, and imperial expansion.

## you gotta know

- The Liberal Party coalesced around anti-slavery positions, free-trade doctrine (opposing tariffs and feudal privileges), and gradual democratic expansion; its intellectual roots lay in *classical liberalism* and utilitarian philosophy.
- *William Gladstone* led the Liberals through four terms as Prime Minister (1868–94), championing Irish *Home Rule*, educational reform, and anti-imperialist foreign policy—though he remained constrained by Victorian propriety.
- The party alternated power with the *Conservative Party* throughout the Victorian era; the interplay between *Gladstone* and *Benjamin Disraeli* (Conservative PM) defined mid-to-late 19th-century British politics.
- Liberal governments passed transformative legislation: the *Second Reform Act* (1867) expanded the suffrage, *Education Act* (1870) began state schooling, and labor laws regulated working conditions—incrementally widening democratic participation.
- The party supported *free trade* and opposed protectionist tariffs, making it the party of industrialists, traders, and urban professionals; however, it remained imperialist in practice despite anti-imperialist rhetoric.
- Internal divisions over Irish *Home Rule* weakened the party after 1886; *Gladstone*'s commitment to Irish autonomy split the Liberals and fractured their coalition, contributing to their eventual decline.
- The rise of the *Labour Party* in the early 20th century siphoned working-class voters from the Liberals, whose middle-class orientation became an electoral liability in an age of class politics.
