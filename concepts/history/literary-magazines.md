## summary

**Literary magazines** (or "little magazines") are small-circulation periodicals dedicated to publishing poetry, prose, and criticism, often championing avant-garde and experimental work. These journals have historically been incubators for modernist innovation, platforms for neglected voices, and venues where canonical authors first appeared before achieving wider recognition.

## you gotta know

- The late 19th and early 20th centuries saw an explosion of literary magazines: *The Yellow Book*, *The Savoy*, *Poetry*, *The Dial*, and *The Little Review* became venues where experimental voices found outlets unavailable in mainstream publishing.
- *Harriet Monroe*'s *Poetry* magazine (founded 1912 in Chicago) published early work by *T.S. Eliot*, *Ezra Pound*, *Robert Frost*, and *Carl Sandburg*, becoming a canonical venue for American modernist verse.
- *James Joyce*'s *Ulysses* was serialized in *The Little Review* (1918–1920) before book publication; the magazine's editor *Margaret Anderson* faced obscenity charges for publishing it.
- *Ezra Pound* served as a de facto editor-at-large for multiple magazines, championing modernist and imagist poetry and discovering major talents like *Eliot* and *Joyce*.
- Magazines like *The Dial* and *transition* published cutting-edge criticism alongside creative work, creating intellectual ecosystems around modernism and literary theory.
- These magazines had tiny circulations (often under 1,000 copies) but disproportionate cultural influence: they shaped canons, determined what was "serious" literature, and often operated at financial loss sustained by wealthy patrons.
- The magazine model democratized publishing: unknown writers could appear alongside established figures, and editors could curate coherent aesthetic movements rather than chasing commercial bestsellers.
