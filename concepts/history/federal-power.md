## summary

**Federal power** refers to the authority vested in the national government by the *U.S. Constitution*, distinguishing it from the reserved powers of states. The Constitution enumerates specific federal powers while allowing implied powers through the *Necessary and Proper Clause*, creating ongoing tension between centralized and decentralized authority.

## you gotta know

- Enumerated powers in *Article I* grant Congress specific, listed authority over coinage, interstate commerce, taxation, national defense, and regulating immigration.
- The *Necessary and Proper Clause* (also called the Elastic Clause) permits Congress to pass laws needed to carry out its enumerated powers, vastly expanding federal reach.
- The *Commerce Clause* broadly authorizes federal regulation of interstate and foreign commerce, becoming the basis for extensive federal regulatory power covering manufacturing, agriculture, and labor.
- *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) empowered the Supreme Court to strike down federal laws as unconstitutional, limiting federal power through judicial review and establishing courts as constitutional interpreters.
- *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) affirmed that federal power takes precedence over state power and that *implied powers* derived from enumerated powers are constitutional and legitimate.
- The *Supremacy Clause* establishes that federal law is superior to state law when there is conflict, making federal authority binding on states and individuals.
- The *Tenth Amendment* reserves all powers not delegated to the federal government to the states or the people, but courts have read this narrowly, limiting its restraining effect.
- Federal power expanded dramatically during the *Progressive Era* and the *New Deal*, as the Court expanded the *Commerce Clause* to justify regulation of virtually any economic activity.
- Federalism — the distribution of power between national and state governments — remains a central and contentious tension in American constitutional law, with battles over healthcare, regulation, and civil rights.
