## summary

**Nuclear espionage** was the systematic effort by the Soviet Union and allied intelligence services to obtain classified information about American and British nuclear weapons development during and after the Manhattan Project. Soviet agents penetrated scientific laboratories and military installations, recruiting scientists, technicians, and sympathizers who provided designs, manufacturing methods, and test data that accelerated Soviet atomic capabilities. The most famous cases—Julius and Ethel Rosenberg, Klaus Fuchs—became symbols of Cold War security anxieties and ideological betrayal.

## you gotta know

- Soviet intelligence services prioritized nuclear secrets as the single highest intelligence objective, deploying dozens of agents and cultivating sources inside the Manhattan Project.
- Klaus Fuchs, a German-born British physicist, passed detailed information about bomb design and plutonium production directly to Soviet handlers, significantly advancing Soviet weapons development.
- Julius Rosenberg, an electrical engineer at Los Alamos, coordinated a ring of spies including his wife Ethel, his brother-in-law David Greenglass, and others, stealing weapons designs and test data.
- The Rosenbergs were executed in 1953, the only U.S. civilians executed for espionage during the Cold War—a punishment that reflected both the era's security hysteria and genuine damage to American security.
- Soviet acquisition of nuclear technology through espionage compressed the timeline to Soviet atomic weapons (achieved 1949) and thermonuclear weapons (achieved 1953), intensifying superpower arms race anxiety.
- Exposed the vulnerability of scientific institutions to recruitment and betrayal, leading to rigorous security clearances, compartmentalization, and security cultures that persisted throughout the Cold War.

- [[julius-and-ethel-rosenberg]] — the most notorious nuclear spies, whose execution became a symbol of Cold War espionage conflict.
