## summary

**McCarthyism** was the pervasive anti-communist paranoia and political persecution that gripped the United States from the late 1940s through the 1950s, particularly under Senator Joseph McCarthy's leadership. It exemplified how Cold War anxiety could corrode civil liberties and democratic norms, turning political dissent into suspicion and destroying careers through accusation alone.

## you gotta know

- Named after *Senator Joseph McCarthy*, a Wisconsin Republican who led the charge to root out alleged communists in government, the military, and entertainment.
- Reached its peak influence during 1950–1954, leveraging televised Senate hearings to investigate communist infiltration with minimal evidence.
- Fed by Cold War paranoia following the Soviet atomic bomb test (1949) and the communist revolution in China, turning ideological fear into institutional action.
- Created a climate of denunciation where accusation alone—without proof or due process—could destroy reputations, blacklist artists, and end careers.
- The House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) became notorious for interrogating suspected communists in Hollywood, academia, and federal agencies.
- Arthur Miller's play *The Crucible* (1953) used the Salem witch trials as an allegory for McCarthyist persecution, becoming the cultural touchstone for understanding the era.
- McCarthy's credibility collapsed during the televised Army-McCarthy hearings (1954), revealing his bullying tactics and spurious accusations to the American public.
- The Senate formally censured McCarthy in 1954 for contempt and abuse of power, marking his political decline, though the era's damage to civil liberties and reputation persisted long after.
- Prominent figures like Alger Hiss, Ethel and Julius Rosenberg, and others fell victim to McCarthy-era accusations, becoming symbols of how mass hysteria could override legal protections and individual rights.
