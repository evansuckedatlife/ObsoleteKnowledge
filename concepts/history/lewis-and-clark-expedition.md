## summary

The **Lewis and Clark Expedition** (1804–1806) was a U.S. government-sponsored exploration of the western territories acquired in the *Louisiana Purchase*, led by *Meriwether Lewis* and *William Clark*. The Corps of Discovery, numbering about 30 to 40 men plus the invaluable Shoshone woman *Sacagawea* and her infant son, traveled from the Missouri River to the Pacific Ocean and back, covering approximately 8,000 miles through uncharted wilderness. The expedition provided crucial geographical, botanical, zoological, and ethnographic knowledge of the American West and served *Thomas Jefferson*'s scientific interests while advancing American territorial claims and fur-trading networks.

## you gotta know

- President *Jefferson* commissioned the expedition in 1803, before the Louisiana Purchase was even completed, to explore western territories and search for a water route to the Pacific (the legendary *Northwest Passage*); the exploration served both scientific and geopolitical aims.
- *Meriwether Lewis*, a skilled naturalist and Jefferson's personal secretary, and *William Clark*, an experienced militia officer and frontiersman, were chosen to co-lead the expedition; their complementary skills and partnership were crucial to its success.
- The Corps of Discovery departed from *St. Louis* in May 1804 and spent the first winter (1804–1805) among the *Mandan* and *Hidatsa* peoples of North Dakota; during this stay, they hired *Sacagawea*, a Shoshone woman whose language skills, geographical knowledge, and cultural presence proved invaluable.
- The expedition documented thousands of species previously unknown to American science, including the grizzly bear, prairie dog, and numerous plant species; Lewis kept detailed journals of flora, fauna, geology, and weather.
- Reaching the *Pacific Ocean* in November 1805, the expedition established Fort Clatsop for the winter, then began the return journey in spring 1806; the party split into multiple groups to explore different routes and acquire more geographical data.
- Sacagawea's presence was crucial not only for translation but also for building diplomatic relationships with tribes the expedition encountered; her knowledge of terrain and her symbolic significance as a woman and mother humanized the American expedition to indigenous peoples.
- The expedition's return in September 1806 was celebrated as a triumph; Lewis and Clark were awarded substantial land grants and money, though Lewis's subsequent struggles with the publication of journals and depression led to his death in 1809.
- The expedition's findings encouraged American commercial interests in fur trading and western settlement, strengthened American claims to the Pacific Northwest, and established a national narrative of westward expansion and exploration that became central to American identity.
