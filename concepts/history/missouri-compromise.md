## summary

The **Missouri Compromise** (1820) was a federal legislative agreement that temporarily resolved the sectional conflict over [[slavery|slavery's]] expansion by admitting Missouri as a slave state while admitting Maine as a free state, maintaining equal representation in the Senate. Engineered by [[henry-clay]], the compromise also prohibited slavery in future territories north of the 36°30' latitude line, establishing a geographical formula for managing slavery expansion. Though it deferred conflict for three decades, the compromise ultimately failed to resolve the fundamental incompatibility between slavery and free-labor systems, contributing to the conditions that precipitated the [[american-civil-war]].

## you gotta know

- Missouri's application for statehood in 1819 triggered the crisis: free-state representatives opposed admitting another slave state, threatening Southern political balance in Congress.
- [[henry-clay]]'s compromise paired Missouri's admission as a slave state with Maine's admission as a free state, maintaining the Senate's sectional balance at 11 slave, 11 free states.
- The compromise established the 36°30' parallel as the demarcation: slavery permitted south of this line (except in Missouri itself), prohibited north, creating a simple geographic rule.
- The compromise included an explicit congressional prohibition on slavery in the [[louisiana-purchase]] territories north of 36°30', marking federal assertion of regulatory power over slavery's expansion.
- The agreement proved temporary: the [[compromise-of-1850]] re-addressed slavery in territories acquired from the Mexican-American War, introducing the concept of popular sovereignty.
- The [[dred-scott-v-sandford]] decision (1857) declared the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional, inflaming sectional tensions and accelerating the path toward civil war.

## connections

- [[henry-clay]] — the Kentucky politician and Senator whose negotiating skill made the compromise possible.
- [[slavery]] — the institution around which the sectional crisis revolved, dividing North and South over its expansion.
- [[american-civil-war]] — the conflict that the Missouri Compromise and its successors attempted but ultimately failed to prevent.
- [[compromise-of-1850]] — the later sectional accord that used different mechanisms (popular sovereignty) to address similar tensions.
- [[dred-scott-v-sandford]] — the Supreme Court decision that invalidated the compromise's geographical prohibition and sharpened sectional conflict.
- [[slavery-in-the-united-states]] — the broader institutional system that the compromise sought to manage and contain rather than resolve.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — the document whose Articles generated the sectional representation question the compromise addressed.
