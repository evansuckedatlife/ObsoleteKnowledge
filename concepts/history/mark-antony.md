## summary

**Mark Antony** (83–30 BCE) was a Roman military commander and political figure of the late Republic, most famous for his alliance with *Julius Caesar*, his role in the *Second Triumvirate* governing Rome after Caesar's death, and his romantic and political partnership with *Cleopatra* of Egypt. Ambitious and skilled in war, Antony embodied the militaristic values of late-Republican Rome but was ultimately outmaneuvered by *Octavian*, his rival in the triumvirate, leading to his defeat and death in Egypt. His relationship with Cleopatra made him legendary in literature and history, though it was primarily a strategic alliance between two ambitious leaders.

## you gotta know

- Mark Antony was born into a wealthy and political family with ties to the populist faction of the late Republic; he became an accomplished military officer and supporter of *Julius Caesar*, serving as his ally and subordinate during the Civil War against Pompey.
- After Caesar's assassination (44 BCE), Antony positioned himself as Caesar's avenger and formed the *Second Triumvirate* with *Octavian* (Caesar's heir) and *Marcus Lepidus* (a general); the three men divided the Roman world into spheres and initially worked together against Caesar's assassins.
- The Triumvirate engaged in proscriptions (killing political enemies) and civil war to consolidate power; Antony was particularly successful militarily against *Parthia*'s threats in the east, though an invasion of Parthia itself proved costly and unsuccessful.
- Antony's alliance with *Cleopatra* of Egypt strengthened his position in the eastern Mediterranean; the two leaders combined resources, political legitimacy, and personal attraction to create a powerful eastern coalition against Octavian's growing power in the west.
- The triumvirate gradually collapsed as Octavian maneuvered against his rivals; he positioned himself as the defender of Rome and Roman traditions against Antony and Cleopatra, whom he portrayed as under the spell of an exotic foreign queen.
- At the *Battle of Actium* (31 BCE), Octavian defeated Antony and Cleopatra's naval forces in a decisive battle; Antony and Cleopatra retreated to Egypt, and when Octavian pursued them, Antony committed suicide rather than face capture.
- Antony represented the old militaristic virtue of the Republic, while Octavian embodied the ruthlessness and political calculation that characterized imperial Rome; Antony's defeat marked the transition from the Republic to the Empire.
- Antony has been portrayed in literature and drama as a tragic figure—capable, charismatic, but ultimately undone by love for Cleopatra and overambition; Shakespeare's *Antony and Cleopatra* is the most famous literary treatment of his story.
