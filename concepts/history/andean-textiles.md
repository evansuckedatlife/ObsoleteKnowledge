## summary

**Andean textiles** and ancient traditions of **Andean weaving** represent one of the most sophisticated, technically versatile fiber art systems in world history, originating before 2500 BCE in western South America and culminating under the *Inca Empire*. Crafted from native cotton and the fleece of domesticated camelids on portable backstrap looms, these fabrics fulfilled essential religious, funerary, diplomatic, and administrative functions across successive civilizations including the *Chavín*, *Paracas*, *Moche*, and *Wari*. Exceptional preservation in hyper-arid coastal burial environments has allowed thousands of intricate tapestries, double cloths, and embroideries to survive intact, revealing a culture that valued woven fiber far above precious gold or silver. The scholarly study of these textiles demonstrates how fiber technology served as the preeminent visual and political communication system of ancient Andean civilization prior to the arrival of [[spain]].

## you gotta know

- Royal *Inca* tunic garments known as *unku* functioned as the supreme markers of social authority, featuring intricate geometric boxed patterns called *tocapu* that signaled ethnic lineage, military honors, and bureaucratic standing throughout the empire.
- The [[quipu]], a sophisticated apparatus of colored, knotted cotton and camelid cords, served as the primary statistical, census, and narrative record-keeping instrument in the absence of alphabetic writing.
- *Paracas* necropolis mortuary mantles, preserved in hyper-arid desert peninsula sands, feature astonishing block-color and linear-style embroidery depicting flying shamans, predatory birds, felines, and polymorphic underworld deities.
- Raw materials centered on two indigenous ecological resources: coastal gossypium cotton and highland camelids, with delicate *vicuña* and silky *alpaca* wool reserved exclusively for aristocratic and religious garments.
- The imperial state systematically monopolized elite textile production through *acllacuna* ("chosen women"), who spun sacred *qompi* cloth within secluded cloisters (*acllahuasi*) for ceremonial burnt offerings and political patronage gifts.
- The *backstrap loom*, tethered to a post and anchored around the weaver's waist, allowed continuous tactile tension adjustments to produce complete four-selvage fabrics without cutting warp threads.
- Archaeological excavations at pre-ceramic coastal sites like *Huaca Prieta* revealed indigo-dyed cotton fabrics dating back roughly 6,000 years, confirming that sophisticated Andean weaving techniques long predated the regional adoption of fired pottery.

## connections

- [[spain]] — the conquering monarchy that looted Inca textile storehouses, disrupted state weaving networks, and forcibly transformed Andean camelid pasturing.
- [[colonialism]] — the imperial administrative framework that compelled native weavers into oppressive textile sweatshops known as *obrajes* to settle Spanish tribute demands.
- [[age-of-exploration]] — the era of global European expansion that brought Spanish expeditions into contact with the sophisticated fiber-based economies of Tawantinsuyu.
- [[dna]] — biomolecular analysis applied to archaeological camelid fibers and cotton cultivars from desert burials to map pre-Columbian breeding selections and trade routes.
- [[silk-road]] — the Eurasian commercial network where woven textiles similarly operated as high-value currency, diplomatic tribute, and elite markers of sovereignty.
- [[quipu]] — the Andean recording medium that drew directly upon textile techniques, incorporating cord spin direction, ply structure, and color symbolism.
- [[inca-empire]] — the late pre-Columbian civilization that utilized centralized cloth redistribution as its primary mechanism for sustaining loyalty, reciprocity, and military conscription.
