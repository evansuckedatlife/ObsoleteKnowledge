## summary

**Icon painting** is the practice of creating sacred religious images in the *Byzantine* and *Orthodox Christian* traditions, rooted in theology as much as aesthetics. Icons depict Christ, the Virgin Mary, saints, and biblical narratives using gold leaf, tempera paint, and stylized forms designed to open spiritual rather than naturalistic windows into divine presence. Developed across the [[byzantine-empire|Byzantine Empire]] and central to [[eastern-orthodox-church|Eastern Orthodox]] worship, icon painting remains one of history's most sophisticated visual-theological traditions, balancing rigid liturgical convention with profound artistic innovation.

## you gotta know

- Emerged during the [[byzantine-empire|Byzantine period]] (roughly 4th–15th centuries) as a distinctly Orthodox approach to religious visual art.
- Grounded in theological principle: icons were understood as windows to the divine rather than mere decoration or superstition.
- Iconoclasm (image-breaking) persisted as a doctrinal crisis from roughly 726–843 CE, with emperors and bishops debating whether religious images violated monotheistic purity.
- The Seventh Ecumenical Council (787) officially sanctioned icon veneration, distinguishing between worshipping God and honoring images as aids to worship.
- Used highly formal conventions: frontal poses, gold backgrounds (representing eternity), elongated figures, symbolic colors, and stylized proportions rather than anatomical realism.
- [[perspective|Perspective]], which revolutionized Western Renaissance art, was deliberately rejected in Orthodox icons, which used "reverse perspective" to emphasize spiritual reality over optical naturalism.
- Extended beyond [[byzantine-empire|Byzantium]] to Russia, Serbia, and other Orthodox lands, becoming central to religious identity and national culture.
