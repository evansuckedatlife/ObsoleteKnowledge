## summary

The **Early Middle Ages** (c. 500–1000 CE), once called the "Dark Ages," was the period of Europe's transformation following the collapse of the Western *Roman Empire*. Barbarian kingdoms replaced Roman authority, the Christian Church became the primary institution of continuity, and feudalism emerged as the organizing principle of power and land. Though literacy declined and cities shrunk, this era laid the foundations for medieval and modern Europe.

## you gotta know

- Marked by the retreat of Roman governmental structures and the rise of Germanic kingdoms (Visigothic, Ostrogothic, Vandal, Frank).
- The *Frankish Empire* under *Charlemagne* (768–814) briefly reunified much of western Europe, establishing the template for medieval kingship.
- The Christian Church filled the vacuum left by Roman collapse, preserving literacy through monasteries and providing moral authority to fragmented kingdoms.
- Islam's expansion (seventh–eighth centuries) erased Christian North Africa and the Mediterranean as a unified zone, redirecting trade routes eastward.
- *Feudalism* developed as a system of reciprocal obligations between lords and vassals, replacing large-scale imperial administration with local strongmen.
- Population declined, cities were abandoned for rural settlements, and long-distance trade contracted sharply.
- The *Battle of Tours* (732) halted Islamic expansion into western Europe, preserving Christendom's territorial base and shaping the continental divide for centuries.
- Written Latin fragmented into Romance languages; literacy became the province of clergy and monasteries, leaving most of the population illiterate.
- *Charlemagne's* coronation as "Emperor of the Romans" in 800 CE attempted to restore imperial continuity, but his empire fragmented after his death, dividing among his heirs.
- Monastic communities became centers of learning and preservation; monks copied manuscripts, maintained agricultural innovation, and maintained continuity of classical knowledge through the cultural darkness.
