## summary

The **Corn Laws** were British tariffs on imported grain enacted to protect domestic agricultural interests from foreign competition. Maintained through the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, they kept bread prices artificially high, enriching landowners while impoverishing workers and farmers. Industrial manufacturers demanded their repeal, arguing that cheap grain would lower wages and increase profits; working-class movements demanded repeal to reduce hunger. Their repeal by *Robert Peel* in 1846 marked a victory for free-trade doctrine and a turning point in British industrial dominance.

## you gotta know

- The Corn Laws originated in medieval grain export monopolies but were systematized in the eighteenth century as protective tariffs; they banned grain imports unless domestic prices reached specified thresholds, artificially inflating bread prices.
- Agricultural interests—the landowning aristocracy that dominated Parliament—supported the Corn Laws to maintain high grain prices and rents; industrialists and workers opposed them as obstacles to profit, wage compression, and cheap living costs.
- The *Irish Potato Famine* (1845–1849) forced the issue: with millions starving while Ireland exported grain, Peel made repeal of the *Corn Laws* urgent; he argued that free trade in grain would lower prices and save lives.
- The *Anti-Corn Law League*, founded in 1839, mobilized public opinion against the tariffs through rallies, petitions, and pamphlets; led by *Richard Cobden* and *John Bright*, it became one of the first mass political campaigns in modern history.
- Peel, a Conservative, repealed the *Corn Laws* in 1846 despite landowner opposition within his party; the repeal split the Conservative Party and vindicated free-trade ideology championed by economists like *Adam Smith* and *David Ricardo*.
- The *Corn Laws*' repeal symbolized the ascendance of industrial capital over agrarian capital in Britain; it hastened agricultural decline and urbanization, contributed to Britain's industrial and commercial supremacy, and established free trade as British orthodoxy until 1931.
