## summary

**Judicial review** is the power of courts, particularly the *Supreme Court* of the *United States*, to examine laws and government actions and determine whether they conform to the *Constitution*; laws or acts deemed unconstitutional can be struck down and rendered unenforceable. Though not explicitly mentioned in the Constitution, the doctrine was first asserted by the Supreme Court in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) and has since become a cornerstone of American constitutional governance. Judicial review enables the judiciary to enforce constitutional limits on legislative and executive power, making it essential to the system of [[checks-and-balances]].

## you gotta know

- The *Supreme Court* first claimed the power of judicial review in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), when Chief Justice *John Marshall* ruled that the Court had authority to void unconstitutional acts of Congress.
- Judicial review applies to federal laws, state laws, and executive actions; all are subject to scrutiny against constitutional requirements, though the Court exercises this power selectively through its docket discretion.
- The doctrine rests on the principle that the Constitution is the supreme law of the land; any statute contradicting it is therefore void, and it is the Court's role to say what the Constitution means.
- Judicial review does not give courts unlimited power; the Court may defer to legislative or executive judgments when constitutional meaning is ambiguous, or when the Constitution grants discretion to the political branches.
- The *Fourteenth Amendment*, ratified in 1868, became a major vehicle for judicial review; the Court has used it to strike down state laws restricting civil rights, particularly after the mid-20th century expansion of constitutional protections.
- *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) exemplified judicial review's power; the Supreme Court used it to overturn longstanding precedent and declare racial segregation in schools unconstitutional, forcing social change through judicial mandate.
- Different constitutional eras have seen different levels of judicial activism; the Court in the early 20th century struck down Progressive legislation, while the mid-20th century Court expanded rights protections.
