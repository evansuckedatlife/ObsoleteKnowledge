## summary

**War crimes** are violations of the laws of war—acts perpetrated during armed conflict that breach internationally recognized conventions governing treatment of civilians, prisoners of war, and the disabled. Though organized warfare has ancient codes of conduct, formal codification emerged only after World War II through the *Nuremberg Trials* and the *Geneva Conventions*. War crimes remain contested in application: some acts are universally condemned, while others are debated as inherent to warfare, strategic necessity, or cultural context.

## you gotta know

- The *Hague Conventions* (1899, 1907) first attempted to codify laws of warfare in international law, prohibiting certain weapons and mandating humane treatment of the wounded and prisoners.
- World War II generated atrocities on an industrial scale: Nazi genocide, Japanese medical experiments, and Allied bombing of civilian centers raised the question of whether *all* wartime killing could be prosecuted.
- The *Nuremberg Trials* (1945–1946) established the principle that military orders do not excuse crimes against humanity—commanders and officials could be held personally accountable for atrocities.
- The *Geneva Conventions* (1949, revised) created binding international standards for treatment of prisoners, the wounded, and civilians; subsequent protocols (1977) extended protection.
- [[hiroshima-and-nagasaki]] (1945) killed ~200,000 civilians in atomic bombings; debate persists whether strategic bombing of civilian populations constitutes a war crime or is defensible as hastening war's end.
- [[nanjing-massacre]] (1937–1938) saw Japanese forces systematically rape, torture, and execute an estimated 300,000 Chinese civilians; Japanese denial of the scale and nature of atrocities remained contentious for decades.
- [[my-lai]] massacre (1968), where U.S. troops murdered 347–504 Vietnamese civilians including children, became the definitive modern example of prosecutable war crime; only the commanding officer, *William Calley*, was initially convicted.
- Prosecution of war crimes remains politically contested: powerful nations block investigation of their own conduct (U.S. refusal to ratify the International Criminal Court), while weaker nations face disproportionate scrutiny.

## connections

- [[hiroshima-and-nagasaki]] — atomic bombing of civilian centers, debated as war crime or strategic necessity.
- [[nanjing-massacre]] — systematic slaughter of Chinese civilians by Japanese forces.
- [[my-lai]] — massacre of Vietnamese civilians by U.S. troops, the era's most infamous war crime prosecution.
- [[nuremberg-trials]] — post-war trials that established individual accountability for wartime atrocities.
- [[international-law]] — framework within which war crimes are defined and prosecuted.
- [[geneva-conventions]] — treaties codifying treatment of prisoners, wounded, and civilians.
- [[world-war-ii]] — conflict that generated systematic war crimes and prompted legal codification.
