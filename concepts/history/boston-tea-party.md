## summary

The **Boston Tea Party**, referred to by contemporaries as **The Destruction of the Tea**, was a pivotal political protest carried out on the night of December 16, 1773, at Griffin's Wharf in Boston, Massachusetts. American colonists, organized by the *Sons of Liberty*, boarded three merchant vessels and cast 342 chests of imported tea into Boston Harbor to defy the imperial British *Tea Act of 1773*. This dramatic act of defiance galvanized resistance across the Thirteen Colonies, provoked the punitive *Coercive Acts* from the Parliament of [[england]], and set the colonies on an irrevocable trajectory toward the [[american-revolution]].

## you gotta know

- Organized primarily by the *Sons of Liberty*, an underground patriot network guided by political organizers including *Samuel Adams*, who rallied thousands of townsfolk at the *Old South Meeting House* before the raid.
- Sparked by the *Tea Act of 1773*, which granted the financially distressed *British East India Company* a monopoly on tea importation into British America and undercut local colonial merchants despite retaining the Townshend import duty.
- Carried out aboard three merchant ships—the *Dartmouth*, the *Eleanor*, and the *Beaver*—while a fourth ship, the *William*, had run aground off Cape Cod and never reached harbor.
- Disguised superficially as *Mohawk* warriors, more than one hundred men boarded the vessels in disciplined squads, carefully smashed 342 wooden chests with axes, and dumped roughly forty-five tons of leaves without looting other cargo or damaging private property.
- Led the British Parliament and *King George III* to enact the *Coercive Acts* (known in the colonies as the *Intolerable Acts*) in 1774, which shut the Port of Boston, revoked the Massachusetts charter, and mandated the quartering of British soldiers in private buildings.
- Prompted the convening of the First *Continental Congress* in Philadelphia in September 1774, where colonial delegates unified to coordinate an economic boycott against British imports and petition the crown for redress.
- Inspired subsequent tea protests throughout other American ports, including the burning of the *Peggy Stewart* in Annapolis, Maryland, and copycat dumpings in New York and Charleston.
- Remained largely known as the *Destruction of the Tea* for fifty years until nineteenth-century historical memory, revived during the 1830s by surviving participants such as *George Robert Twelves Hewes*, popularized the enduring modern title.

## connections

- [[american-revolution]] — the military and political rupture precipitated by British retaliation to the protest.
- [[british-empire]] — the imperial power whose parliamentary taxes and colonial monopolies provoked the rebellion.
- [[george-washington]] — the future continental commander who disapproved of private property destruction while vigorously opposing the *Coercive Acts*.
- [[thomas-jefferson]] — the Virginian statesman who drafted resolutions supporting Boston and linked its plight to continental liberty.
- [[england]] — the metropolitan nation whose Parliament passed punitive legislation targeting Massachusetts.
- [[colonialism]] — the broader global framework of imperial extraction and mercantilism that privileged crown chartered corporations over colonial subjects.
- [[british-burma]] — a later colonial territory subjugated by the same *British East India Company* whose commercial privileges sparked the Boston uprising.
