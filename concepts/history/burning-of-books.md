## summary

The **burning of books** refers to the state-directed intellectual purge carried out in 213–212 BCE under Qin Shi Huang, the founding emperor of the Qin dynasty in imperial *China*. Known in traditional historiography alongside the execution of intellectuals as *fenshu kengru* ("**burning of books and burying of scholars**"), the campaign was designed by Chancellor Li Si to enforce ideological conformity through strict Legalism. By eradicating unofficial histories and classical philosophical works, the imperial decree sought to delegitimize dissent that appealed to antique Confucian precedent and consolidate absolute state authority.

## you gotta know

- Promulgated in 213 BCE by Chancellor Li Si, who argued that independent scholars citing ancient annals and classics undermined public order and imperial authority.
- Mandated the burning of all regional historical chronicles other than the Qin records, along with private copies of the Confucian *Classic of History* and *Classic of Poetry*.
- Spared practical and utilitarian volumes, including treatises on agriculture, medicine, pharmacology, forestry, and traditional forms of divination such as the *I Ching*.
- Commanded that individuals discussing ancient literature in public be put to death, and those who praised the past to criticize the present be executed alongside their clans.
- Paired by Han historian Sima Qian in the *Records of the Grand Historian* (*Shiji*) with the burying alive of 460 Confucian scholars (*kengru*) who allegedly defied imperial decrees.
- Prompted scholars to risk their lives by concealing scrolls within walls and hollowed house pillars, as famously occurred at the family home of Confucius in Qufu.
- Sparked the long-lasting Han dynasty intellectual schism between the "New Text" and "Old Text" traditions over reconstructing lost pre-Qin literature.
- Reassessed by modern historians who argue that Han-era Confucian writers exaggerated the total destructiveness of the event to paint Legalist Qin rule in a tyrannical light.

## connections

- [[china]] — ancient civilization whose first centralized empire implemented this sweeping cultural purge to enforce political conformity.
- [[silk-road]] — trans-Eurasian trade network that later flourished under the Han dynasty, which worked tirelessly to reconstruct destroyed classical texts.
- [[poetry]] — literary form whose foundational anthology, the Confucian *Classic of Poetry* (*Shijing*), was banned and purged from private hands.
- [[enlightenment]] — modern European intellectual movement whose defense of free expression stands in stark contrast to ancient Qin ideological suppression.
- [[tragedy]] — dramatic concept frequently applied by classical historians when describing the annihilation of ancestral literature and scholarly lives.
- [[satire]] — literary mode of social critique that was completely outlawed under Qin statutes punishing covert mockery of imperial law.
