## summary

**The labor movement**—also known as **organized labor** or **trade unionism**—is the broad historical and political struggle by working people to secure fair wages, safer workplaces, reasonable hours, and democratic representation on the job. Arising in force during the late nineteenth century across the [[united-states]] and Europe in response to the rapid rise of industrial capitalism following the [[industrial-revolution]], the movement transformed economic life through mass strikes, collective bargaining, and legislative lobbying. It fundamentally reshaped modern society by winning foundational protections such as the eight-hour workday, the weekend, child labor bans, and the legal right to organize.

## you gotta know

- Mobilized nationwide strikes and demonstrations demanding the eight-hour workday, culminating in the tragic 1886 *Haymarket affair* in Chicago, which became a global rallying cry and established *May Day* as international workers' day.
- Launched the massive 1894 *Pullman Strike* against wage cuts under the leadership of *Eugene V. Debs* and the *American Railway Union*, paralyzing western railroads until President *Grover Cleveland* deployed federal troops to break the strike.
- Achieved its decisive legislative triumph during the *New Deal* with the 1935 *National Labor Relations Act* (the *Wagner Act*), which guaranteed private-sector employees the right to unionize, established the *National Labor Relations Board*, and penalized unfair labor practices.
- Divided historically between craft unionism championed by *Samuel Gompers* in the *American Federation of Labor* (AFL) and wall-to-wall industrial unionism organized by *John L. Lewis* in the *Congress of Industrial Organizations* (CIO), which merged in 1955.
- Prompted sweepingly rigorous workplace safety standards, fire codes, and compulsory workers' compensation laws following the 1911 *Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire* in [[new-york-city]], reforms spearheaded in government by future Labor Secretary *Frances Perkins*.
- Faced severe legislative retrenchment during the early [[cold-war]] under the 1947 *Taft-Hartley Act*, which curbed strike powers, outlawed jurisdictional disputes and secondary boycotts, and permitted state right-to-work statutes over presidential veto.
- Developed militant tactics of nonviolent direct action during the landmark 1936–1937 *Flint sit-down strike*, in which *United Auto Workers* members occupied assembly plants to force *General Motors* into recognizing their union.
- Generated radical socialist and anarcho-syndicalist currents embodied by the *Industrial Workers of the World* (the *Wobblies*), led by *Big Bill Haywood* and *Mother Jones*, who advocated organizing all wage workers into "One Big Union."

## connections

- [[industrial-revolution]] — created the mechanization, hazardous factory labor, and urban wage-dependency that necessitated worker solidarity and collective defense.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — allied closely in campaigns for racial and economic justice, culminating in the 1963 March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom co-organized by *A. Philip Randolph*.
- [[cold-war]] — unleashed anti-communist loyalty oaths and purges that expelled left-wing unions and curtailed radical rank-and-file activism.
- [[world-war-i]] — catalyzed temporary federal labor protections and board mediation to prevent wartime walkouts, followed by intense post-war strike waves and employer crackdowns.
- [[reconstruction-era]] — fostered early national organizations such as the *National Labor Union* and the *Knights of Labor* amidst sweeping debates over free labor.
- [[new-york-city]] — served as the epicenter of immigrant garment workers' strikes, radical intellectual publishing, and pivotal municipal public-sector union struggles.
- [[martin-luther-king-jr]] — championed the interconnectedness of civil rights and economic dignity, meeting his assassination while marching in solidarity with striking sanitation workers.
