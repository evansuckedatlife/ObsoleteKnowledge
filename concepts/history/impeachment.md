## summary

**Impeachment** is the constitutional process by which the legislative branch of the [[united-states]] formally indicts, tries, and potentially removes civil officers—most notably the president—for egregious official misconduct. Codified within the [[united-states-constitution]] and derived from centuries of English parliamentary practice, this mechanism assigns the sole authority of drafting and approving articles of indictment to the House of Representatives, while granting the Senate the exclusive power to try the accused. As the ultimate check in the American system of separated powers, it provides a peaceful, legal avenue for expelling federal officials guilty of *"treason, bribery, or other high crimes and misdemeanors"*, thereby preserving constitutional governance against executive tyranny and systemic corruption.

## you gotta know

- The [[united-states-constitution]] divides the procedure into two distinct stages: the House of Representatives acts as a grand jury to investigate and approve articles of impeachment by simple majority, while the Senate conducts the formal trial.
- Conviction and removal require a two-thirds supermajority of senators present, an exacting standard designed by the framers to prevent purely partisan removals from destabilizing the executive branch.
- Three American presidents have been impeached by the House: *Andrew Johnson* in 1868 during the [[reconstruction-era]], [[bill-clinton]] in 1998, and *Donald Trump* in 2019 and 2021; none was convicted by the Senate.
- President *Richard Nixon* resigned in August 1974 after the House Judiciary Committee approved three articles during the Watergate scandal, preempting full House impeachment and virtually certain Senate conviction.
- When the president of the [[united-states]] is tried, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court presides over the Senate, eliminating the conflict of interest inherent in having the Vice President oversee the trial.
- The standard *"high crimes and misdemeanors"* was adopted from English parliamentary jurisprudence during the 1787 Constitutional Convention, chosen by *George Mason* and *James Madison* to target abuses of public power while rejecting the vague term *"maladministration"*.
- Constitutional penalties upon conviction are strictly limited to removal from office and potential disqualification from holding future federal office, leaving criminal liability and punishment to ordinary judicial courts.
- Precedent established during the 1797 trial of Senator *William Blount* determined that members of Congress are not *"civil officers"* subject to impeachment, meaning legislators can only be expelled by their respective chambers.

## connections

- [[united-states-constitution]] — establishes the legal standards, procedural division of power, and voting thresholds for federal removal.
- [[united-states]] — the federal republic whose separation of powers and system of checks and balances rely on this mechanism.
- [[reconstruction-era]] — the volatile post-war era that witnessed the historic impeachment and near-conviction of *Andrew Johnson*.
- [[bill-clinton]] — the 42nd president, impeached by the House following the Whitewater inquiry and independent counsel *Kenneth Starr's* findings.
- [[14th-amendment]] — landmark Reconstruction amendment enacted alongside the bitter congressional disputes over executive authority and reconstruction.
- [[american-civil-war]] — the national conflict whose aftermath produced the fierce executive-legislative confrontation culminating in Johnson's trial.
- [[george-washington]] — presided over the 1787 Constitutional Convention that established and debated the constitutional removal clauses.
