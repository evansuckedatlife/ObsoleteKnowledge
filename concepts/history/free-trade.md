## summary

**Free trade** is the economic principle that goods and services should move across borders without tariffs, quotas, or other protective barriers, determined by market forces rather than government intervention. Also known as **laissez-faire** when applied to all economic sectors, free trade was championed by [[adam-smith]] and later codified as economic policy during the [[industrial-revolution]], becoming the dominant ideology of 19th-century Britain under the [[anti-corn-law-league]]. The doctrine posits that specialization and unrestricted commerce benefit all trading parties, though it remains historically contested by nations seeking to protect domestic industries.

## you gotta know

- [[adam-smith]]'s *Wealth of Nations* (1776) laid the philosophical groundwork for free trade by arguing that self-interest and competition drive efficient production and lower prices.
- The [[corn-laws]], which imposed tariffs protecting English grain producers, were repealed in 1846 after decades of campaign by the [[anti-corn-law-league]], marking free trade's political triumph in Britain.
- Free trade theory asserts comparative advantage: nations should specialize in goods they produce most efficiently and trade for others, benefiting the entire world economy.
- The [[industrial-revolution]] accelerated free trade adoption as British manufacturers, confident in their technological superiority, sought unrestricted export markets.
- *Protectionism*—the opposite philosophy—resurged during economic crises, notably the Great Depression, with nations raising tariffs in response to competitive devaluation.
- Modern free trade is embodied in institutions like the WTO and trade agreements like NAFTA, though "free trade" agreements typically contain many exceptions and carve-outs.

## connections

- [[adam-smith]] — the Scottish philosopher whose economic theories provided the intellectual foundation for free trade doctrine.
- [[anti-corn-law-league]] — the political movement that successfully campaigned for free trade by abolishing protective grain tariffs.
- [[corn-laws]] — the protectionist tariffs that became the focal point of the free trade debate in 19th-century Britain.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — the economic transformation that made British manufacturers confident enough to embrace free trade competition globally.
- [[mercantilism]] — the earlier economic philosophy emphasizing national self-sufficiency and trade surpluses that free trade rejected.
- [[england]] — the nation where free trade theory was developed and first implemented as national policy.
- [[tariff]] — the tax instrument through which governments restrict or facilitate international trade.
