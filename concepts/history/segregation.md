## summary

**Segregation** was the systematic separation of people by race in American society, enforced through law and custom from the Reconstruction era through the Civil Rights Movement. It took two forms: *de jure* segregation, mandated by statute (particularly in the South), and *de facto* segregation, enforced by social practice and economics (especially in the North). The doctrine of "separate but equal," upheld by the Supreme Court in *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896), legitimized racial apartheid for nearly six decades until [[Brown v. Board of Education]] dismantled it.

## you gotta know

- **De jure segregation** was enforced by law through [[Jim Crow laws]], which mandated separate public facilities, schools, and transportation for Black and white Americans.
- **De facto segregation** operated through residential discrimination, economic barriers, and social custom without explicit legal mandate, particularly in northern states.
- The doctrine of "separate but equal" was established by the 1896 Supreme Court decision in *Plessy v. Ferguson*, which upheld segregation as constitutional.
- *Separate but equal* in practice meant unequal: Black schools received a fraction of white schools' funding; facilities were vastly inferior.
- Segregation permeated American life: water fountains, restaurants, hospitals, theaters, prisons, and even cemeteries maintained racial separation.
- The [[Civil Rights Movement]] of the 1950s–60s directly challenged segregation through sit-ins, freedom rides, and mass demonstrations, making it a defining moral issue.
- [[Brown v. Board of Education]] (1954) declared school segregation unconstitutional, though implementation faced massive resistance in the South.

## connections
