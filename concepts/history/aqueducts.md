## summary

**Aqueducts** were monumental water-delivery systems built by the *Romans* to transport fresh water from distant sources to cities and military camps. An engineering marvel of the ancient world, they relied on gravity, arches, and underground channels to supply Rome and its empire with reliable freshwater for drinking, bathing, and irrigation.

## you gotta know

- Built over 11 major aqueducts into *Rome* alone by the imperial period; at peak (2nd century CE), delivered over 1 million cubic meters of water daily to support a city of ~1 million people.
- Relied on calculated gradients (typically 0.3% slope) to move water using gravity alone rather than pumps—a feat of surveying precision and engineering foresight.
- Extended 50–90 km from mountain springs to the city, with underground tunnels, concrete piping, and surface channels; the *Aqua Claudia* reached 70 km.
- Signature arched viaducts (like the *Pont du Gard* in southern France, 50 m high) were architectural icons as well as functional, using the Roman arch innovation for massive spans.
- Supplied water to public fountains (often free to citizens), bathhouses (*thermae*—luxury public baths drawing thousands daily), and wealthy private residences connected by pipes.
- Required constant maintenance and a specialized workforce (*aquarii*, *vilici aquarum*); *Frontinus*, a chief water administrator (c. 97 CE), documented their operation in his surviving treatise *De aquaeducti*.
- Extended far beyond Rome: presence throughout the empire (Spain, North Africa, the Levant, Britain); dozens of regional aqueducts served provincial cities, enabling urbanization.
- Supported the mythology and reality of Roman civilization: unlimited water symbolized Roman power and prosperity; loss of aqueduct networks marked the decline of Roman cities.
- Collapsed after Roman infrastructure breakdown; knowledge of large-scale water engineering and organized maintenance systems was lost to medieval Europe for centuries.
