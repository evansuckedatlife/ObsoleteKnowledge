## summary

**Separation of Church and State** is the principle that government institutions must remain neutral toward religion, neither promoting nor inhibiting religious practice. Rooted in [[Enlightenment]] political theory and embedded in the American Constitution through the [[First Amendment]]'s Establishment Clause, it held that civil law and religious authority operate in distinct spheres. *James Madison* and *Thomas Jefferson* championed it as essential to both religious liberty and secular governance—ensuring neither the state oppresses faith nor the church corrupts politics.

## you gotta know

- The principle emerged from Enlightenment thinkers, particularly *John Locke*, who argued that religious conscience cannot be coerced by civil law.
- *Thomas Jefferson* wrote that a "wall of separation" must exist between church and state to protect both institutions from corruption.
- *James Madison* fought for Virginia's disestablishment, successfully severing the state's funding of churches and establishing religious liberty as a civil right.
- The First Amendment's Establishment Clause—"Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion"—encoded the principle at the national level.
- *Plessy v. Ferguson* and later cases tested whether public funds could support religious institutions without violating the principle.
- The Supreme Court's *Engel v. Vitale* (1962) ruled school prayer unconstitutional, extending the separation to public education.
- Modern debates center on where the boundary lies: can government fund religious schools? Display religious symbols on public grounds?
