## summary

**Bushido**, literally "the way of the warrior," is the ethical and moral code governing the conduct of *samurai* in feudal *Japan*. Synthesizing elements of Buddhism, Shinto, and Confucian philosophy, bushido emphasized loyalty, honor, martial skill, and readiness for death. Though formalized as a philosophical system primarily during the Edo Period (1603–1868), it has become central to Japanese identity and continues to influence Japanese culture, aesthetics, and values.

## you gotta know

- The name combines "bushi" (warrior) and "do" (way/path), reflecting a philosophical rather than merely practical code of conduct.
- Emerged during the feudal period but was systematized and codified intellectually during the *Edo Period*, when samurai had transitioned from active warriors to administrative bureaucrats.
- Central virtues included loyalty to one's lord (*daimyo*), honor above life itself, martial proficiency, and readiness to commit *seppuku* (ritual suicide) rather than face dishonor.
- Influenced by Confucian emphasis on hierarchy, duty, and filial piety; Buddhist acceptance of death and impermanence; and Shinto reverence for the sacred and the warrior's spiritual purification.
- Prescribed aesthetic refinement alongside martial skill; the ideal samurai practiced calligraphy, poetry, and tea ceremony as expressions of inner cultivation and spiritual discipline.
- Valued decisiveness, emotional restraint, and stoicism; fear and hesitation were seen as dishonorable; death in service was preferable to living with shame.
- Decline began with the Meiji Restoration (1868), which abolished the samurai class and modernized Japan's military; bushido survived as a cultural ideal and influenced modern Japanese nationalism.
- Invoked (and distorted) during World War II to justify kamikaze pilots and militaristic expansionism; postwar interpretations have emphasized its philosophical and ethical dimensions.
