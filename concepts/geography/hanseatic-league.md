## summary

The **Hanseatic League** was a confederation of merchant guilds and market towns spanning the Baltic Sea, the North Sea, and inland river systems (particularly the Elbe and Rhine) from the 13th to 17th centuries. At its height (14th–15th centuries), it encompassed nearly 200 cities, from London to Novgorod, and functioned as an economic network binding northern Europe through standardized weights, measures, legal codes, and trading privileges. The League's geography—connecting Scandinavia, Russia, the Low Countries, and Central Europe—made it a crucial economic institution that rivaled kingdoms in power and shaped the commercial networks that would eventually underpin the modern European economy.

## you gotta know

- Originated in the 13th century as informal association of merchant communities in Baltic and North Sea coastal towns, gradually formalizing into a collective trading network with shared interests.
- Geography centered on the Baltic and North Sea littoral: major cities like *Lübeck*, *Hamburg*, *Gdańsk* (then Danzig), and *Bergen* (Norway) were its nodes, connected by sea routes and inland rivers.
- The League established warehouses (*kontore*) in foreign cities—London, Novgorod, Bergen, and Bruges—as enclaves where Hanseatic merchants monopolized trade in certain goods (timber, furs, grain, fish, amber).
- Communicated through standardized legal codes, weights, and measures, making Hanseatic commercial law and standards influential across northern Europe and a predecessor to modern commercial standardization.
- Used collective economic power to negotiate with kings and princes: guaranteed trading rights, established market monopolies, and defended against piracy through merchant-organized fleets.
- Declined in the 16th–17th centuries as nation-states centralized power, oceanic trade routes (Atlantic, Indian Ocean) overshadowed Baltic commerce, and rival trading powers (English, Dutch, Swedish) emerged.
