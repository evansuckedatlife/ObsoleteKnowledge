## summary

**Prohibition** was the period from 1920 to 1933 when the United States banned the production, importation, transportation, and sale of alcoholic beverages, enforced by the **Volstead Act**. Intended by reformers as moral and public-health legislation, Prohibition instead created a vast underground economy, fueled organized crime, and ultimately failed due to lack of public support and the vast gap between law and popular practice. Its repeal via the 21st Amendment stands as the only time the U.S. Constitution reversed a previous amendment, marking a pivotal moment in American political culture and the limits of federal regulatory power.

## you gotta know

- Prohibition was championed by progressive reformers, religious groups, and women's organizations as a solution to alcoholism, poverty, and violence; it passed with overwhelming congressional support after decades of advocacy by the Anti-Saloon League and temperance movements.
- The 18th Amendment (ratified 1919) and the Volstead Act established Prohibition; enforcement fell to a vastly underfunded federal Prohibition Bureau with only 1,500 agents attempting to police a nation of 100+ million, making effective enforcement essentially impossible.
- Rather than eliminating alcohol consumption, Prohibition created speakeasies (illegal bars), bootleg production, smuggling networks, and criminal syndicates; organized crime figures like Al Capone became enormously profitable and powerful, gaining social and political influence.
- The "Noble Experiment" disproportionately affected the poor and working class, who consumed illegally produced and often dangerous liquor, while the wealthy accessed smuggled quality products through private clubs and personal connections to bootleggers.
- Prohibition overlapped with and energized the Harlem Renaissance and the Jazz Age; speakeasies became centers of jazz music, African American culture, and social transgression in Northern cities, driving nightlife and cultural innovation during the 1920s.
- The apparent hypocrisy of Prohibition—widespread law-breaking sanctioned by the government's own unrealistic law—undermined faith in government institutions and contributed to widespread moral relativism that persisted through the Depression and beyond.
- Repeal came through the 21st Amendment (ratified 1933) after years of growing opposition; the failure of Prohibition became a cautionary lesson about the limits of federal regulatory power over deeply entrenched social practices.

## connections

- [[jazz-age]] — Prohibition's underground alcohol culture became intertwined with jazz clubs, speakeasies, and the cultural explosion of the era.
- [[roaring-twenties]] — Prohibition defined the decade's contradictions: official morality versus underground hedonism, enforcement failure versus commercial ingenuity.
- [[united-states]] — Prohibition was a distinctly American experiment and a defining moment in U.S. legal and social history.
- [[organized-crime]] — criminal organizations became wealthy and powerful during Prohibition, reshaping American organized crime's structure and reach.
- [[women-s-suffrage]] — women's organizations were instrumental in driving both Prohibition and suffrage reforms in the early 20th century.
