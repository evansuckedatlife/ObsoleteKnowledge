## summary

**Biodiversity**, or *biological diversity*, denotes the comprehensive variety of life across genetic, species, and ecosystem levels that sustains the stability and function of the biosphere. Coined as a contracted term in 1985 and popularized during the 1986 National Forum on BioDiversity, the concept is the foundational cornerstone of biogeography, ecological science, and modern global conservation strategy. Global patterns of living organisms reveal profound geographic unevenness, governed by the latitudinal diversity gradient where species richness reaches its maximum in tropical biomes such as [[south-america]] before diminishing toward the poles. Preserving this ecological wealth is crucial for maintaining planetary resilience, regulating biogeochemical cycles, and protecting natural ecosystems against unprecedented anthropogenic change and habitat degradation.

## you gotta know

- Entomologist *E. O. Wilson* popularized the concept through his landmark 1988 volume *BioDiversity*, following the coining of the term by *Walter G. Rosen* during planning for a 1986 National Academy of Sciences forum.
- Biologists analyze the concept across three hierarchical tiers: genetic diversity governed by variation in [[dna]], species diversity reflecting community richness and evenness, and ecosystem diversity encompassing distinct habitats and landscapes.
- The *latitudinal diversity gradient* represents the planet's most prominent biogeographic distribution pattern, wherein species richness consistently peaks in the equatorial tropics of [[south-america]] and declines steadily toward higher latitudes.
- British ecologist *Norman Myers* introduced the framework of *biodiversity hotspots* in 1988, designating regions that contain at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species and have lost at least 70 percent of their primary vegetation, exemplified by the [[andes]] and the rainforests of [[southeast-asia]].
- Ecologists quantify biological variety using statistical indices such as the *Shannon-Wiener index* and *Simpson's diversity index*, which mathematically integrate species abundance, richness, and evenness across sampled communities.
- The equilibrium theory of *island biogeography*, formulated by *Robert MacArthur* and *E. O. Wilson*, models species richness on isolated landmasses as a dynamic balance between immigration rates and extinction rates dictated by land area and isolation.
- Prolonged geographical isolation has produced extraordinary continental endemism, notably in [[australia]], where over 80 percent of terrestrial mammals, flowering plants, and reptiles are found nowhere else on the planet.
- Modern anthropogenic declines are widely recognized as the *Sixth Mass Extinction*, accelerated since the [[industrial-revolution]] by drivers *Wilson* summarized under the acronym *HIPPO*: habitat destruction, invasive species, pollution, human population expansion, and overharvesting.

## connections

- [[south-america]] — continent containing the world's greatest concentration of terrestrial species within the Amazon basin.
- [[andes]] — mountain system harboring the Tropical Andes, recognized as the most species-rich biodiversity hotspot on Earth.
- [[australia]] — isolated landmass that evolved exceptionally high rates of endemic biodiversity across marsupial and floral lineages.
- [[southeast-asia]] — archipelago and peninsula region hosting the Sundaland and Wallacea biodiversity hotspots separated by the Wallace Line.
- [[dna]] — the molecular foundation of genetic diversity that enables natural selection and evolutionary adaptation.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — historical inflection point that drastically accelerated environmental degradation, landscape fragmentation, and extinction rates worldwide.
- [[enlightenment]] — philosophical and scientific era that established formal botanical taxonomy and comparative biogeographical surveys under *Carl Linnaeus* and *Alexander von Humboldt*.
