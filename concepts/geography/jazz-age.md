## summary

The **Jazz Age** was a cultural epoch of the 1920s centered in New York City—particularly Harlem—marked by jazz music, artistic innovation, and social liberation following World War I. The era embodied a youthful rebellion against Victorian propriety, embraced African American artistic expression on a national stage, and coincided with economic prosperity, Prohibition, and changing attitudes toward sexuality and gender. The Jazz Age left an indelible mark on American literature, music, and visual culture, defining a generation's sensibility and ambitions.

## you gotta know

- Centered in 1920s Harlem and lower Manhattan; speakeasies, cabarets, and dance halls provided venues where jazz music flourished despite Prohibition (1920–1933).
- Harlem Renaissance occurred within the Jazz Age; Black musicians like Duke Ellington, Louis Armstrong, and Bessie Smith achieved prominence and cross-racial audiences.
- The flappers—young women who smoked, drank, danced, and challenged traditional gender roles—became the cultural symbol of generational rebellion and modernity.
- F. Scott Fitzgerald captured the era's glamour and moral emptiness in novels like *The Great Gatsby* (1925), while Langston Hughes gave voice to the African American experience.
- Represented a geographic concentration: New York's cultural dominance meant that artistic movements in Harlem and Greenwich Village resonated nationally, then globally.
- Ended abruptly with the Stock Market Crash of 1929 and the Great Depression; the era's excess and optimism gave way to economic hardship and social retrenchment.
- The Cotton Club and other Harlem venues embodied the era's racial paradox: Black artists performed for predominantly white audiences in segregated spaces, highlighting both achievement and systemic racism.
- Prohibition (1920–1933) created speakeasies and bootlegging industries; organized crime expanded dramatically, including figures like Al Capone, whose activities intertwined with cultural defiance.
- The era's technological innovations—radio, automobiles, phonographs—enabled mass consumption of jazz and fast-paced urban culture, spreading the Jazz Age sensibility nationwide.
- Writers like Ernest Hemingway, Gertrude Stein, and Langston Hughes chronicled the era; the Lost Generation aesthetic of disillusionment after WWI permeated 1920s artistic output.
- The Jazz Age's racial and sexual liberation was limited: segregation persisted despite Black artistic prominence, and women's freedoms, while expanded, remained constrained by law and social expectation.
