## summary

**Medieval trade** refers to the networks of commerce that connected European, Asian, and African markets from roughly the 5th to the 15th centuries, driven by demand for luxury goods, spices, and raw materials. Medieval merchants developed sophisticated credit systems, organized trading guilds, and established sea and land routes—most famously the Silk Road—that linked distant civilizations. Trade was both a driver of cultural exchange and a source of wealth inequality, fueling the rise of merchant classes and merchant republics like Venice and the emergence of powerful trading confederations like the Hanseatic League.

## you gotta know

- Medieval trade routes connected the Mediterranean, Baltic, and Indian Ocean worlds through overland Silk Road caravan networks and maritime routes; the Silk Road brought Chinese silks, spices from Southeast Asia, and Islamic textiles westward into European markets.
- The Hanseatic League (13th–17th centuries) unified Northern European merchants into a powerful confederation that dominated Baltic and North Sea trade, establishing a network of fortified trading posts (kontors) across Scandinavia, the Baltic, and northern Russia.
- Venice and Genoa became wealthy merchant republics by controlling Mediterranean trade routes to the Islamic world and beyond; Venetian traders enjoyed special commercial privileges in Constantinople and other Byzantine ports, accumulating immense wealth and political power.
- Medieval trade goods included spices (pepper, nutmeg, cloves), silk, furs, amber, tin, wool, and wine; these luxury items were often more valuable than their weight in gold and drove exploration, risk-taking, and the development of complex merchant networks.
- Medieval fairs—particularly those in Champagne and Flanders—became seasonal meeting points where merchants from across Europe exchanged goods, developed credit instruments and letters of exchange, and negotiated commercial law and tariffs, enabling transactions across vast distances.
- The Islamic world served as the crucial intermediary for medieval European trade with Asia; Arab and Persian merchants controlled the routes through the Levant and Indian Ocean, profiting enormously from European demand for Eastern goods and using credit systems sophisticated for their time.
- Medieval trade stimulated urban growth, guild formation, and the rise of a merchant class distinct from feudal nobility; banking families like the Medici emerged from trade wealth and eventually dominated European finance and politics.
