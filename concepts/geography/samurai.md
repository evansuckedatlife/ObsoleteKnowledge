## summary

The **samurai** were the hereditary warrior class of feudal Japan, bound by a strict code of honor called *Bushidō*. From their rise in the medieval period through their formal abolition in the 19th century, samurai embodied Japanese martial ideals, aesthetic refinement, and personal loyalty to their feudal lords. Their cultural legacy—deeply embedded in Japanese art, literature, and philosophy—remains a defining symbol of Japan's pre-modern identity.

## you gotta know

- Emerged during the Heian period as militarized landowning elites; by the Edo period (1603–1868), formed a hereditary warrior class of about 5–6% of the population with strict social codes.
- Followed *Bushidō*, a code emphasizing loyalty to one's lord (*daimyo*), martial skill, personal honor, and aesthetic refinement including poetry and calligraphy; defined their entire worldview.
- Armed with the katana (curved sword), bow, and later firearms; trained in both combat and the arts to embody the warrior-scholar ideal perfectly.
- During the Edo period, samurai were politically subordinated but culturally celebrated; they served as administrators, artists, teachers, and scholars rather than active soldiers.
- Formally abolished during the Meiji Restoration (1868) when Japan modernized and created a centralized, Western-style military; nearly 270 years of warrior tradition ended abruptly.
- Featured prominently in Japanese literature, theater (*Noh* and *Kabuki*), and visual arts; their image became inseparable from Japanese cultural identity and national consciousness.
- Retained cultural significance after abolition; writers like Mishima Yukio explored samurai ideals in the modern era, complicating their legacy and meaning.
