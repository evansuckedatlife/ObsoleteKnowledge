# Trade routes

## summary

**Trade routes** are established pathways—whether overland, maritime, or riverine—through which merchants and goods move across regions, connecting distant peoples and economies. From the *Silk Road* spanning Central Asia to oceanic routes linking Europe and Asia, trade routes have been the conduits for not merely commerce but also ideas, religions, technologies, and cultures across centuries. They shape geopolitical boundaries, urban development, and the rise and fall of civilizations.

## you gotta know

- The *Silk Road* was not a single path but a network of overland and maritime routes linking China with Central Asia, Persia, and ultimately Europe and Africa from roughly the 2nd century BCE onward.
- Medieval Mediterranean trade was dominated by Italian city-states like *Venice* and *Genoa*, which controlled routes to the *Middle East* and profited enormously from Eastern luxuries.
- River routes (the *Danube*, *Nile*, *Yangtze*) historically served as internal trade corridors, connecting inland regions to ports and maritime networks.
- The *spice trade* from Southeast Asia drove European exploration and colonization in the 15th–17th centuries, as spices were rare, valuable, and used for food preservation.
- Monsoon winds and ocean currents dictated the timing and seasonality of maritime routes; the *Indian Ocean* became the nexus of trade after technologies improved.
- The *Hanseatic League* monopolized Baltic and Northern European trade via river and coastal routes, establishing merchant colonies and standardizing commercial practices.
- Modern trade routes reflect ancient patterns: major ports like *Rotterdam*, *Singapore*, and *Shanghai* occupy positions where geography and history intersect.
