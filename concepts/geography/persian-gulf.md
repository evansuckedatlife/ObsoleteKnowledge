## summary

The **Persian Gulf** is a strategic body of water in the Middle East, bounded by Iran to the east and a string of Arab states—Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Bahrain, and Kuwait—to the west. It connects to the Arabian Sea through the Strait of Hormuz, one of the world's most critical chokepoints for global energy supply. The gulf's vast oil reserves and geopolitical importance have made it a region of persistent international tension, conflict, and great-power competition.

## you gotta know

- The Persian Gulf contains approximately one-third of the world's proven oil reserves and one-quarter of its natural gas reserves, making it indispensable to global energy security; dependence on gulf oil drives geopolitical competition and military presence.
- The Strait of Hormuz, the only sea passage out of the gulf to the world's oceans, is only 21 miles wide at its narrowest point and carries roughly one-third of all seaborne traded oil globally; control of this chokepoint grants enormous geopolitical leverage.
- Ancient Mesopotamian and Persian civilizations flourished along the gulf's shores; the region is home to Sumer, Babylon, and Persian empires, and served as a crucial crossroads of maritime and overland trade routes connecting Asia, Africa, and Europe.
- The modern oil industry in the Gulf began with exploration in the early 20th century; the Anglo-Persian Oil Company (later BP) and American companies discovered vast reserves, fundamentally reshaping Middle Eastern politics, wealth distribution, and international relations.
- The discovery of oil enriched Gulf emirates and Saudi Arabia, creating petrostates with vast wealth but limited economic diversification; oil revenues funded state-building but also dependency on commodity exports and Western technological and military partnerships.
- The Gulf War (1990–1991) demonstrated the strategic importance of the Persian Gulf when Iraq's invasion of Kuwait triggered a massive international military response led by the United States; the conflict showed the cost of Gulf instability for global oil markets.
- Iran and Arab Gulf states (particularly Saudi Arabia) have engaged in persistent rivalry for regional dominance, with territorial disputes, naval confrontations, and proxy conflicts in Syria, Yemen, and Iraq; the 2015 nuclear deal with Iran attempted to manage these tensions.
