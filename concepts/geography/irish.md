## summary

**Irish** (also called **Gaeilge**) is a Celtic language native to Ireland, part of the Goidelic language family alongside Scottish Gaelic and Manx. Once the dominant tongue across the island, it was systematically suppressed under English colonial rule but has experienced gradual revival through education and cultural nationalism. Today it remains an official language of the Irish state and serves as a symbol of Irish identity and cultural resistance to colonization.

## you gotta know

- Irish is one of the oldest written languages in Europe, with records dating back to the 4th century in ogham script and medieval manuscripts like the Book of Kells inscribed in beautiful Gaelic calligraphy, preserving Celtic culture through the centuries.
- Under British rule, particularly during the 18th and 19th centuries, English was systematically imposed as the language of education and administration through the Penal Laws, causing Irish to decline sharply among the native population and become increasingly confined to rural areas and the western coast.
- The Great Famine (1845–1852) devastated Irish-speaking communities disproportionately, as Gaelic speakers were concentrated in rural western regions most affected by crop failure; disease and emigration further depleted the language's speaker base.
- The Gaelic Revival movement of the late 19th century sought to revive the language as a nationalist symbol and assertion of Irish cultural independence, leading to its reestablishment in schools and public life after Irish independence in 1922.
- The modern Irish language exists in different dialects across the three major Gaeltacht (Irish-speaking) regions: Connemara in the west, the Aran Islands offshore, and Donegal in the northwest, each with distinct pronunciation and vocabulary features.
- Irish grammar retains archaic features of the broader Celtic language family, including vowel mutations, a system of initial consonant changes called lenition, and a verb-subject-object word order distinct from English and continental European languages.
- The Irish language acts (1922 onward) made Irish an official language and required its teaching in all schools, though the rate of fluent speakers remains a persistent policy concern and source of cultural nationalism debate in modern Ireland and among diaspora communities.
