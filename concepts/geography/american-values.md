## summary

**American Values**, historically synthesized as the **American Creed**, refer to the core political, social, and philosophical ideals that define the national identity of the [[united-states]]. Originating during the 18th-century [[enlightenment]] and forged through the [[american-revolution]], these principles fundamentally center on liberty, egalitarianism, individualism, republicanism, democracy, and the rule of law. Codified in foundational documents like the *Declaration of Independence* and the [[united-states-constitution]], they provide a unifying civic framework for an exceptionally diverse nation. In quiz bowl and American studies, this ethos is essential for understanding ideological debates, constitutional jurisprudence, and recurring reform movements across United States history.

## you gotta know

- Articulated famously in the 1776 *Declaration of Independence* by *Thomas Jefferson*, who asserted as self-evident truths that all men are created equal and endowed with unalienable rights to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.
- Codified into organic law through the [[united-states-constitution]] and the *Bill of Rights*, establishing constitutionalism, federalism, separation of powers, and protections for individual civil liberties against state overreach.
- Analyzed extensively by French political philosopher *Alexis de Tocqueville* in his landmark 1835 treatise *Democracy in America*, which identified equality of conditions, voluntary associations, religion, and individualism as defining characteristics of the young republic.
- Tested and redefined during the [[american-civil-war]] and [[reconstruction-era]], where *Abraham Lincoln* framed the preservation of the Union and the abolition of slavery as a rebirth of freedom dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal.
- Invoked as moral leverage by the [[civil-rights-movement]], notably in *Martin Luther King Jr.*'s 1963 *I Have a Dream* speech, which demanded that the nation live out the true meaning of its founding creed and cash its promissory note.
- Conceptualized formally as the *American Creed* by Swedish sociologist *Gunnar Myrdal* in his influential 1944 study *An American Dilemma*, which highlighted the agonizing moral contradiction between egalitarian democratic ideals and systemic racial subjugation.
- Synthesized in modern political sociology by *Seymour Martin Lipset* in *American Exceptionalism*, identifying a five-part value cluster of liberty, egalitarianism, individualism, populism, and laissez-faire capitalism that distinguishes American political culture from European social democracy.
- Examined by sociologist *Robert Bellah* in his 1967 essay *Civil Religion in America*, which argued that public rituals, inaugural addresses, and national monuments constitute an institutionalized, non-sectarian civic faith binding the populace together.

## connections

- [[united-states]] — the federal republic whose national identity and civic culture are defined by these principles.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — the supreme legal framework that codifies these ideals into institutional governance and rights protections.
- [[enlightenment]] — the European intellectual movement whose ideas on social contracts and natural rights provided the philosophical bedrock for American republicanism.
- [[american-revolution]] — the war of independence fought to establish self-governance and vindicate the rights articulated in the founding creed.
- [[american-civil-war]] — the existential conflict that tested whether a nation conceived in liberty could endure, resulting in the destruction of slavery.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — the 20th-century crusade that pressured the nation to fulfill its foundational promises of equality and constitutional protection.
- [[cold-war]] — the geopolitical confrontation in which the United States projected its civic values of liberal democracy and capitalism against Soviet communism.
