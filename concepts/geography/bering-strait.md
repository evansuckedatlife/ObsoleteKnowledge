## summary

The **Bering Strait** is a narrow waterway (roughly 53 miles at its narrowest point) separating *Alaska* from eastern *Russia*, connecting the *Arctic Ocean* to the *Bering Sea*. Despite its modest width, the strait represents one of geography's most significant boundaries—a barrier between two continents, two nations, and historically, two hemispheres of human civilization. The strait holds profound significance for both indigenous Arctic peoples and theories of American prehistory, and continues to shape modern geopolitical contests.

## you gotta know

- Named after Danish explorer Vitus Bering, who explored the region in the 1720s for the Russian imperial navy and first mapped its strategic importance.
- The strait marks the international date line and the international border between Russia and the United States (Alaska).
- At its narrowest, the Big Diomede Island (Russia) and Little Diomede Island (USA) are only about 2.4 miles apart—close enough to see across.
- During the last ice age (when sea levels were dramatically lower), the Bering land bridge (*Beringia*) connected Asia and North America, enabling human migration into the Americas.
- Indigenous peoples on both sides—Siberian Yupik, Iñupiat (Alaska), and Chukchi (Russia)—developed sophisticated maritime cultures hunting whale and seal despite Arctic conditions.
- The strait was never heavily traversed by ships until the modern era due to extreme cold, ice, and storms; navigation remains dangerous and seasonal.
- The region's geopolitical importance has escalated with Arctic ice retreat from climate change, opening new shipping routes and territorial claims.
- Whaling and fur trade historically drove Russian expansion into the region; control shifted when Russia sold Alaska to the United States (1867).
