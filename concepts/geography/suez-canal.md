# Suez Canal

## summary

The **Suez Canal** is an artificial waterway linking the *Mediterranean Sea* to the *Red Sea*, running through *Egypt* and eliminating the need for ships to navigate around Africa. Opened in 1869 and controlled by *Britain* and later *Egypt*, it became one of the world's most strategically vital shipping routes, handling roughly 12% of global maritime trade and connecting Europe, Asia, and Africa directly.

## you gotta know

- The canal spans roughly 193 kilometers through the *Sinai Peninsula* and *Egyptian* territory, connecting *Port Said* on the Mediterranean to *Port Suez* on the Red Sea.
- *Ferdinand de Lesseps*, a French diplomat and engineer, conceived and directed the canal's construction (1859–1869) using predominantly *Egyptian* labor; the project cost enormous resources and Egyptian lives.
- The canal shortened shipping routes from Europe to Asia dramatically, reducing transit times from months (around Africa) to days and shifting global trade patterns profoundly.
- *Britain* secured financial control and eventual political dominance of the canal following construction, positioning it as guardian of the route and a guarantor of access; this strategic dominance underpinned British naval supremacy.
- The *Suez Crisis* of 1956 erupted when *Egyptian* President *Gamal Abdel Nasser* nationalized the canal in response to Western pressure; *Britain*, *France*, and *Israel* invaded militarily but were forced to withdraw by U.S. and Soviet pressure.
- *Egypt* has controlled the canal since 1956, with traffic increasing dramatically; it is a crucial source of revenue and strategic leverage for *Egypt* in regional politics.
- The canal remains one of the world's busiest shipping lanes, handling roughly 13,000 vessels annually and approximately 30% of global containerized cargo; any closure (even temporary, as with the 2021 *Ever Given* grounding) has worldwide economic impact.
