# Eucalyptus

## summary

**Eucalyptus** is a genus of trees native to Australia that dominates the continent's landscape and ecology. Over 600 species of eucalyptus—ranging from shrubs to giants over 300 feet tall—are uniquely adapted to Australia's arid, fire-prone climate through waxy leaves, deep root systems, and the ability to resprout after fire. The trees are so emblematic of Australia that they appear in its national symbolism, yet their introduction to other continents has sometimes proven ecologically invasive.

## you gotta know

- Leaves contain aromatic oils; the genus name derives from the Greek for "well-hidden," referring to the flower's hidden stamens, not the leaves' scent.
- Perfectly adapted to drought and fire: leaves have reduced surface area and waxy coating to minimize water loss; many species release seeds only after fires, regenerating in the ash.
- The koala is almost exclusively dependent on eucalyptus leaves for food, and its digestive system is uniquely adapted to process their toxins.
- Aboriginal Australians used eucalyptus for food (lerp insects on branches), wood, and medicines for thousands of years before European settlement.
- Planted worldwide in the 19th and 20th centuries for timber, pulp, and ornament; in some regions (Mediterranean, California) it has become invasive and alters fire regimes.
- Fast-growing and efficient at water uptake; sometimes planted to drain swamps, but in dry regions it can deplete groundwater.
- The stringybark, ironbark, and snow gum are among the most commercially important species.
- Survives in poor soil where other trees fail, making it ecologically dominant across Australia's interior.
