# Celtic languages

## summary

The **Celtic languages** are an ancient Indo-European language family spoken across the British Isles, Brittany, and the Iberian Peninsula. Once dominant across much of Europe, they were displaced by Latin and Germanic languages; today, a handful of survivors (Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Welsh, Breton) persist largely through cultural revival and legislative protection. Celtic languages encode the identity and heritage of Celtic peoples and remain at the center of cultural nationalism in *Ireland* and *Scotland*.

## you gotta know

- Celtic languages split into two main branches: *Insular* (Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and now-extinct Pictish) and *Brythonic* (Welsh, Breton, and now-extinct Cornish).
- *Irish* (Gaeilge) is an official language of the Republic of Ireland with roughly 500,000 speakers; compulsory in schools but often spoken as a second language.
- *Welsh* is the most robust Celtic language with roughly 900,000 speakers (30% of Wales' population); Welsh-medium education and media have sustained it more effectively than other Celtic languages.
- *Scottish Gaelic* (Gàidhlig) has roughly 57,000 speakers, concentrated in the Outer Hebrides; it was suppressed after the 1746 Jacobite defeat but has seen modest revival.
- *Breton* (Brezhoneg) is spoken by roughly 200,000 people in Brittany, *France*; it is a distinct branch and has no official status in France, complicating preservation efforts.
- Linguistically, Celtic languages are SVO (subject-verb-object) and undergo initial consonant mutation (lenition), features distinctive within Indo-European.
- Language revival movements in Ireland, Scotland, and Wales have restored limited functionality to Celtic languages through education, media, and cultural activism, though they remain endangered.
