# The Great Gatsby

## summary

**The Great Gatsby** (1925), by F. Scott Fitzgerald, is a novel set in the summer of 1922 on Long Island, New York, during the Jazz Age. It chronicles the obsessive pursuit of wealth and romantic illusion through the story of the mysterious millionaire *Jay Gatsby*, his lost love *Daisy Buchanan*, and the narrator *Nick Carraway*. Beneath its glittering surface lies a critique of the American dream itself—the moral emptiness beneath material abundance, the impossibility of recapturing the past, and the collision between illusion and reality.

## you gotta know

- Gatsby, born James Gatz, reinvents himself as a wealthy man through criminal enterprise (bootlegging during Prohibition) to win back his childhood sweetheart, Daisy.
- Daisy, married to the brutish Tom Buchanan, is alluring but ultimately shallow and cowardly; she represents the wealth Gatsby has obtained but also his self-deception.
- Nick Carraway, the narrator, is a bond dealer and the moral observer; he's fascinated by Gatsby's intensity but horrified by the carelessness of the wealthy.
- The green light at the end of Daisy's dock is the symbol of Gatsby's longing—forever out of reach, receding as he moves toward it.
- Gatsby's lavish parties are the novel's vivid set pieces, filled with carousing crowds of the rich and famous, yet they underscore Gatsby's isolation and the hollowness of Jazz Age excess.
- The novel's climax involves a car accident where Gatsby's car (driven by Daisy) kills Tom's mistress *Myrtle Wilson*; Gatsby takes the blame to protect Daisy, and is then shot by Myrtle's grieving husband.
- Nick's closing meditation—"So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past"—crystallized the novel's theme of unfulfilled longing and the tragedy of American aspiration.

## connections

- [[new-york-city]] — the geographic and social landscape; Long Island and Manhattan symbolize both wealth and moral corruption.
- [[american-dream]] — the novel's central theme; the pursuit of self-made wealth and its inevitable disillusionment.
