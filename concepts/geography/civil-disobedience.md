# Civil disobedience

## summary

**Civil disobedience** is the deliberate violation of laws deemed unjust, coupled with willingness to accept legal punishment, as a method of political protest and social change. Rooted philosophically in *Enlightenment* thought but crystallized as a mass strategy by *Mahatma Gandhi* in *India* and adopted by *Martin Luther King Jr.* in the American *Civil Rights Movement*, it has become the defining nonviolent tactic of modern social movements. By accepting arrest and suffering, practitioners make the injustice of the law viscerally visible to the public.

## you gotta know

- *Thoreau* articulated civil disobedience philosophically in his 1849 essay, arguing that unjust laws need not be obeyed; the essay influenced later practitioners globally.
- *Mahatma Gandhi* deployed *satyagraha* ("truth-force"), a Sanskrit term for nonviolent resistance, during India's independence struggle (1920s–1940s), making it a mass movement.
- The *Salt March* (1930) exemplified Gandhian civil disobedience: Gandhi and followers marched 240 miles to the sea to illegally make salt, defying British monopoly and inspiring mass arrests and resistance.
- *Martin Luther King Jr.* adapted Gandhian tactics for the American *Civil Rights Movement* (1950s–1960s), using sit-ins, marches, and boycotts to challenge *Jim Crow* segregation.
- *Emmeline Pankhurst* pioneered strategic civil disobedience for women's suffrage in Britain (1900s–1920s), using property destruction and symbolic arrests to amplify the suffragettes' cause.
- Effectiveness is contested: civil disobedience has contributed to major social changes (independence, voting rights, desegregation) but also relies on specific conditions (public sympathy, media attention, opponent restraint).
- Modern variants include boycotts, sit-ins, strikes, and climate activism; the tactic remains central to social movements from anti-apartheid campaigns to LGBTQ+ rights.
