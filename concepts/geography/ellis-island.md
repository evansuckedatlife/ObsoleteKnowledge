## summary

**Ellis Island** is a small island in Upper New York Harbor, just off the New Jersey shore near the Statue of Liberty, that served as the primary immigration reception station for the United States from 1892 to 1954. Processing over 12 million arrivals—roughly 40 percent of all Americans living today have an ancestor who entered through Ellis Island—it became the symbolic gateway to American opportunity and the physical threshold of naturalization. Its geography, situated at the harbor's eastern edge and owned by the federal government, made it the ideal location for immigration bureaucracy.

## you gotta know

- Located in Upper New York Harbor, approximately 1,300 feet from the New Jersey coast, yet under federal (not state) jurisdiction, making it the venue for centralized immigration processing.
- The original Oyster Island (its historical name) was expanded through landfill in the 1890s to accommodate the massive immigration station built to replace the crowded Battery Park immigration depot in Manhattan.
- Peak arrival years saw 5,000–10,000 immigrants processed daily; the island handled the wave of Southern and Eastern European migration (1890–1920) that transformed American demographics.
- New arrivals underwent health inspections (including examinations for "idiocy," tuberculosis, and other conditions) and background checks; those deemed unfit were deported on return ships.
- Symbolically linked to the *Statue of Liberty*, visible on the island's west side, creating a composite icon of American immigration and freedom for arriving populations.
- Officially closed in 1954; later abandoned and deteriorated until restoration efforts in the 1980s–1990s converted it into a museum of immigration history.
