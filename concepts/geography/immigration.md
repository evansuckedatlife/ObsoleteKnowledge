# Immigration

## summary

**Immigration** to the United States—the mass arrival of millions of people seeking economic opportunity, political freedom, and escape from hardship—transformed America from a nation of 4 million (1790) to over 300 million by the late 20th century. Between 1840 and 1920, roughly 35 million people entered the United States, predominantly from Europe; subsequent waves brought Asians, Latin Americans, and Africans. Ellis Island (1892–1954) became the symbolic gateway, processing over 12 million immigrants; the phenomenon reshaped American culture, labor markets, and politics while generating persistent nativism and legal restriction.

## you gotta know

- Early waves (1840s–1880s) were predominantly Irish and German, fleeing famine and political upheaval; they faced discrimination but assimilated within two generations.
- The "new immigration" (1880s–1924) brought Italians, Poles, Jews, and others from southern and eastern Europe; they clustered in industrial cities, maintaining ethnic communities and languages longer than northern Europeans.
- Anti-Chinese sentiment led to the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882), the first explicit U.S. law barring an ethnic group; similar exclusions followed.
- Ellis Island opened as the immigration processing station in 1892; over 70% of all U.S. immigrants passed through it; the *Statue of Liberty*, built earlier, became its symbol.
- Most immigrants were economic migrants seeking factory work, land ownership, or better conditions; push factors included industrialization, land scarcity, and pogroms in Europe and Asia.
- Nativism—the fear that immigrants threatened American culture and wages—drove the Immigration Act of 1924, which imposed strict national-origin quotas favoring northern Europeans and cutting immigration to a trickle.
- The quota system lasted until 1965; subsequent immigration has been more diverse globally and legalized through specialized categories (work, family, refugee).
- Immigration remains central to American self-mythology (the nation of immigrants) despite legal restrictions; debates over immigration define contemporary politics.

- [[statue-of-liberty]] — the symbol of immigration and freedom, greeting arriving ships in New York Harbor.
- [[united-states]] — the destination nation whose economic opportunity and political ideals attracted immigrants.
- [[new-york-city]] — the primary arrival point and home to the largest concentrations of immigrant communities.
- [[american-dream]] — the aspiration that drove immigration; the belief that hard work could yield prosperity and social mobility.
- [[nativism]] — the anti-immigrant backlash, rooted in economic anxiety and cultural fear, that shaped immigration policy.
