## summary

The **American Dream** is the belief that anyone, regardless of background or class, can achieve prosperity and success through hard work and determination in the United States. This ideological concept emerged from America's founding narratives of opportunity and self-determination, becoming a defining cultural aspiration by the 19th century. It represents both the promise of upward social mobility and the profound anxieties surrounding whether that promise remains attainable in an increasingly stratified society. The dream remains deeply embedded in American culture, even as its achievability faces mounting challenges and skepticism.

## you gotta know

- Rooted in the Enlightenment ideals of equality and individual merit, promising that birth or circumstance need not limit achievement; this notion was revolutionary in a feudal world.
- Gained momentum during the 19th century as immigration surged; the promise of opportunity drew millions seeking to escape rigid class hierarchies abroad, particularly from Europe.
- Reflects capitalist ideology: the belief that free markets and entrepreneurship reward talent rather than privilege or inheritance, though this assumption proved false for many.
- Often tied to westward expansion and frontier mythology—the notion that unsettled land represented limitless possibility; the frontier was imagined as a safety valve for social discontent.
- Challenged in literature and film throughout the 20th century; works like *The Great Gatsby* and *Death of a Salesman* interrogate whether the dream is achievable or illusory.
- Economically dependent on the ability to acquire land, capital, and education—resources not equally accessible to all groups historically; race and gender systematically blocked access for many.
- Underwent significant scrutiny following deindustrialization and wealth concentration in the late 20th century; contemporary scholars question whether upward mobility remains possible.
