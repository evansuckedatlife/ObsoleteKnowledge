## summary

**Ecology** is the scientific study of organisms in relationship to one another and to their physical environment. It examines how energy flows through systems, how populations compete and cooperate, and how entire landscapes organize themselves into self-sustaining or fragile communities. Emerging as a formal discipline in the late 19th century, ecology has become foundational to understanding climate change, extinction, and human impact on planetary systems.

## you gotta know

- The term derives from the Greek *oikos* (household) and *logos* (study); ecology treats nature as an interconnected household rather than a collection of separate organisms.
- *Darwin*'s *theory of evolution* provided the conceptual foundation for ecology, explaining why organisms are adapted to specific environmental niches and how competition drives adaptation.
- Ecosystems are characterized by energy flow (from sun through producers to consumers) and nutrient cycling; disruption at any level cascades through the system.
- Keystone species—organisms whose impact is disproportionate to their abundance—can determine ecosystem structure; their loss triggers collapse of dependent species.
- Succession describes how ecosystems change over time: pioneer species colonize bare ground, gradually building soil and complexity until mature ecosystems establish.
- Carrying capacity is the maximum population size an environment can sustain indefinitely; exceeding it triggers resource depletion, disease, and population crash.
- Modern ecology incorporates systems thinking: feedback loops, tipping points, and emergent properties that resist reduction to individual components.
- *Buckminster Fuller* articulated ecological principles in design and systems theory, arguing that efficiency and resource-consciousness must be central to technological civilization.
- Invasive species—organisms introduced beyond their native range—can devastate ecosystems by outcompeting natives and disrupting established relationships; cane toads in Australia exemplify how invasions can spiral beyond control.
- Trophic cascades demonstrate how the removal or recovery of top predators ripples through ecosystems; the reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone reorganized plant communities, stream dynamics, and entire food webs.
