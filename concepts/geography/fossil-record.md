## summary

The **fossil record** is the aggregate of all known *fossils*—preserved remains and traces of ancient organisms preserved in rock layers. It documents life's transformation over billions of years, providing the primary empirical foundation for *evolution* and revealing the rise and fall of species, ecological communities, and entire biomes. Though incomplete and geographically scattered, the fossil record demonstrates that life has continuously changed, that organisms are connected through descent, and that extinction is a natural process.

## you gotta know

- Fossils form under specific conditions: rapid burial (preventing decomposition), anoxic environments (inhibiting bacterial decay), and mineral replacement over geological time; fossils are therefore rare and biased toward organisms with hard parts (shells, bones, teeth).
- Radiometric dating of rocks encasing fossils allows assignment of absolute ages; the *fossil record* is organized into geological epochs and eras, each characterized by distinctive fauna and flora.
- The fossil record documents major evolutionary transitions: fish-tetrapod (with intermediate forms like *Tiktaalik*), reptile-bird, and land-mammal-whale, showing that seemingly distinct groups are linked by ancestors.
- Mass extinctions appear in the record as sudden drops in fossil diversity; five major extinction events are documented, each followed by ecological reorganization and evolutionary innovation in surviving lineages.
- *Transitional forms*—organisms with characteristics of two groups—are common in the fossil record; *Archaeopteryx* (feathered dinosaur) and *Homo floresiensis* (small human ancestor) exemplify intermediate stages.
- The record reveals *adaptation* in action: fossil horses show gradual increase in body size and tooth complexity over millions of years, correlating with climate and vegetation change.
- Gaps in the record are not evidence against evolution but rather reflect uneven preservation and unequal exploration; new discoveries constantly fill previously "missing links."
- Biostratigraphy—using distinctive fossils to correlate and date rock layers worldwide—emerged as a discipline in the 19th century and remains central to *paleontology*.
