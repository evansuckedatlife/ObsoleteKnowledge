## summary

The **Textile Industry** was the first sector to be industrialized during the 18th–19th centuries, transforming production from hand-loom cottage work to mechanized factory systems powered by steam engines. Centered initially in England, textile manufacturing became the engine of the *Industrial Revolution*, creating vast wealth, urbanization, and the modern working class while establishing the factory model for all subsequent industries. The industry's technological innovations and labor organization laid the essential blueprint for industrial capitalism and the modern factory system.

## you gotta know

- Manual textile production had been a cottage industry for centuries; automation began with inventions like the *Flying Shuttle* (1733) and *Spinning Jenny* (1764).
- *James Watt's* steam engine (1769) provided the power source that made large-scale mechanized production economical, multiplying output and destroying cottage-industry livelihoods overnight.
- *Manchester* became the world's textile capital, transforming from a market town of 25,000 (1772) to a sprawling industrial city of 300,000+ by mid-century.
- Cotton (imported from colonial plantations, particularly slave-grown American cotton) became the primary fiber; England's textile industry depended on colonial slavery and imperial trade.
- Factory systems concentrated previously dispersed workers into cities, creating massive social disruption: urban overcrowding, pollution, child labor, and 16-hour workdays became standard.
- The industry's profitability financed railroad construction, banking networks, and capital accumulation that funded further industrialization across all sectors.
- Mechanization created enormous wealth for manufacturers while impoverishing hand workers; this class conflict spawned early labor organization, working-class consciousness, and socialist thought.
- The industry's dominance was short-lived; by the late 19th century, other nations (especially the United States) had industrialized textile production more efficiently, shifting England's economy.
