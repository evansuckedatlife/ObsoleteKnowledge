## summary

A **rainforest** is a dense, biodiverse forest ecosystem characterized by heavy year-round rainfall, complex layered canopy structure, and extraordinary species richness, occurring primarily in tropical regions near the equator in Africa, Asia, and South America. Rainforests cover less than 6% of Earth's land surface yet contain over half of the planet's terrestrial species, functioning as both ecological engines driving nutrient cycling and climate regulators through evapotranspiration. They are critical to global oxygen production and carbon storage, yet remain under severe threat from deforestation, agricultural expansion, and climate change—representing one of humanity's most consequential environmental crises.

## you gotta know

- Rainforests receive at least 80 inches (2,000 mm) of rain annually and maintain warm, humid conditions year-round (tropical rainforests average 70-85°F), creating an ideal environment for rapid plant growth and extraordinary biodiversity.
- The forest structure consists of four distinct layers: the emergent layer (isolated trees rising above 200 feet), the dense canopy (100-150 feet high, blocking 95% of sunlight), the shaded understory, and the nutrient-rich forest floor, each supporting specialized ecosystems and organisms.
- Rainforest soils are surprisingly poor in nutrients despite the lush vegetation; the ecosystem's richness depends on rapid nutrient cycling where fallen plant matter quickly decomposes via fungi and is reabsorbed by shallow root systems before leaching removes minerals.
- Tropical rainforests are found in three main regions: the Amazon Basin in South America (55% of global rainforest), the Congo Basin in Central Africa, and Southeast Asia—together accounting for the vast majority of the world's remaining rainforest coverage.
- An estimated 80% of rainforest animal and plant species have never been scientifically studied; new species are discovered regularly, and indigenous communities hold vast traditional ecological knowledge accumulated over thousands of years of forest inhabitation.
- Rainforests produce approximately 20% of the world's oxygen and store massive amounts of carbon in biomass and soil, making their destruction a major contributor to climate change and global atmospheric CO2 concentrations.
- Rainforest deforestation (driven by logging, agriculture, cattle ranching, and development) occurs at catastrophic rates—roughly 137 species go extinct daily in rainforests—threatening both global climate stability and the survival of indigenous peoples.
