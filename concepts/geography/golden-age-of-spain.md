## summary

The **Golden Age of Spain**, or *Siglo de Oro* ("century of gold"), encompasses the 16th and 17th centuries when *Spain* achieved unprecedented power, wealth, and cultural brilliance. Fueled by New World gold and imperial expansion, Spanish kingdoms produced a dazzling literary and artistic efflorescence—the era of *Cervantes*, *Lope de Vega*, and *Velázquez*. Though political decline accelerated as the century progressed, the cultural legacy endures as one of history's finest achievements in literature, philosophy, and visual arts.

## you gotta know

- Politically, Spain unified under Catholic monarchs *Ferdinand* and *Isabella* (late 15th century), conquered Granada (ending Al-Andalus), sponsored *Columbus*, and built a transatlantic empire spanning the Americas, Philippines, and Mediterranean.
- Gold and silver from the *New World*—especially the Potosí mines—flooded Spanish treasuries, funding armies, architecture, and the patronage of the arts on an unprecedented scale.
- Literature flourished: *Miguel de Cervantes* wrote *Don Quixote* (1605), often called the first modern novel; *Lope de Vega* composed over 1,000 plays; poets like *Luis de Góngora* and *Francisco de Quevedo* experimented with baroque complexity.
- *Spanish mysticism* produced profound spiritual writers like *Saint Teresa of Ávila* and *Saint John of the Cross*, whose works remain touchstones of European spirituality and literature.
- Visual arts reached sublime heights: *El Greco* (Domenikos Theotokopoulos), a Cretan painter working in Toledo, created visionary religious canvases; *Diego Velázquez* produced court paintings of unprecedented psychological depth.
- The *Counter-Reformation* shaped Spanish culture profoundly; the Inquisition persecuted *Muslims*, *Jews*, and heterodox thinkers, driving intellectual conformity even as individual genius found expression in permitted forms.
- By the 17th century's end, Spanish military power had waned (defeats in the *Thirty Years' War*, loss of naval supremacy) and the economy declined, yet cultural production continued at high levels.
- The Golden Age represented the convergence of imperial wealth, religious fervor, intellectual ambition, and artistic genius—a moment when Spain led European civilization before passing that role to *France* and eventually to Atlantic powers.
