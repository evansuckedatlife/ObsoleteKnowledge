## summary

**Acceleration** is the vector quantity that measures the rate at which an object changes its velocity over time, encompassing changes in both speed and directional orientation. Formally codified by *Sir Isaac Newton* in his 1687 treatise *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, it serves as the dynamical link between net applied force and [[mass]] through the second of the [[newton-laws-of-motion]]. In modern physics, it bridges classical Newtonian mechanics and the general theory of relativity formulated by [[albert-einstein]], where gravitational effects are recognized as indistinguishable from inertial acceleration.

## you gotta know

- Defined mathematically as the first derivative of velocity with respect to time, or the second derivative of position with respect to time, commonly expressed in SI units of meters per second squared.
- Governed in classical mechanics by the second of the [[newton-laws-of-motion]], establishing that the net force exerted on a body equals the product of its [[mass]] and its induced acceleration.
- Operates strictly as a vector quantity possessing both magnitude and direction, meaning that any change in trajectory—such as uniform circular motion driven by centripetal force—constitutes acceleration even when scalar speed remains constant.
- Measured near the surface of Earth as standard gravitational acceleration ($g$), roughly equal to $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, which *Galileo Galilei* demonstrated acts uniformly on all bodies in a vacuum regardless of their individual mass.
- Formulates the foundational cornerstone of general relativity through the *equivalence principle* of [[albert-einstein]], which posits that the physical experience of uniform acceleration is locally indistinguishable from immersion within a gravitational field.
- Generates electromagnetic radiation known as bremsstrahlung or synchrotron radiation when a charged particle, such as an [[electron]], undergoes transverse or longitudinal acceleration through electric or magnetic fields.
- Quantified relativistically via the four-acceleration vector, whose invariant norm corresponds to proper acceleration—the physical acceleration directly measured by an onboard accelerometer traversing a spacetime worldline.
- Appears in non-inertial rotating reference frames as fictitious or inertial accelerations, notably the centrifugal acceleration and the Coriolis effect that influences large-scale atmospheric cyclonic circulation on planets like [[jupiter]].

## connections

- [[mass]] — the intrinsic inertial property of matter that resists changes in motion and inversely scales the acceleration generated by a net force.
- [[newton-laws-of-motion]] — the classical dynamical laws whose second postulate directly relates net external force to mass and acceleration.
- [[gravity]] — the fundamental attraction that accelerates unconstrained masses toward gravitating bodies along geodesics in curved spacetime.
- [[albert-einstein]] — the theoretical physicist whose equivalence principle established the physical identity between inertial acceleration and gravitational fields.
- [[quantum-mechanics]] — the subatomic theory wherein Ehrenfest's theorem proves that the quantum expectation values of observables obey classical equations of acceleration.
- [[standard-model]] — the modern framework of particle physics governing how fundamental gauge bosons mediate forces that accelerate elementary fermions.
- [[electron]] — the negatively charged lepton whose rapid acceleration produces characteristic continuous spectrum X-rays and synchrotron emissions.
- [[jupiter]] — the massive gas giant whose immense gravitational field provides orbital acceleration during spacecraft gravity-assist slingshot maneuvers.
