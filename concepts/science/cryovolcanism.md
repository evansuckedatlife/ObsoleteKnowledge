## summary

**Cryovolcanism** is volcanic activity on icy bodies throughout the solar system, where water, ice, and organic compounds erupt instead of molten silicate rock. Unlike terrestrial volcanism driven by heat, cryovolcanic eruptions are powered by internal pressure and subsurface oceans beneath frozen crusts. These processes have shaped the geology of moons like Enceladus, Europa, and Triton, making them targets for the search for extraterrestrial life.

## you gotta know

- Occurs on icy bodies with subsurface oceans or internal volatile layers: Saturn's Enceladus, Europa (Jupiter's Galilean moon), Triton (Neptune satellite), Pluto's Charon.
- Erupts water-ice fountains mixed with ammonia, methane, organics, and salts rather than silicate magma; material is less dense than rock, launched higher.
- Driven by tidal heating: gravitational flexing from orbital eccentricity or resonances generates internal friction; opposes radiogenic heat that drives terrestrial volcanism.
- Pressure from overlying ice or from subsurface ocean compression provides the driving force; no convecting mantle required.
- Enceladus (discovered active, 2005 Cassini): geysers eject ~200 km, forming Saturn's E-ring; plume composition reveals subsurface organic chemistry and salts (ocean-world signature).
- Europa (high priority target): surface cracks and "chaos terrain" suggest active tidal heating; models predict a global ocean beneath 10–30 km ice shell.
- Triton (encountered Voyager 2, 1989): nitrogen geysers at −235°C, among coldest sustained eruptions in solar system; captured Kuiper Belt object with tidal dissipation.
- Pluto-Charon system (New Horizons, 2015): Tombaugh Regio's smooth, young surface suggests cryovolcanism; binary system's orbital dynamics provide tidal heat.
- Astrobiological significance: liquid water + energy + organic material create potential habitats; extremophile analogs on Earth (deep subsurface biomes) support habitability scenarios.
- Detection methods: spectroscopy (water/ammonia signatures), thermal imaging (warm plume sources), particle/dust analysis (composition); future orbiters/landers target ocean worlds for biosignatures.
