## summary

**Electronegativity** is a measure of an atom's tendency to attract electrons toward itself in a chemical bond. Elements with high electronegativity strongly pull electrons away from bonding partners; those with low electronegativity release electrons more readily. This property determines bond type and molecular polarity.

## you gotta know

- Electronegativity is a relative scale—there is no absolute unit—and depends on the atom's position in the periodic table and its nuclear charge.
- Fluorine is the most electronegative element; alkali metals (lithium, sodium, potassium) are the least electronegative.
- Differences in electronegativity between bonded atoms predict bond type and character: large differences create [[ion|ionic bonds]], small differences create [[covalent-bond|covalent bonds]].
- The [[pauling-scale]] quantifies electronegativity on a scale of roughly 0.7 to 3.98; it allows chemists to predict bond polarity numerically.
- High electronegativity elements (like oxygen, nitrogen, and halogens) strongly attract electrons, creating permanent dipoles in molecules; this unequal electron distribution makes water and ammonia highly polar solvents.
- Electronegativity increases moving up and to the right on the periodic table (toward fluorine, the most electronegative element); it decreases moving down and to the left (toward cesium and francium, the least electronegative metals), reflecting the balance of nuclear charge and electron shielding.
- Electronegativity differences explain bond polarity and reactivity: highly electronegative atoms readily gain electrons to become anions, while weakly electronegative atoms readily lose electrons to become cations, driving ionic bonding and chemical reactions.
