## summary

**John Stewart Bell** (1928–1990) was an Irish physicist who developed [[bell-inequality|Bell's inequality]], a mathematical statement that fundamentally challenged the foundations of [[quantum-mechanics]]. His 1964 theorem showed that no [[local-realism|local realist]] theory could reproduce all predictions of quantum mechanics, forcing physicists to confront whether quantum entanglement violates locality, causality, or realism itself. Bell's work transformed the philosophical interpretation of quantum mechanics from academic debate into experimentally testable physics.

## you gotta know

- Born in Belfast, Northern Ireland in 1928 and studied physics and mathematics at [[university-of-ulster|Queen's University Belfast]].
- Developed [[bell-inequality|Bell's inequality]] in 1964 while at CERN (European Organization for Nuclear Research) in Geneva, initially as a response to the EPR paradox proposed by [[albert-einstein]] and collaborators.
- Bell's theorem proved that *any* local hidden-variable theory must obey his inequality; quantum mechanics violates it, meaning either locality or realism must be abandoned.
- The inequality is mathematically elegant: for measurements on entangled particles, the quantum predictions exceed the classical bound by at most a factor of √2, a result independent of the specific quantum system.
- Bell's work inspired decades of increasingly precise experiments to test his inequality; most confirmed quantum mechanics and rejected local realism, though *loopholes* remained until 2015.
- Authored the influential essay "On the Problem of Hidden Variables in Quantum Mechanics" (1966) and later wrote accessibly about quantum foundations in "Speakable and Unspeakable in Quantum Mechanics" (1987).
- Worked at CERN for most of his career, contributing to both theoretical quantum mechanics and accelerator physics.
- Died in 1990 shortly before experimental evidence strongly supported his predictions; the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for experiments closing loopholes in Bell tests he had inspired.
- His humble demeanor and philosophical insights made him unique among theoretical physicists; he communicated quantum foundations ideas with unusual clarity and accessibility to both specialists and general audiences.
