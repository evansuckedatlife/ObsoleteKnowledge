## summary

A **mutation** is a permanent alteration in the DNA sequence of an organism, affecting one or more nucleotides. Mutations arise through errors in DNA replication, spontaneous chemical changes, exposure to radiation or mutagens, or recombination errors. While most mutations are neutral or deleterious, rare beneficial mutations drive evolution, providing the raw genetic variation upon which natural selection acts.

## you gotta know

- Point mutations alter a single nucleotide; substituting thymine for cytosine, for example, may change a codon, potentially changing the amino acid inserted during translation.
- Frameshift mutations insert or delete nucleotides; because the genetic code is read in triplets, insertions or deletions shift the reading frame, corrupting all downstream codons (usually fatal).
- Silent mutations change a codon but do not alter the amino acid (due to codon degeneracy); nonsense mutations create a stop codon prematurely, truncating the protein.
- Missense mutations change an amino acid; some amino substitutions have negligible effect (conservative changes), while others drastically alter protein folding or function.
- Spontaneous mutation rates are approximately 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻¹⁰ per base pair per division in humans; proofreading and mismatch repair reduce errors 100-1000 fold below the raw polymerase error rate.
- Mutagens—chemicals, radiation, or biological agents—increase mutation rates; examples include benzene, UV light, X-rays, and nitrous acid.
- Somatic mutations occur in non-reproductive cells (affecting that individual only); germline mutations occur in germ cells, transmissible to offspring and shaping populations over generations.
- Natural selection culls deleterious mutations; beneficial mutations increase in frequency; neutral mutations drift randomly, potentially fixing by chance in small populations.
