## summary

**Electricity** encompasses the diverse range of physical phenomena associated with the stationary presence, accumulation, or dynamic movement of electric charges, fundamentally mediated at the subatomic scale by the [[electron]]. First recorded through static electrostatic attraction in antiquity and atmospheric lightning, its mathematical formalization during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries enabled widespread mechanical harness during the [[industrial-revolution]]. In the contemporary era, it forms the indispensable physical substrate of global civilization, driving municipal power grids, telecommunications networks, and low-voltage digital circuitry across the [[motherboard]] to sustain computation.

## you gotta know

- Formulated as *Ohm's Law*, the fundamental baseline of circuit analysis dictates that electric current is directly proportional to potential difference and inversely proportional to resistance, relationships graphically represented by *Gustav Kirchhoff*'s circuit laws and physically regulated within circuits by the [[resistor]].
- The late-nineteenth-century *War of the Currents* pitted *Thomas Edison*'s direct current against *Nikola Tesla* and *George Westinghouse*'s alternating current, ultimately establishing AC as the worldwide municipal grid standard because alternating voltages can easily be stepped up and down with transformers to minimize resistive transmission losses.
- Demonstrated by *Michael Faraday* in 1831, electromagnetic induction proves that a time-varying magnetic flux through a conducting circuit induces an electromotive force, providing the fundamental operating principle for electric motors, dynamos, and large-scale utility generation during the [[industrial-revolution]] across [[england]] and the [[united-states]].
- In metallic conductors, electrical conduction manifests as the slow collective drift velocity of the mobile [[electron]] superimposed upon rapid thermal motion under an electric field, an interaction comprehensively unified with magnetism in the classical electrodynamic field equations formulated by *James Clerk Maxwell*.
- Modern computer architecture relies on electricity as an information carrier, utilizing power supply units to rectify high-voltage alternating grid current into regulated low-voltage direct current rails that flow across the [[motherboard]] to toggle microscopic transistors within the [[central-processing-unit]].
- Explained by [[albert-einstein]] in 1905 using *Max Planck*'s quantum hypothesis, the photoelectric effect demonstrated that light shining on a metallic surface ejects electrons only above a threshold frequency via discrete energy packets called the [[photon]], proving particle-wave duality and launching [[quantum-mechanics]].
- Biological nervous systems harness electrical potential differences across [[cell]] membranes, employing specialized voltage-gated ion channels that shuttle cations such as [[sodium]] across lipid bilayers to propagate action potentials down axons and coordinate muscular contraction.
- At high energies, electromagnetism is unified with the weak nuclear force under the electroweak theory of the [[standard-model]] developed by *Sheldon Glashow*, *Abdus Salam*, and *Steven Weinberg*, an interaction mediated by vector bosons that couples to leptons and each flavor of [[quark]].

## connections

- [[electron]] — the fundamental subatomic lepton whose negative charge and mobility in metals make electrical conduction possible.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — the historical era of technological expansion accelerated by central electrical grids, dynamos, and motor power.
- [[motherboard]] — the main printed circuit board distributing regulated direct-current voltages to maintain digital logic across components.
- [[central-processing-unit]] — the primary computational processor executing program instructions through billions of electrically switched microscopic transistors.
- [[resistor]] — foundational passive circuit component that opposes electric current flow and drops voltage in accordance with Ohm's law.
- [[albert-einstein]] — theoretical physicist who explained the photoelectric effect by treating light as discrete packets of energy.
- [[quantum-mechanics]] — fundamental physical theory explaining energy band structures in solids that govern electrical conductivity, insulation, and semiconduction.
