## summary

An **energy level** is a quantized, discrete value of energy that a bound physical system—most famously an [[electron]] bound within an [[atom]]—is permitted to occupy. Formalized in the early twentieth century through the *Bohr model* and the subsequent mathematical maturity of [[quantum-mechanics]], these discrete states resolve the catastrophic classical paradox of orbital decay and explain why atomic matter remains structurally stable. Understanding these quantized states is fundamental across modern physics and physical chemistry, as radiative transitions between them govern how matter absorbs and emits a [[photon]], underpinning technologies ranging from analytical *spectroscopy* and astronomical observation to semiconductor electronics and quantum computing.

## you gotta know

- When an [[electron]] shifts between an excited state and a lower energy level, it emits a [[photon]] whose frequency is directly proportional to the energy difference via the *Planck-Einstein relation*, generating unique, diagnostic spectral lines.
- In 1913, Danish physicist *Niels Bohr* postulated that orbital angular momentum is quantized in integer multiples of the reduced *Planck constant*, successfully deriving the empirical *Rydberg formula* and predicting spectral series such as the *Lyman* and *Balmer* series.
- The lowest accessible energy configuration of a bound system is designated the *ground state*; due to the *Heisenberg uncertainty principle*, a localized quantum system cannot possess zero kinetic energy and retains a permanent *zero-point energy*.
- In wave mechanics, treating an [[electron]] as a [[wavefunction]] confined by an electrostatic Coulomb potential transforms the time-independent *Schrödinger equation* into an eigenvalue problem, where spatial boundary conditions mathematically eliminate all but a discrete spectrum of allowed energy states.
- When multiple distinct quantum states share an identical energy value, the state is termed *degenerate*, with individual sub-states indexed by principal, azimuthal, and magnetic quantum numbers that reflect underlying geometric and spatial symmetries.
- Subjecting an [[atom]] to external electromagnetic environments breaks spatial rotational symmetry, splitting degenerate levels into observable multiplets known as the *Zeeman effect* in magnetic fields and the *Stark effect* in electric fields.
- Relativistic kinematics and *spin-orbit coupling* split coarse spectral states into *fine structure*, while interactions between orbital leptons and the magnetic dipole moment of the atomic [[nucleus]] produce delicate *hyperfine structure*, which generates the critical 21-centimeter cosmic hydrogen line.

## connections

- [[quantum-mechanics]] — the overarching theoretical framework establishing that physical confinement restricts continuous energy distributions into discrete eigenvalues.
- [[electron]] — the negatively charged fundamental lepton whose electrostatic binding within an atomic potential well establishes discrete electronic shell structures.
- [[photon]] — the elementary quantum of light that is absorbed or emitted whenever a charged particle transitions across two distinct states.
- [[atom]] — the bound chemical entity whose quantized internal configurations determine chemical bonding, reactivity, and characteristic emission spectra.
- [[wavefunction]] — the mathematical amplitude function whose normalizable boundary conditions inside a potential well dictate allowed stationary states.
- [[nucleus]] — the dense, positively charged core whose electrostatic Coulomb field provides the physical potential well confining orbital electrons.
- [[albert-einstein]] — formulated the photoelectric effect and derived the statistical *A and B coefficients* governing spontaneous and stimulated radiative transitions.
