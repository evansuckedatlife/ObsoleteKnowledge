## summary

The **W boson** is a massive elementary particle that mediates the weak nuclear force, responsible for radioactive decay and certain particle interactions. Existing in two charged varieties (W+ and W−), it is among the heaviest fundamental particles known and was experimentally confirmed in 1983. The W boson's discovery vindicated the electroweak unification theory and remains central to understanding matter's subatomic transformations.

## you gotta know

- Exists in two charge states: W+ (positive) and W− (negative), allowing it to mediate different decay processes and charge transformations among quarks and leptons.
- Extraordinarily massive for a boson—approximately 80.4 GeV/c², nearly 80 times the proton mass—giving the weak force its characteristically short range of only about 10^-18 meters.
- Discovered experimentally in 1983 by Carlo Rubbia and Simon van der Meer at CERN using the Super Proton Synchrotron, a discovery that earned them the Nobel Prize in Physics.
- Responsible for beta decay, where a neutron transforms into a proton by emitting a W− boson (which decays into an electron and antineutrino), a process crucial to stellar nucleosynthesis.
- Mediates interactions between leptons (like electrons) and quarks, enabling fundamental processes in stars (where it drives fusion reactions) and the early universe.
- Forms part of the electroweak unification: the W and [[z-boson]] bosons unify electromagnetic and weak interactions at energies above approximately 100 GeV, a prediction confirmed at the Large Hadron Collider.
- Cannot be produced or observed directly; detected only through its decay products (leptons and quarks) in particle accelerator experiments, revealing itself through missing energy signatures.
- Predicted theoretically by Sheldon Glashow, Abdus Salam, and Steven Weinberg in the 1960s as part of the electroweak theory, years before experimental confirmation in the 1980s.
- Production occurs only at the most powerful particle accelerators; the Large Hadron Collider produces millions of W bosons annually, each decaying in picoseconds to reveal its identity through detector signatures.
