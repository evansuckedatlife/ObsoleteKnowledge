## summary

**General relativity**, also called the **general theory of relativity**, is the geometric theory of gravitation formulated by *[[albert-einstein|Albert Einstein]]*, which describes gravity not as an attractive force but as the curvature of four-dimensional spacetime shaped by [[mass]] and energy. Published in *[[germany|Germany]]* in late 1915, the theory revolutionized classical physics by showing that freely falling objects simply follow straight paths, known as geodesics, across warped geometry. It remains an indispensable pillar of modern astrophysics and cosmology, providing the mathematical foundation for understanding black holes, the expansion of the universe, and gravitational waves.

## you gotta know

- The equivalence principle forms the core conceptual foundation of the theory, asserting that the local physical effects of gravity are indistinguishable from uniform acceleration, famously visualized by *Einstein's* thought experiment of an observer inside a windowless, freely falling elevator.
- The *Einstein field equations* mathematically describe how matter and energy dictate the geometry of spacetime, setting the *Einstein tensor*—composed of the Ricci curvature tensor and scalar curvature—proportional to the *stress-energy tensor* through Newton's gravitational constant and the speed of light.
- Gravitational lensing, the deflection of light around massive astronomical bodies, was dramatically verified during the total solar eclipse of May 1919 by British expeditions led by *Arthur Eddington*, an observational triumph that brought *[[albert-einstein|Einstein]]* worldwide acclaim following the devastation of *[[world-war-i|World War I]]*.
- The theory famously explained the anomalous perihelion precession of Mercury, accounting for an extra forty-three arcseconds of shift per century that classical Newtonian models of [[planetary-motion]] could not explain, eliminating the need for a hypothesized inner planet called *Vulcan*.
- Gravitational time dilation dictates that clocks tick slower in deeper gravitational potentials, a relativistic phenomenon directly verified terrestrially by the *Pound-Rebka experiment* using gamma-ray [[photon|photons]] and regularly accounted for in modern satellite navigation constellations and [[nasa|NASA]] space probes.
- The *Schwarzschild metric*, derived in 1916 by *Karl Schwarzschild* while serving on the Russian front, provided the first exact non-trivial solution to the vacuum field equations, establishing the theoretical reality of non-rotating black holes and their surrounding event horizons.
- The cosmological constant was originally added to the field equations to allow for a static universe, an addition *Einstein* famously abandoned after astronomical observations proved cosmic expansion, though it has since reemerged in modern cosmology as dark energy driving the accelerating universe.
- Frame-dragging, formally known as the *Lense-Thirring effect*, predicts that massive rotating bodies drag the surrounding inertial frames of spacetime with them, a delicate relativistic prediction conclusively verified in Earth orbit by the superconducting gyroscopes of the *Gravity Probe B* satellite.

## connections

- [[albert-einstein]] — theoretical physicist who formulated the field equations in 1915.
- [[quantum-mechanics]] — foundational modern theory whose probabilistic principles remain notoriously difficult to unify with smooth spacetime geometry.
- [[standard-model]] — framework governing fundamental particle physics that successfully incorporates three fundamental forces while omitting gravity.
- [[photon]] — massless light particle whose trajectories follow curved null geodesics and undergo gravitational redshift.
- [[planetary-motion]] — celestial orbital mechanics whose Newtonian descriptions required relativistic corrections to resolve orbital anomalies.
- [[germany]] — country where the foundational papers were presented to the Prussian Academy of Sciences.
- [[nasa]] — space agency whose navigational telemetry and deep-space missions require relativistic gravitational corrections.
