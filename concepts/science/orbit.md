## summary

An **orbit** is the curved path traced by an object as it moves under the influence of gravity from a massive central body. Described mathematically by [[newton-laws-of-motion|Newton's laws]] and [[gravity|gravitational force]], orbits are conic sections (ellipses, circles, parabolas, hyperbolas) depending on the object's energy and angular momentum. Planets orbit stars, moons orbit planets, and artificial [[satellite|satellites]] orbit Earth—all following the same gravitational principles. Understanding orbits is essential to celestial mechanics, space exploration, and predicting the motion of celestial bodies.

## you gotta know

- **Elliptical orbits** dominate: planets, moons, and satellites follow ellipses with the primary (star or planet) at one focus; a circular orbit is a special case with both foci coincident.
- **Orbital period** follows Kepler's third law: **T² ∝ a³**, where *T* is the period and *a* is the semi-major axis; Mars, farther from the Sun than Earth, takes longer to orbit proportionally to the cube root of distance.
- **Aphelion** (or apogee for Earth satellites) is the farthest point in an orbit; **perihelion** (or perigee) is the nearest; the difference defines orbital eccentricity, ranging from 0 (circle) to 1 (parabola, escape velocity).
- **Escape velocity** (~11.2 km/s from Earth) is the speed needed to reach infinite distance; objects below this speed remain bound and orbit; above it, they follow hyperbolic paths and escape the gravitational well.
- **Retrograde orbits** are unusual, moving counter to the primary's rotation; they arise from gravitational capture of asteroids or from deliberate orbital maneuvers in spacecraft (e.g., interplanetary transfers).
- **Orbital velocity** decreases with altitude: low Earth orbit satellites move ~7.8 km/s; the Moon, 384,000 km away, moves only 1 km/s around Earth.
- **Orbital decay** occurs when atmospheric drag or other perturbations remove energy; low-orbit satellites gradually spiral downward until atmospheric reentry.
- **Orbital resonance** occurs when orbital periods share integer ratios (e.g., Neptune's moon Triton orbits in a 1:1 resonance with Neptune's rotation, causing tidal heating); this drives orbital dynamics and stability in multi-body systems.

## connections

- [[gravity]] — the force that creates and maintains orbits.
- [[newton-laws-of-motion]] — the framework predicting orbital paths and periods.
- [[moon]] — an orbiting body locked in a synchronous orbit, always showing the same face to its primary.
- [[planet]] — typically in nearly circular orbits around its star.
- [[satellite]] — any object orbiting a primary, whether natural (moon) or artificial (space station).
