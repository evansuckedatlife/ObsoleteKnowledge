## summary

The **Scoville scale** is a measurement of the pungency (spiciness) of hot peppers and spicy foods, quantified in *Scoville Heat Units* (SHU). Developed in 1912 by pharmacist Wilbur Lincoln Scoville, it originally relied on human tasters to detect the heat threshold of pepper extracts. Modern labs use high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to measure capsaicinoids directly, providing objective numeric values that range from 0 for bell peppers to over 2 million for superhot varieties.

## you gotta know

- Named after Wilbur Scoville, a chemist who devised the original organoleptic (taste-based) test.
- Bell peppers score 0 SHU; jalapeños range 2,500–8,000; habaneros reach 100,000–350,000.
- The hottest peppers on record (Carolina Reaper, Trinidad Moruga Scorpion) exceed 2 million SHU.
- Originally measured by diluting pepper extract in sugar water until tasters could no longer detect heat.
- Modern HPLC methods directly quantify capsaicin and dihydrocapsaicin, the alkaloids responsible for heat.
- The scale is logarithmic in practical use—a twofold increase in SHU feels far hotter than linear doubling might suggest.
- Used not only by chefs and food manufacturers but also in hot-sauce competitions and military applications.
- Capsaicinoids bind to pain receptors (TRPV1) on the tongue, creating the illusion of burning; they do not cause actual cellular damage.
- The Scoville test has been largely superseded by instrumental analysis, yet remains culturally iconic in eating challenges and pepper grading.
- Individual peppers of the same variety vary in heat due to genetics, soil nutrients, water availability, and harvest ripeness.
