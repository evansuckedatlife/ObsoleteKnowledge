## summary

The **weak force** is one of the four fundamental interactions in physics, responsible for radioactive decay and neutrino interactions. Operating at subatomic scales with extremely short range, it mediates the transformation of quarks and leptons, enabling neutrons to decay and fueling stellar nuclear fusion. Despite its name, the weak force becomes comparable in strength to electromagnetism at very high energies, a unification principle central to modern particle physics.

## you gotta know

- Mediated by three massive bosons: the W+, W−, and Z bosons, each approximately 80–91 GeV/c² in mass, making them thousands of times heavier than electrons.
- Responsible for beta decay, the process by which a neutron converts to a proton, releasing an electron and antineutrino—a transformation fundamental to nuclear stability and radioactivity.
- Operates only among leptons (electrons, muons, tau particles, neutrinos) and hadrons (particles made of quarks), though it does not mediate interactions between photons or gluons.
- Violates parity conservation—processes mediated by the weak force do not mirror their mirror images, a discovery in 1956 that earned Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen Ning Yang a Nobel Prize.
- Extraordinarily short-range due to the high mass of its mediator bosons; its effects vanish beyond subatomic distances of about 10^-18 meters, explaining why it is "weak" at low energies.
- Combines with electromagnetism into the electroweak force at energies above ~100 GeV, a unification predicted by Sheldon Glashow and confirmed experimentally in 1973 via neutral-current interactions.
- Essential for stellar nucleosynthesis: without the weak force, hydrogen could not fuse into helium in stars via the proton-proton chain reaction, and the universe would lack heavy elements and carbon-based life.
- One of only four fundamental forces known to physics, alongside electromagnetism, the strong force, and gravity, though it is the only one that violates CP (charge-parity) symmetry.
