## summary

**Atoms** are the smallest units of matter that retain the identity of a chemical element, consisting of a nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by a cloud of electrons. The building blocks of all ordinary matter, atoms combine through chemical bonding to form molecules and extended structures. Modern atomic theory emerged from *Rutherford's* gold-foil experiment (1909), which revealed that atoms are mostly empty space with a dense, positively charged nucleus.

## you gotta know

- Nucleus (protons + neutrons) contains 99.9% of the atom's mass in a volume ~10⁻¹⁵ m; electrons orbit in ~10⁻¹⁰ m radius.
- Atomic number (Z) = number of protons; determines element identity and chemical properties.
- Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons; defines the isotope and determines atomic mass (approximately).
- Electrons occupy orbitals arranged in shells (K, L, M...) according to quantum mechanics, not Bohr's classical planetary model.
- Chemical properties arise from valence electrons (outermost shell); filled shells are stable and confer chemical inertness (noble gases).
- Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different neutron counts; vary in mass but identical chemistry.
- Bound together by electromagnetic force (attraction between nucleus and electrons); nuclear force (strong force) holds nucleus together.
- Atoms are mostly empty space; the electron probability cloud defines effective atomic radius, not a solid boundary.
- Ionization removes one or more electrons, creating positively charged cations; gaining electrons forms negatively charged anions.
- Atomic radius trends: increases down a group (more electron shells), decreases across a period (increased nuclear charge pulling electrons closer).
- Electronegativity (tendency to attract electrons) and ionization energy (energy to remove an electron) vary systematically across the periodic table; determine chemical reactivity and bonding type.
- Periodic table organization reflects electronic structure: groups share valence-electron configuration, explaining recurring chemical properties (e.g., noble gases' inertness, halogens' reactivity).
- Modern atomic theory reconciles quantum mechanics with classical observation: atoms are stable, discrete entities despite being mostly empty space.
