## summary

A **hurricane** is a rotating tropical cyclone characterized by violent winds exceeding 119 km/h (74 mph), a low-pressure center, and spiral rain bands, powered by warm ocean water and occurring in the North Atlantic basin (the term "cyclone" and "typhoon" apply to the same phenomenon in other ocean basins). Hurricanes are among Earth's most energetic and destructive weather phenomena, capable of releasing energy equivalent to thousands of nuclear bombs and causing catastrophic damage to coastal communities through wind, storm surge, and flooding.

## you gotta know

- Hurricanes form over warm tropical oceans (at least 26.5°C / 80°F) where evaporation provides latent heat energy; the Coriolis effect spins rising air into a rotating vortex.
- The *eye* at the hurricane's center is a region of relative calm (light winds, clear skies), surrounded by the *eyewall*—the most violent part, with the strongest winds and heaviest rainfall.
- *Wind shear*, ocean temperature, atmospheric moisture, and atmospheric instability are the key factors determining whether a tropical depression intensifies into a hurricane; climate change is increasing warm water availability and precipitation potential.
- The *Saffir-Simpson scale* classifies hurricanes by sustained wind speed (Categories 1–5), with Category 5 representing winds above 252 km/h and potential for catastrophic damage. Wind alone is deadly; *storm surge* (raised water level from pressure and wind) often causes more damage than wind.
- Hurricanes rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere (due to the Coriolis effect) and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere; they weaken rapidly over land or cold water as their energy source (warm ocean) is cut off.
- Historically, hurricanes were named only after 1950; names now alternate male and female (and recently other designations) to aid public communication. Hurricane frequency follows seasonal peaks (September peak in the Atlantic basin) and shows multi-year cycles (e.g., Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation).

- Rapid intensification is the most dangerous behavior: a tropical depression can explosively strengthen into a Category 5 hurricane in 24 hours when conditions align perfectly (warm water, low shear, high humidity).
- Storm surge is often deadlier than wind: the storm's low pressure physically raises the ocean surface, and winds push water onto land. A 5-meter surge in a populated coast kills thousands.
- Climate projections suggest hurricanes will intensify (max wind speeds increase) but frequency may decrease slightly—fewer storms, but more intense and potentially more damaging ones.

## connections

- [[tropical-cyclone]] — the generic term for rotating storm systems; hurricane is the Atlantic/East Pacific name.
- [[wind]] — the primary hazard of hurricanes; sustained high winds cause structural damage and downed trees.
- [[atmospheric-pressure]] — hurricanes form where very low pressure (sometimes below 900 mb) creates strong pressure gradients.
- [[beaufort-wind-force-scale]] — the historical scale (now supplemented by Saffir-Simpson) classifying hurricane-force winds (Force 12).
- [[saffir-simpson-scale]] — the modern intensity scale for hurricanes based on sustained wind speed and damage potential.
- [[water-cycle]] — hurricanes are engines of the water cycle, evaporating vast amounts of ocean water and depositing it as intense rainfall.
- [[climate]] — hurricane intensity and frequency are influenced by global sea-surface temperatures and climate oscillations.
