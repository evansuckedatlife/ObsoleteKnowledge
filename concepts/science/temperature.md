# Temperature

## summary

**Temperature** is a quantitative measure of the average kinetic energy of particles (atoms and molecules) in a substance, reflecting how rapidly they are moving. It is distinct from heat (which is energy transferred between objects at different temperatures) and is measured using standardized scales—Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin—that have shaped scientific practice, everyday life, and international standards for over two centuries.

## you gotta know

- *Temperature* reflects the *average kinetic energy* of particles; higher temperature means faster molecular motion, while lower temperature means slower motion (approaching absolute zero at –273.15°C).
- *[[Celsius]]* (originally called centigrade) divides the interval between the freezing and boiling points of water into 100 degrees; 0°C is the freezing point and 100°C is the boiling point at standard atmospheric pressure.
- *[[Fahrenheit]]*, used primarily in the United States, assigns 32°F to water's freezing point and 212°F to its boiling point, yielding 180 degrees between these landmarks; the scale's arbitrary origin reflects 18th-century thermometer calibration practices.
- *[[Kelvin scale]]*, the SI standard for science, begins at absolute zero (0 K = –273.15°C), the theoretical lowest possible temperature where molecular motion ceases; water freezes at 273.15 K and boils at 373.15 K.
- *Absolute zero* (0 K) cannot be reached experimentally, though laboratory temperatures now routinely reach near-absolute-zero conditions where quantum effects dominate; as temperature approaches zero, most gases liquefy, then solidify.
- *Temperature gradients* drive heat flow from hotter to colder regions; this drives winds (pressure differences from uneven heating), ocean currents, and geological processes like mantle convection and volcanic activity.
- *Thermometers* measure temperature by exploiting properties that change predictably with temperature—liquid expansion (mercury or alcohol), electrical resistance (resistance thermometers), or infrared radiation (pyrometers).

## connections

- [[celsius-scale]] — the most common scale for everyday and scientific measurements, especially in most countries.
- [[fahrenheit-scale]] — the scale used in the United States and associated territories for weather and medical measurements.
- [[kelvin-scale]] — the absolute scale used in science and defined by the SI system of units.
- [[heat]] — the transfer of energy from higher to lower temperatures; temperature is the driving force.
- [[thermodynamics]] — the science of temperature, heat, and energy transformations.
