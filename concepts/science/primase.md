## summary

**Primase** is a specialized RNA polymerase that synthesizes short RNA primers during DNA replication, providing the 3'-OH group that DNA polymerase requires to begin synthesis. Essential to the replication process, primase catalyzes the formation of primers on both the leading and lagging strands, enabling complete copying of the double helix. It represents a molecular solution to the problem of initiating DNA synthesis.

## you gotta know

- Belongs to the primase enzyme family, distinct from standard DNA polymerases; it synthesizes RNA rather than DNA, producing primers roughly 8-12 nucleotides in length in prokaryotes.
- DNA polymerase cannot initiate synthesis *de novo* (from scratch); it requires a 3'-OH group already present to extend from, making primase essential for every replication event.
- Creates short RNA-DNA hybrid molecules (the primer plus the start of DNA synthesis); these primers are later removed and replaced with DNA by DNA polymerase I (in prokaryotes) or other exonucleases and polymerases (in eukaryotes).
- Acts continuously on the lagging strand to synthesize many primers for each Okazaki fragment (~1000 nucleotides in prokaryotes, ~100-200 in eukaryotes), but only once on the leading strand.
- Couples with DNA polymerase III (prokaryotes) or DNA polymerase alpha (eukaryotes); the polymerase immediately extends the primer synthesized by primase.
- Functions as part of the primosome complex in prokaryotes, a multiprotein assembly that couples primer synthesis with strand unwinding and polymerase loading.
- Highly conserved across all domains of life (bacteria, archaea, eukaryotes), indicating its ancient and essential role in DNA metabolism.

## connections

- [[dna-replication]] — the process for which primase synthesizes primers.

- [[enzyme]] — primase is a ribozyme-like enzyme (proteinaceous in modern cells) catalyzing nucleotide polymerization.
