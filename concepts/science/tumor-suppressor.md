## summary

**Tumor suppressors** are genes that encode proteins acting as brakes on cell division and growth, preventing uncontrolled proliferation that leads to cancer. By detecting DNA damage, inducing cell cycle arrest, triggering apoptosis, or promoting DNA repair, tumor suppressors maintain cellular health and protect against malignant transformation. Loss or inactivation of tumor suppressor genes is a hallmark of cancer, making them central to understanding oncology and developing cancer therapies.

## you gotta know

- The p53 gene (TP53) encodes a transcription factor controlling DNA damage response, cell cycle checkpoints, apoptosis, and metabolism; it is the most frequently mutated gene in human cancers.
- p53 acts as "guardian of the genome" by halting cell cycle at the G1/S checkpoint when DNA damage is detected, allowing time for repair or triggering apoptosis if damage is irreparable.
- The retinoblastoma protein (Rb) controls the G1/S checkpoint by binding and inactivating the E2F transcription factor; loss of Rb allows uncontrolled entry into S phase.
- Tumor suppressors often work in networks: BRCA1 and BRCA2 repair DNA via homologous recombination; their mutations predispose to breast and ovarian cancers.
- Both copies of a tumor suppressor gene must be lost (Knudson's "two-hit hypothesis") for cancer to develop, since a single functional copy often provides sufficient protection.
- DNA mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2) prevent tumor formation by correcting DNA replication errors; their loss leads to microsatellite instability and Lynch syndrome.
- Tumor suppressors are inactivated in cancer through mutations, deletions, promoter methylation, or interactions with viral oncoproteins (human papillomavirus E6 inactivates p53).
- APC protein regulates Wnt signaling and prevents excessive cell proliferation; its loss allows adenomatous polyp formation and colorectal cancer development.
- Senescence (cellular aging) induction by tumor suppressors like p16 prevents unlimited cell division; bypass of senescence is a requirement for cancer cell immortalization.
