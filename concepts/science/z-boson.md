## summary

The **Z boson** is a massive, electrically neutral elementary particle that mediates the weak nuclear force through neutral current interactions. Discovered in 1983 alongside the W boson at CERN, the Z boson represents a crucial experimental confirmation of electroweak unification theory. Existing only briefly before decaying into lighter particles, it enables neutrino interactions and rare particle transformations that would be impossible under earlier theoretical frameworks.

## you gotta know

- Electrically neutral (hence "Z0"), distinguishing it from the charged W+ and W− bosons that also mediate weak interactions, allowing it to mediate processes without changing charge.
- Approximately 91.2 GeV/c² in mass, slightly heavier than the W bosons; its mass is predicted with high precision by the Standard Model and confirmed experimentally to remarkable accuracy.
- Discovered experimentally in 1983 by Carlo Rubbia and Simon van der Meer at CERN's Super Proton Synchrotron, earning them the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1984.
- Mediates neutral weak currents, allowing neutrinos to scatter off electrons and quarks without exchanging electric charge—a process predicted by Sheldon Glashow in the 1960s.
- Cannot be created directly; only observed through its decay products (electrons, muons, tau particles, or quarks) in particle detector experiments, making it even more ephemeral than the W boson.
- Its discovery provided conclusive evidence for the electroweak unification—at energies above ~100 GeV, electromagnetic and weak forces merge into a single unified electroweak interaction.
- Participates in rare processes like lepton flavor-changing neutral currents and precision electroweak measurements that test the Standard Model and search for physics beyond it.
- Plays a crucial role in the Big Bang theory; the symmetric electroweak force in the early universe would have produced Z bosons that mediated key interactions in particle creation and annihilation.
