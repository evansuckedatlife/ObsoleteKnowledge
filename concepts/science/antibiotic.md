## summary

**Antibiotics** are molecules (usually produced by microorganisms or synthesized chemically) that kill bacteria or inhibit their growth by targeting essential bacterial structures or processes. Discovered accidentally in 1928 when *Alexander Fleming* observed that mold contamination killed bacteria around it, antibiotics revolutionized medicine and transformed bacterial infections from often-fatal to treatable. Today, antibiotic resistance—bacteria evolving immunity through genetic mutation—poses a major public health challenge.

## you gotta know

- Penicillin, discovered from the mold *Penicillium*, was the first widely used antibiotic; it works by inhibiting bacterial cell-wall synthesis, causing lysis (bursting).
- Beta-lactam antibiotics (penicillins, cephalosporins) share a four-membered lactam ring that binds penicillin-binding proteins in the bacterial cell wall.
- Aminoglycosides (streptomycin, gentamicin) inhibit bacterial ribosomal protein synthesis by binding the 30S ribosomal subunit, preventing translation.
- Tetracyclines block bacterial protein synthesis at the 30S ribosome; they are broad-spectrum and can treat many gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria.
- Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin) inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, preventing DNA replication and causing cell death.
- Selective toxicity is the principle behind antibiotic action: they target bacterial structures or enzymes that humans lack (peptidoglycan cell walls, 70S ribosomes) or that differ significantly in eukaryotes.
- Antibiotic resistance emerges when bacteria acquire mutations or genes (via conjugation, transformation, or transduction) that confer resistance—expressed as modified target enzymes, efflux pumps, or degradative enzymes.
- Common resistance mechanisms: beta-lactamase production (breaking down beta-lactams), ribosomal mutations (preventing antibiotic binding), and active efflux (pumping antibiotics out of the cell).
