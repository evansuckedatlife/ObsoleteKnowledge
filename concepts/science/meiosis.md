## summary

**Meiosis** is the specialized cell division that produces gametes (sperm and egg), reducing the chromosome number by half (from diploid to haploid) and generating genetic diversity through crossing over and independent assortment. In contrast to mitosis's identical copies, meiosis creates four unique haploid cells from one diploid parent, ensuring that offspring receive exactly one set of chromosomes from each parent. This reduction is essential for sexual reproduction to maintain constant chromosome numbers across generations.

## you gotta know

- Two sequential divisions: Meiosis I (reduction division) separates homologous chromosome pairs; Meiosis II resembles mitosis, separating sister chromatids.
- Produces four genetically unique haploid gametes (n) from one diploid precursor (2n); during oogenesis, polar bodies are often non-functional, yielding one egg per meiosis.
- Prophase I: homologous chromosomes pair (synapsis) via the synaptonemal complex; crossing over (recombination) exchanges DNA segments between non-sister chromatids.
- Metaphase I: homologous pairs align randomly at the metaphase plate; orientation determines which chromosome moves to which pole (independent assortment).
- Anaphase I: homologous chromosomes separate (not sister chromatids); each pole receives one chromosome per homologous pair, randomly assorted.
- Telophase I and cytokinesis: two daughter cells form, each haploid but consisting of unreplicated chromosomes; no interphase-like replication before Meiosis II.
- Meiosis II: essentially mitosis on haploid cells; sister chromatids separate, yielding four unique haploid gametes in a symmetric division.
- Genetic variation arises from crossing over (~100–300 per human meiosis) and independent assortment (2ⁿ possibilities; n=23 in humans = >8 million combinations).
- Errors in meiosis (nondisjunction): failure to separate homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids produces aneuploid gametes (wrong chromosome count).
- Aneuploidy consequences: trisomy-21 (Down syndrome, viable), trisomy-18/-13 (usually embryonic lethal), monosomy-X (Turner syndrome, viable); autosomal monosomies are typically lethal.
- Meiosis is the driver of sexual reproduction and genetic diversity; explains why siblings are unique despite sharing parents.
