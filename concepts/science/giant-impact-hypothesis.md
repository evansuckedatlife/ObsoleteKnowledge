# Giant Impact Hypothesis

## summary

The **giant impact hypothesis** (also called the "Big Whack") proposes that the Moon formed when a Mars-sized protoplanet named *Theia* collided with the young Earth approximately 4.5 billion years ago during the Hadean Eon. The impact vaporized much of both bodies and ejected a disk of debris into orbit around Earth, where it rapidly coalesced into the Moon. This elegant theory explains the Moon's composition, size, orbital dynamics, and Earth's tilted rotation—and is the scientific consensus after decades of modeling and isotopic analysis.

## you gotta know

- Before the 1970s, the Moon's origin was a mystery; three competing theories (co-accretion, fission, and capture) each had fatal flaws. The giant impact theory resolved them all.
- *Theia* (named after the Titans in Greek mythology) is thought to have been roughly the size of Mars, with sufficient momentum to vaporize upon impact and redistribute material into a disk.
- The impact was catastrophic: it melted both bodies, added enormous rotational momentum to Earth (creating our 24-hour day and tilted axis), and fundamentally altered Earth's chemistry and heat budget.
- The Moon's *oxygen isotope ratios* closely match Earth's, suggesting common origin; if the Moon had formed elsewhere and been captured, isotope ratios would differ significantly—key evidence supporting the impact theory.
- Computer simulations (N-body modeling) show a Mars-sized body striking Earth at a glancing angle naturally produces a Moon-mass satellite orbiting at the right distance and speed, matching observations.
- The collision explains why the Moon lacks a large iron core (Theia's iron sank into Earth), the Moon's relatively large size compared to its parent planet, and the rapid cooling required to explain lunar geology.

- Isotopic evidence is the key: measuring ratios of oxygen and tungsten isotopes in lunar vs. terrestrial rocks shows they match, implying a common origin. If the Moon had formed elsewhere and been captured, isotope ratios would differ.
- The collision was not a head-on impact but a glancing blow: a Mars-sized body struck at an angle, vaporizing both colliding bodies and ejecting a disk of material. This off-center impact is crucial to explain the Moon's size and orbit.
- Late Heavy Bombardment: after the giant impact, Earth remained a target in a chaotic early solar system, pummeled by asteroids and comets. The Moon's heavily cratered surface preserves this violent period.
