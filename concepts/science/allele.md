## summary

**Alleles** are alternative versions of a gene at the same locus on a chromosome, differing in their DNA sequence and often producing different phenotypic outcomes. Most genes exist in multiple alleles within a population; an individual carries two copies (one from each parent), determining their phenotype through dominance relationships. Alleles are the units of inheritance whose frequencies change under selection, mutation, and genetic drift, driving evolution and genetic diversity.

## you gotta know

- One allele per gene per chromosome; diploid organisms carry two copies (homozygous if identical, heterozygous if different).
- Arise through mutation: any change to DNA sequence at a locus generates a new allele (substitution, insertion, deletion, duplication).
- Allele frequency (p, q, etc.) is the proportion in a population; Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium predicts genotype frequencies from allele frequencies in the absence of evolutionary forces.
- Dominant alleles mask recessive phenotypes in heterozygotes; recessive alleles express only when homozygous; dominance is defined by the phenotype, not the allele.
- Incomplete dominance: heterozygotes show an intermediate phenotype (neither parental type); codominance: both parental traits appear simultaneously in heterozygotes.
- Population genetics tracks allele frequencies; frequencies change through selection (favored alleles increase), genetic drift (random sampling in small populations), mutation, and migration.
- Inferred by *Mendel* from pea-plant breeding ratios (calling them "factors"); formally named "alleles" by *Hugo de Vries* in the 1900s.
- Selection coefficient (s) quantifies the fitness cost of an allele; selection intensity depends on s and allele frequency, making rare alleles harder to purge.
- Multiple alleles can exist in a population: ABO blood group (three alleles: Iᴬ, Iᴮ, i), HLA loci (~10,000 known alleles); diversity is maintained by selection and mutation-drift balance.
- Balancing selection maintains multiple alleles (e.g., heterozygote advantage in sickle-cell trait; frequency-dependent selection); loss of alleles reduces population genetic variation.
- Allele frequencies change under selection, drift, and mutation; allele-frequency change is the essence of evolution at the molecular level.
