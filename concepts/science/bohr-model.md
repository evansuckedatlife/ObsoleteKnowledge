## summary

The **Bohr model** is a simplified atomic model proposed by *Niels Bohr* in 1913, depicting electrons orbiting the nucleus in discrete, quantized shells (energy levels) rather than a diffuse electron cloud. Though superseded by quantum mechanics and the *Schrödinger* equation, the Bohr model successfully explained the hydrogen spectrum and introduced the revolutionary idea of energy quantization. It remains pedagogically valuable for understanding atomic structure and the origin of spectral lines.

## you gotta know

- The model assumes electrons orbit the nucleus like planets around the sun, but only in specific allowed orbits corresponding to discrete energy levels (quantization).
- The first Bohr radius (a₀ ≈ 0.53 Ångströms) defines the size of the hydrogen atom; successive orbitals are at distances proportional to *n*² (where *n* = 1, 2, 3...).
- Energy of the *n*-th orbit in hydrogen is *E*ₙ = −13.6 eV / *n*²; electrons can only occupy these discrete levels, not the spaces between.
- Spectral lines arise when electrons transition between energy levels: absorbing photons to jump to higher levels (excitation), or emitting photons when falling to lower levels (de-excitation).
- The Rydberg formula, *ν* = *R* × (1/*n*₁² − 1/*n*₂²), predicted the frequency of emitted light with remarkable accuracy for hydrogen.
- Angular momentum is quantized in the Bohr model: *L* = *n*ℏ (where ℏ is the reduced Planck constant), explaining why only certain orbits are allowed.
- The model fails for multi-electron atoms because it does not account for electron-electron repulsion and relativistic effects, and it incorrectly predicts orbital shapes.
- The Schrödinger equation and quantum mechanics replaced the Bohr model with orbitals (probability distributions) rather than definite trajectories; *n* remains quantized (principal quantum number) but the path is undefined.
