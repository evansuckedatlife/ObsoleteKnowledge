## summary

An **extinction event** is a period of rapid loss of biodiversity in which a significant fraction of the world's species disappear in a relatively short geological time. Earth has experienced five major extinction events over the past 540 million years; each reshaped ecosystems and opened ecological niches for survivors.

## you gotta know

- The "Big Five" mass extinction events are: Ordovician-Silurian (450 mya), Late Devonian (375 mya), Permian-Triassic (252 mya), Triassic-Jurassic (200 mya), and Cretaceous-Paleogene (66 mya); these span the entire history of complex multicellular life.
- The Permian-Triassic extinction (the "Great Dying") eliminated roughly 96% of marine species and 70% of terrestrial vertebrate species—the most severe extinction known.
- The Cretaceous-Paleogene (K-Pg) extinction, 66 million years ago, wiped out non-avian [[dinosaurs]]; a massive asteroid impact near present-day Mexico is the primary cause.
- [[synapsids]]—proto-mammals—were devastated by the Permian extinction but lineages survived to evolve into modern mammals.
- Causes of extinction events include asteroid impacts, massive volcanism, climate shifts, ocean acidification, and disruption of food chains.
- Extinction opens ecological opportunity by freeing resources: after non-avian dinosaurs died, mammals diversified rapidly and eventually dominated terrestrial ecosystems, filling the vacant niches.
- Modern biodiversity loss is sometimes called the "Sixth Extinction," driven by human activity (habitat destruction, climate change, pollution) rather than natural catastrophes, occurring at rates comparable to or exceeding past mass extinction events.
