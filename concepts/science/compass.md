## summary

A **compass** is a navigational instrument that determines direction relative to the terrestrial surface using a magnetized pointer aligned with Earth's geomagnetic field. First developed in ancient China during the *Han dynasty* using natural lodestone and refined into a needle instrument during the *Song dynasty*, it transformed global maritime travel. By establishing reliable headings irrespective of overcast skies or open-sea horizon obscurity, the magnetic compass served as a cornerstone of the *Age of Discovery*, trade network expansion, and modern navigational science.

## you gotta know

- Utilizes a magnetized needle mounted on a low-friction pivot that freely aligns itself with the local horizontal component of the geomagnetic field.
- The fundamental physics rests on the intrinsic magnetic dipole moments of unpaired *[[electron]]* spins within ferromagnetic materials such as magnetite or tempered iron.
- Earliest prototypes in China, known as south-pointing fish or spoons carved from mineral lodestone, were initially employed for geomancy and *feng shui* before naval adoption.
- Described in military navigation by *Shen Kuo* in his eleventh-century polymath work *Dream Pool Essays*, noting magnetic declination away from true astronomical north.
- Adopted by European navigators by the late twelfth century, enabling dead reckoning across open maritime expanses like the Mediterranean and Atlantic.
- The dry-mariner compass incorporates a gimballed suspension to maintain horizontal equilibrium despite the rolling and pitching motions of seafaring vessels.
- Modern variants counter magnetic deviation induced by iron ship hulls through compensatory soft iron spheres named *Kelvin's balls* mounted near the binnacle.
- Contemporary aviation and naval fleets supplement magnetic designs with the non-magnetic gyrocompass, which detects true geographic north using rotational angular momentum.
