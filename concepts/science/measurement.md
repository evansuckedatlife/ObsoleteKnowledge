## summary

In quantum mechanics, **measurement** refers to the act of observing or extracting information from a quantum system, which fundamentally alters its state. A quantum system exists in a superposition of states until measured; upon measurement, the wavefunction "collapses" to a single eigenstate corresponding to the observed result. This counterintuitive phenomenon—that observation itself influences reality—lies at the heart of quantum weirdness and remains philosophically contentious.

## you gotta know

- A quantum system before measurement is described by a wavefunction |ψ⟩ that is a superposition of multiple possible states, each with an associated probability amplitude.
- Upon measurement of an observable (like position or energy), the wavefunction collapses irreversibly; the outcome is one eigenvalue of the corresponding operator, with probability given by the squared amplitude.
- The uncertainty principle limits what can be simultaneously known: measuring position precisely destroys knowledge of momentum, and vice versa—Δ*x* · Δ*p* ≥ *ℏ*/2.
- Wave-particle duality is intimately tied to measurement: particles exhibit wave-like interference until measured, at which point they "choose" a single particle-like path.
- In quantum entanglement, measuring one particle instantaneously "tells" an entangled partner what to be, collapsing its superposition at spacelike separation (Einstein's "spooky action at a distance").
- The measurement problem is unsolved philosophically: interpretations like Copenhagen (wavefunction is real but collapses upon measurement), many-worlds (collapse is illusory; measurement branches the universe), and pilot-wave (deterministic), each propose different mechanisms.
- Repeated measurements of the same system in the same state yield identical results (predictability), but different states yield distributions matching quantum predictions—evidence that measurement's physical action is real.
