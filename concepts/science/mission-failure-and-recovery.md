## summary

**Mission failure and recovery** encompasses the procedures, decision-making, and teamwork required to handle unexpected emergencies during space missions and return spacecraft and crew safely. The canonical example is [[apollo-13]], the 1970 lunar mission crippled by an oxygen-tank explosion that forced engineers and astronauts to adapt equipment and improvise solutions under extreme pressure. Success in crisis recovery depends on robust mission design, ground-based expertise at [[mission-control]], clear communication, redundant systems, and the ability to reprioritize objectives from exploration to survival.

## you gotta know

- Apollo 13's oxygen-tank explosion stranded three astronauts 380,000 km from Earth; mission control in Houston performed real-time calculations to plot a safe return using the Lunar Module as a lifeboat.
- The onboard systems were not designed for the specific failure mode: using the LM's life-support systems to absorb CO₂ from the Command Module required improvised lithium-hydroxide canisters, adapting incompatible hardware interfaces.
- Mission control reversed consumable-depletion predictions by adjusting procedures: reducing electrical power consumption, minimizing water use, and leveraging gravity assists to change trajectory with minimal fuel burns.
- Recovery success hinges on ground-station expertise: engineers understand vehicle systems deeply enough to suggest solutions in real time; this requires a large, trained workforce and institutional knowledge.
- Redundancy and modularity are essential: the Lunar Module served as emergency shelter and propulsion stage, transforming a single-point failure (Command Module) into a recoverable crisis.
- Contingency planning before launch identifies critical failures; simulation and training prepare crews for known scenarios, while problem-solving teams address unanticipated issues as they arise.
- Documentation (systems manuals, electrical schematics, procedures) are lifelines during crisis; crews and ground personnel must quickly find and interpret technical information.
- The [[apollo-program|Apollo]] program's success in recovering from Apollo 13 demonstrated that human spaceflight requires both engineering excellence and human adaptability—machines can fail; humans improvise.
