## summary

**Carbon dating** is an isotopic chronometric method developed in the late 1940s by American chemist *Willard Libby* to determine the age of carbonaceous materials. By measuring the residual activity or atomic ratio of radioactive carbon-14 relative to stable isotopes, scientists can date organic remains up to roughly 50,000 years old. The technique fundamentally transformed modern archaeology, paleontology, and Quaternary geology.

## you gotta know

- Invented at the University of Chicago by *Willard Libby*, who received the 1960 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for establishing the technique.
- Relies on the unstable isotope *carbon-14*, which decays into stable *nitrogen-14* via beta decay with a half-life of roughly 5,730 years.
- Cosmic rays strike nitrogen-14 nuclei in the upper atmosphere, causing neutron capture and proton emission to continuously generate carbon-14 atoms.
- Living organisms incorporate carbon-14 continuously throughout life via plant uptake of ambient gases followed by food web assimilation.
- Isotopic replenishment ceases abruptly upon an organism's death, causing the ratio of carbon-14 to stable carbon-12 to decline exponentially over time.
- Standard modern determinations employ *accelerator mass spectrometry* (*AMS*), which counts individual isotope atoms directly instead of awaiting decay events.
- Raw radiocarbon ages must be corrected against chronological calibration curves, such as *IntCal*, constructed from dendrochronology and speleothems.

## connections

- [[carbon]] — the elemental base whose radioisotope carbon-14 enables the chronological measurement.
- [[nucleus]] — the atomic core that undergoes nuclear beta decay during the transmutation to nitrogen.
- [[atom]] — individual isotopic species counted directly in modern accelerator mass spectrometry.
- [[photosynthesis]] — the biological mechanism through which radiocarbon enters the terrestrial biosphere.
- [[electron]] — the beta particle emitted during the nuclear radioactive decay of carbon-14.
