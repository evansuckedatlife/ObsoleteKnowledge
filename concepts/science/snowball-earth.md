## summary

The **Snowball Earth** hypothesis proposes that Earth experienced episodes of near-total glaciation during the Proterozoic eon, with ice sheets extending to the equator and covering the entire ocean surface. These extreme glacial events, lasting millions of years, were terminated by greenhouse warming from accumulated volcanic carbon dioxide. The Snowball Earth concept revolutionized understanding of Earth's climate extremes and the resilience of life in the most hostile conditions.

## you gotta know

- Snowball Earth events occurred primarily during the Neoproterozoic Era (850–635 million years ago), with at least two or three major glaciation episodes.
- Evidence for extreme glaciation includes glacial deposits (tillites) at equatorial latitudes, dropstones (glacially transported rocks) in marine sediments, and carbon isotope anomalies indicating reduced photosynthesis.
- A global ice cover would reflect nearly all sunlight (high albedo), creating a runaway ice-albedo feedback that perpetuates freezing even at the equator.
- Snowball Earth episodes likely ended when volcanic emissions accumulated sufficient carbon dioxide to trigger a strong greenhouse effect, warming the planet rapidly.
- The transition from glaciation to rapid warming would create extreme environmental stress, yet evidence suggests life not only survived but radiated shortly after, giving rise to complex multicellular organisms.
- The Snowball Earth hypothesis explains the abrupt transition between the Proterozoic and Cambrian periods and the so-called Cambrian explosion of body plans.
- Some researchers propose "Slush ball" variants where thin equatorial ice allowed some photosynthesis, reducing the severity but maintaining the fundamental model.
- The hypothesis was controversial but is now supported by geological evidence including iron oxide deposits formed under anoxic conditions beneath global ice sheets.
- Metabolic innovations—perhaps chemosynthetic bacteria thriving in ice-free refugia—may have enabled the rapid diversification of eukaryotic life following deglaciation.
