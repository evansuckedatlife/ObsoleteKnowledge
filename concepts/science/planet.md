## summary

**Planets** are large celestial bodies that orbit stars, massive enough to have cleared their orbital zones of other debris. The eight planets of our solar system range from rocky terrestrial worlds close to the Sun to vast gas giants in the outer system. Planets are the primary targets for understanding habitability and are central to both solar system science and the search for worlds beyond our Sun.

## you gotta know

- Planets orbit stars, held in place by gravity, and are distinct from moons (which orbit planets) and asteroids (which are smaller and not gravitationally dominant).
- The eight planets in our solar system, in order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.
- Mercury and Venus are terrestrial (rocky) planets with thin or no atmospheres; Earth and Mars are also terrestrial but more complex.
- Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants, composed primarily of hydrogen and helium with no solid surface; they dwarf all other planets.
- Uranus and Neptune are ice giants, containing methane and water ices, orbiting far from the Sun's warmth.
- A planet must satisfy three criteria: orbit a star, have sufficient mass for hydrostatic equilibrium (round shape), and have cleared its orbital neighborhood of other bodies.
- Exoplanets—planets orbiting distant stars—have been discovered by the thousands, revealing extraordinary diversity in size, composition, and orbital characteristics.
- Habitable zone planets, orbiting within the distance where liquid water could exist, are prime targets for astrobiology and the search for extraterrestrial life.
- Planetary motion follows Kepler's laws: planets in elliptical orbits sweep equal areas in equal times, and orbital period relates to distance from the star.
