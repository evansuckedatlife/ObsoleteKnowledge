## summary

The **moment magnitude scale** (abbreviated **Mw** or **MMS**) is the standard logarithmic scale used by modern seismologists to quantify the total energy released by an [[earthquake]]. Developed in 1979 by geophysicists *Thomas C. Hanks* and *Hiroo Kanamori* in the [[united-states]], the system was created to overcome the physical limitations of the classic [[richter-scale]] by deriving magnitude directly from physical fault mechanics. Today, it serves as the authoritative international standard across observational geophysics, providing consistent and reliable measurements for events ranging from minor tremors to the catastrophic megathrust ruptures that shape the surface of the planet.

## you gotta know

- Replaced the classic [[richter-scale]] as the primary metric reported by seismological agencies worldwide, though news outlets still routinely and mistakenly refer to modern earthquake estimates as Richter readings.
- Solved the pervasive problem of magnitude *saturation*, an instrumental barrier where earlier peak-amplitude scales stopped scaling upward during major earthquakes above magnitude eight regardless of their actual physical size.
- Derived from the *seismic moment*, a fundamental physical parameter equal to the product of the rock rigidity, the surface area of the fault rupture, and the average displacement across the [[fault]].
- Scaled such that an increase of one integer unit represents approximately a 31.6-fold jump in total radiated elastic energy, while an increase of two full units represents an exact factor of one thousand.
- Enabled the historical reassessment of massive subduction zone catastrophes, retroactively confirming the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile as the most powerful event ever instrumentally recorded at magnitude 9.5.
- Calibrated to maintain seamless continuity with historical measurements by matching Richter's local magnitude values for moderate temblors, ensuring decades of mid-twentieth-century seismological catalogs remained directly comparable.
- Relies mathematically on the formula two-thirds times the common logarithm of the scalar seismic moment minus 10.7, determined through centroid moment tensor inversions of long-period [[seismic-waves]].

## connections

- [[earthquake]] — the physical fracture and slip event within the lithosphere whose total released energy this metric quantifies.
- [[richter-scale]] — the pioneering local magnitude scale developed by *Charles Richter* whose susceptibility to saturation prompted this modern refinement.
- [[seismic-waves]] — the ground vibrations recorded on long-period seismograms to compute low-frequency spectral amplitudes for moment tensor inversion.
- [[plate-tectonics]] — the global geodynamic system driving the crustal stresses and subduction zones that unleash the planet's largest temblors.
- [[fault]] — the geological boundary whose ruptured surface area and shear slip distance directly enter the seismic moment equation.
- [[logarithmic-scale]] — the mathematical structure of the index, whereby small numerical increments represent exponential leaps in dissipated physical energy.
- [[united-states]] — location of Caltech and the United States Geological Survey, where *Thomas C. Hanks* co-designed the scale in California.
- [[japan]] — home country of co-inventor *Hiroo Kanamori*, whose research on catastrophic Pacific megathrust events established the foundation for moment magnitude.
