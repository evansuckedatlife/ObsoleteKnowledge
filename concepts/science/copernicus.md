## summary

**Nicolaus Copernicus** (1473–1543) was a Polish astronomer and mathematician whose heliocentric model—placing the Sun at the center of the solar system rather than Earth—fundamentally challenged medieval cosmology and the authority of *Ptolemy*'s geocentric model. Though controversial among the Church and contemporary astronomers, the heliocentric theory explained retrograde planetary motion elegantly and provided the foundation for Newton's laws of motion. Copernicus's model marked the beginning of the Scientific Revolution and the transition from ancient to modern astronomy.

## you gotta know

- Copernicus published *De revolutionibus orbium coelestium* (On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres) in 1543, near the end of his life, proposing that Earth and planets orbit the Sun.
- The heliocentric model solved the retrograde motion problem: planets appear to move backward against the stars because observers are on a moving Earth with different orbital speeds.
- Copernicus retained circular orbits (not ellipses), which caused inaccuracies; later corrections by Kepler (elliptical orbits) and Newton (gravity) validated the heliocentric framework.
- The model faced opposition from the Catholic Church and traditional astronomers; the charge of "impiety" made heliocentrism dangerous to propose openly during the Renaissance.
- Copernicus was born in Toruń, Poland, studied at universities in Kraków, Bologna, and Padua, and spent most of his career as a physician and administrator in Warmia.
- Tycho Brahe's precise observations in the 1580s provided empirical evidence favoring heliocentrism; Galileo's telescope observations of Jupiter's moons (1610) provided further support.
- Kepler synthesized Copernican heliocentrism with Tycho's data, proposing elliptical orbits and deriving the three laws of planetary motion—the final synthesis of observational and theoretical astronomy.
- The Copernican principle—that Earth holds no special position in the universe—remains a cornerstone of modern cosmology and philosophy of science.
