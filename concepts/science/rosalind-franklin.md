## summary

**Rosalind Franklin** (1920–1958) was a British X-ray crystallographer whose experiments provided crucial evidence for the double helix structure of DNA. Though she worked largely in obscurity during her lifetime, her Photo 51—a crystallographic image showing the helical geometry of DNA—was instrumental in Watson and Crick's model-building. She died at 37 from cancer, four years before the Nobel Prize was awarded for the DNA structure discovery.

## you gotta know

- Trained as a chemist with expertise in physical chemistry and crystallography, specializing in coal and carbon structures before turning to biological molecules.
- Produced Photo 51 in 1952, an X-ray diffraction image showing the characteristic X-pattern of helical DNA; it provided direct visual evidence for the double helix geometry.
- Her data showed that DNA exists in two forms (A and B forms) with different hydration and helical parameters, indicating remarkable structural flexibility in the molecule.
- Worked at King's College London in the early 1950s under Maurice Wilkins, though the two had a contentious working relationship; her contributions were sometimes understated or overlooked by her male colleagues.
- Generated quantitative crystallographic data on DNA's dimensions: the spacing between base pairs, the pitch of the helix, and the diameter, which constrained the possible models.
- Moved to Birkbeck College in 1953 to study RNA structure, publishing important work on virus structure before her premature death from ovarian cancer in 1958.
- Her role in the DNA structure discovery was largely unrecognized until decades later; her obituary and Watson's later memoir revealed how central her data were to Watson and Crick's model.
