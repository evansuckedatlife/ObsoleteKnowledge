## summary

An **isotope** is a distinct nuclear species of a chemical element that shares the exact same atomic number and proton count as other forms of that element, but differs in its neutron count and total atomic mass. Coined and systematically demonstrated in the early twentieth century by radiochemist *Frederick Soddy*, the concept overturned the classical Daltonian assumption that every [[atom]] of an element possessed an identical, immutable mass. Today, isotopic science provides the foundation for understanding nuclear stability, reconstructing past climate regimes, and measuring deep time across archaeology, while enabling modern medical diagnostics and technologies governed by [[quantum-mechanics]].

## you gotta know

- Variations in neutron count alter the physical mass and nuclear stability of an [[atom]], yet because the outer [[electron]] shell remains unchanged, different isotopes exhibit nearly indistinguishable chemical reactivity.
- Hydrogen is the only element whose primary isotopes possess distinct conventional names: *protium* (no neutrons), *deuterium* (one neutron, used with [[oxygen]] to produce heavy water), and *tritium* (two neutrons, an unstable radioisotope critical to nuclear fusion).
- Unstable radioisotopes undergo spontaneous radioactive decay to achieve lower energy states, emitting ionizing radiation in the form of an alpha particle, beta particle, or high-energy [[photon]].
- Naturally occurring [[carbon]] exists primarily as *carbon-12* and *carbon-13*, while trace cosmogenic *carbon-14* decays with a characteristic 5,730-year half-life, forming the bedrock of radiocarbon dating in archaeology.
- Scottish physician *Margaret Todd* suggested the name in 1913 to chemist *Frederick Soddy*, drawing on *ancient-greece* roots meaning "same place" because all isotopic variants of an element occupy the exact same slot on the periodic table.
- Isotope separation emerged as a monumental technological challenge during [[world-war-ii]], prompting the *Manhattan Project* to construct sprawling enrichment complexes that isolated fissile uranium-235 from abundant uranium-238.
- The *kinetic isotope effect* demonstrates that substituting a heavier isotope at a reaction center alters zero-point vibrational energy and slows reaction rates, serving as a vital diagnostic probe for enzymatic pathways such as [[photosynthesis]].
- British physicist *Francis William Aston* confirmed the isotopic composition of dozens of non-radioactive elements using the mass spectrograph, proving his *whole number rule* that isotopic weights are whole-number multiples of hydrogen.

## connections

- [[atom]] — the fundamental chemical unit whose identity and electron count remain constant across isotopic forms.
- [[nucleus]] — the dense central cluster of nucleons whose variable neutron count defines each specific isotope.
- [[carbon]] — the primary organic element whose radioisotope *carbon-14* enables radiometric dating.
- [[oxygen]] — the abundant element whose stable isotope ratios serve as paleoclimate indicators in ice core records.
- [[electron]] — the subatomic particle whose identical orbital arrangement gives different isotopes the same chemistry.
- [[quantum-mechanics]] — the theoretical framework explaining nuclear stability, radioactive decay, and mass-dependent vibrational states.
- [[photosynthesis]] — the biological pathway whose carbon-fixing enzymes exhibit kinetic isotope discrimination.
- [[world-war-ii]] — the conflict that spurred industrial-scale enrichment facilities to separate fissile uranium isotopes.
