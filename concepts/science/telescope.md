## summary

A **telescope** is an optical instrument that uses lenses or mirrors to collect and focus light, magnifying distant objects and enabling observation of faint celestial bodies invisible to the naked eye. Invented in the early 17th century and revolutionizing astronomy, telescopes evolved from Galileo's refractors to Newton's reflectors to modern space-based observatories. They are among the most transformative instruments in science, extending human perception across the electromagnetic spectrum.

## you gotta know

- **Refracting telescope** uses a large primary lens to focus light; familiar design since Galileo's 1610 observations, but large lenses are difficult and expensive to manufacture, and glass absorbs light at wavelengths beyond the visible.
- **Reflecting telescope** uses a concave mirror as the primary element; invented by Newton in 1668, mirrors are easier to make large and reflect across the entire electromagnetic spectrum from infrared to gamma rays.
- *Magnification* (primary lens/mirror focal length divided by eyepiece focal length) amplifies angular size; *light-gathering power* (proportional to aperture area) is more important for faint objects and determines limiting magnitude.
- *Resolving power* (ability to distinguish fine details) is limited by diffraction; proportional to wavelength and inversely to aperture diameter; larger telescopes see finer details.
- *Chromatic aberration* in refractors: different wavelengths focus at different distances, creating color fringes; corrected via achromatic lenses or eliminated entirely by reflectors.
- Modern ground-based telescopes like Keck (10-meter mirror) use adaptive optics to correct atmospheric distortion; space telescopes (*Hubble*, *James Webb*) escape atmosphere entirely.
- Telescopes operate across the electromagnetic spectrum: radio telescopes detect centimeter to meter wavelengths; infrared telescopes observe heat; ultraviolet and X-ray telescopes require space-based platforms.
