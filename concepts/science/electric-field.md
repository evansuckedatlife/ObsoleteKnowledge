## summary

An **electric field** is a region of space where an electric charge experiences a force; it is the carrier of electromagnetic interaction and permeates all space wherever charges exist. Introduced by Faraday as a conceptual bridge between distant charges, the electric field is one of the two components of the electromagnetic field (the other being the magnetic field), and its behavior is described by Maxwell's equations, which underpin all of classical electromagnetism and modern electronics.

## you gotta know

- A charge creates an electric field in the surrounding space; another charge placed in that field experiences a force proportional to its magnitude and inversely proportional to the square of distance (*Coulomb's law*).
- *Electric field strength* (E) is defined as force per unit charge; it points away from positive charges and toward negative charges. It is a *vector field*—at each point in space, it has magnitude and direction.
- The electric field is *conservative*: the work done moving a charge between two points is independent of the path taken. This enables the concept of *electric potential* (V), a scalar that describes the field.
- *Electrostatic fields* (static charges) and *induced fields* (changing magnetic fields) both contribute to the total electric field; Maxwell's equations unify these and show light is an electromagnetic wave.
- The electric field *energy density* is proportional to E²; strong fields concentrate energy. This principle underlies capacitors, which store energy in their electric fields.
- *Dielectric materials* modify electric fields by becoming polarized; their molecular charges partially cancel the applied field, reducing its strength inside the material.

- Faraday's action-at-a-distance concept was radical: the field allows distant charges to interact without direct contact. This replaced older intuitions about forces requiring physical contact.
- Field superposition: if multiple charges create fields, the total field at any point is the vector sum of individual fields. This principle enables complex field calculations from simple components.
- The electric field inside a conductor (in electrostatic equilibrium) is zero: charges rearrange on the conductor's surface until the field within vanishes. This shielding effect (Faraday cage) protects sensitive electronics.
