## summary

A **retrograde orbit** is the orbital motion of a celestial body in a direction opposite to the rotation of its primary (the body it orbits), or opposite to the orbital direction of other bodies in the same system. Retrograde orbits are relatively rare among moons and planets and often indicate a different origin than bodies with prograde (normal) orbits. Their existence reveals the dynamic history of planetary systems.

## you gotta know

- Defined by the angular momentum vector pointing opposite to the primary's spin angular momentum; an object in retrograde orbit appears to move "backward" when viewed from above a planet's rotational poles.
- The majority of moons orbit prograde (in the same direction as their primary's rotation), suggesting formation from a circumplanetary disk of material around the young planet.
- Retrograde orbits are thought to result primarily from gravitational capture of passing bodies—an object with sufficient velocity to escape from interstellar space is deflected by a planet and settles into orbit with reversed angular momentum.
- Neptune's moon Triton is the most famous example of a retrograde moon; its large mass and distant orbit strongly suggest it was captured from the Kuiper Belt rather than forming in situ.
- Mars's moon Deimos has a retrograde orbit and is significantly smaller than Mars's other moon Phobos, consistent with a captured origin.
- Retrograde orbits tend to be unstable on cosmically long timescales; tidal interactions gradually cause them to spiral inward, eventually leading to collision with the primary or destruction through tidal forces.
- Some planets exhibit retrograde axial rotation (spin) rather than retrograde orbital motion; Venus and Uranus are notable examples of planets spinning backward compared to their solar orbit.
