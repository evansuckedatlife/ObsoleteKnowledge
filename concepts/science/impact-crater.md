## summary

An **impact crater** is a depression in a planetary surface caused by the violent collision of an asteroid, meteoroid, or comet, creating a characteristic circular depression with raised rims and ejected material. Impact craters are among Earth's most dramatic geological features and are found on every solid body in the solar system, with older surfaces like the [[moon]] showing dense crater populations spanning billions of years. Large impacts fundamentally alter planetary evolution, redistribute material across surfaces, and have episodically triggered mass extinctions on Earth.

## you gotta know

- Formed by the sudden deposition of kinetic energy at collision: the impactor compresses rock instantaneously, creating a shock wave that excavates material far beyond the projectile's size.
- Impact structures scale with impactor energy: small meteorites create simple bowl-shaped craters; massive asteroid impacts create complex features with central peaks, terraced walls, and surrounding ejecta blankets.
- The [[moon]] displays hundreds of thousands of craters ranging from microns to thousands of kilometers across, with ancient crater densities used to date lunar surface regions.
- Terrestrial impact craters include *Chicxulub* in Mexico (130 km diameter), linked to the 66-million-year-old [[extinction-event]] that killed the dinosaurs; *Vredefort* in South Africa (300 km), the oldest known; and *Sudbury* in Canada (1.85 km), a fossil complex impact with economic mineral deposits.
- The [[giant-impact-hypothesis]] proposes that the [[moon]] itself formed from debris ejected when a Mars-sized body collided with proto-Earth ~4.5 billion years ago.
- Ejecta (debris hurled from the impact site) blankets surrounding terrain and can contain shocked quartz and tektites—glassy objects formed from vaporized rock that cool mid-flight.
- Impact crater density increases with surface age: ancient highlands show more craters; younger volcanic plains show fewer, allowing planetary geologists to estimate absolute ages of surfaces.
- On Earth, atmospheric entry friction causes most meteoroids to ablate before impact, but larger asteroids penetrate the atmosphere and produce craters; oceans absorb most impacts without leaving permanent scars.

## connections

- [[moon]] — the most densely cratered body in the inner solar system, preserving a 4.5-billion-year record of impacts.
- [[asteroid-belt]] — the source of most impactors striking inner solar system bodies.
- [[giant-impact-hypothesis]] — proposes the [[moon]]'s origin from a planetary-scale impact.
- [[extinction-event]] — large impacts can trigger climate change and mass die-offs, as in the K–Pg extinction.
- [[mars]] — a heavily cratered terrestrial planet showing ancient and recent impact features.
