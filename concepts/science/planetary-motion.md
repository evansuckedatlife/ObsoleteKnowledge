## summary

**Planetary motion** describes the orbital kinematics and mechanics of celestial bodies traversing gravitational fields, most famously formulated through the three empirical regularities known as **Kepler's laws of planetary motion** (or **Kepler's laws**). Developed during the European [[renaissance]] by German mathematician and astronomer *Johannes Kepler* using the meticulous naked-eye observational data of *Tycho Brahe*, these principles broke centuries of dogma by proving that planets orbit along ellipses rather than perfect circles. Decades later in [[england]], Sir *Isaac Newton* unified terrestrial and celestial mechanics within his *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, demonstrating that universal [[gravity]] and his [[newton-laws-of-motion]] mathematically dictate all orbital paths. In the twentieth century, *Albert Einstein* revolutionized this framework through [[general-relativity]], interpreting orbits not as forces acting across space, but as geodesics following the curvature of spacetime.

## you gotta know

- Kepler's first law, the law of ellipses, states that every planet moves in an elliptical orbit around the Sun, with the central star situated at one of the two foci rather than at the center, permanently displacing the geocentric deferents and epicycles inherited from [[ancient-greece]].
- Kepler's second law, the law of equal areas, dictates that an imaginary line segment joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time, establishing that orbital speed varies inversely with distance and illustrating the conservation of angular momentum.
- Kepler's third law, the harmonic law, establishes that the square of a planet's orbital period is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit ($P^2 = a^3$), providing a precise mathematical ratio that governs all bodies orbiting the same central [[mass]].
- Sir *Isaac Newton* derived all three empirical laws from first principles by combining his inverse-square law of universal [[gravity]] with his dynamical laws of motion, proving that closed bound orbits under an inverse-square central [[force]] must form conic sections.
- *Galileo Galilei* provided critical observational confirmation of heliocentrism and orbital mechanics in 1610 when his telescope revealed four large moons, including [[europa]], orbiting [[jupiter]], demonstrating that Earth is not the sole center of motion in the cosmos.
- Gravitational perturbations resulting from the mutual attraction between neighboring planets caused discrepancies in the observed orbit of Uranus, allowing mathematicians *Urbain Le Verrier* and *John Couch Adams* to calculate the existence and exact location of Neptune prior to its visual discovery.
- Classical Newtonian mechanics failed to explain an anomalous precession of forty-three arcseconds per century in the perihelion of Mercury, a puzzle finally resolved when [[albert-einstein]] showed that spacetime curvature near the Sun accounts for the exact relativistic advance.

## connections

- [[gravity]] — the fundamental attractive interaction that governs celestial orbits and holds planetary systems together.
- [[newton-laws-of-motion]] — the dynamical laws used to derive Kepler's empirical observations analytically.
- [[jupiter]] — the massive gas giant whose satellite system supplied early observational proof of non-geocentric orbits.
- [[europa]] — a Galilean moon whose orbital dynamics and tidal heating stem from gravitational resonance.
- [[albert-einstein]] — the theoretical physicist whose field equations resolved classical discrepancies in Mercury's orbit.
- [[general-relativity]] — the geometric theory of gravitation explaining orbital paths as geodesics in curved spacetime.
- [[renaissance]] — the historical era during which Copernicus and Kepler dismantled ancient geocentric cosmology.
- [[ancient-greece]] — the classical culture whose geocentric models and epicycles dominated astronomy until Kepler.
