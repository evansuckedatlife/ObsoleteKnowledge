## summary

**Celsius**, historically known as the *centigrade* scale, is an internationally standardized temperature scale and unit of measurement based on a hundred-degree interval between the freezing and boiling points of water. Formulated in 1742 by Swedish astronomer and physicist *Anders Celsius*, the scale originally featured an inverted graduation—assigning zero degrees to the boiling point and one hundred degrees to the freezing point—to avoid dealing with negative values during bitter Scandinavian winters. Inverted to its familiar modern orientation shortly after its creator's death, the scale became a cornerstone of the metric system established during the European [[enlightenment]] and French Revolution. Today, the **degree Celsius** is officially categorized as an SI-derived unit defined relative to the kelvin, serving as the universal standard for meteorological, clinical, and everyday thermal reporting across the globe.

## you gotta know

- Devised in 1742 by Uppsala University astronomy professor *Anders Celsius*, who calibrated his scale against two stable physical phase transitions: the melting point of snow and the boiling point of pure water under a constant barometric pressure of twenty-nine inches of mercury.
- Originally constructed as an inverted thermometer scale where zero degrees marked the boiling point of water and one hundred degrees marked the freezing point, an arrangement designed to eliminate minus signs in subzero meteorological observations.
- Inverted to the contemporary orientation—zero degrees for freezing and one hundred degrees for boiling—in the mid-1740s, an adaptation widely attributed to botanist *Carl Linnaeus* and Celsius's astronomical successor *Mårten Strömer*.
- Participated directly in the 1736 French Geodesic Mission to Lapland led by *Pierre Louis Maupertuis*, which measured a degree of meridian arc near the Arctic Circle to confirm *Isaac Newton*'s prediction that Earth is an oblate spheroid, successfully disproving the prolate Earth model championed by *Jacques Cassini* of the Paris Observatory.
- Adopted the designation "centigrade"—derived from the Latin roots *centum* for hundred and *gradus* for step—until 1948, when the 9th General Conference on Weights and Measures officially renamed the unit the **Celsius** to resolve nomenclature conflicts and honor its originator.
- Serves as the standard thermal metric for monitoring temperature-sensitive biological mutations in laboratory settings, such as yeast cell cycle arrest experiments in [[cdh1-protein]] pathways typically shifted between twenty-five and thirty-six degrees.
- Formally defined within the International System of Units (SI) such that a temperature interval of one degree Celsius is exactly equivalent to one kelvin, with absolute zero fixed precisely at minus 273.15 degrees.
- Transitioned slowly into widespread civilian use across Europe, as Protestant Sweden wrestled with calendar and measurement adjustments long after neighboring [[catholic-countries]] had synchronized their own scientific institutions.
