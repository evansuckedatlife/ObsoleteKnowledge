## summary

**Prophecy** is the mythological and religious phenomenon of divine revelation—the utterance of hidden knowledge about future events, typically delivered by a god to a mortal through inspired intermediaries such as oracles and seers. More than mere prediction, prophecy in ancient cultures represents a direct channel of communication between the divine and human realms, often shrouded in riddling language or cryptic symbolism. The paradox of prophecy underpins countless myths: to learn one's destined future through prophecy is often to set in motion the very actions that fulfill it.

## you gotta know

- In Greek mythology, the oracle at *Delphi*, priestess of *Apollo*, was the most revered source of prophecy, consulted by kings and commoners alike seeking guidance about their futures.
- Prophecies are frequently delivered in obscure or riddling language, which mortals often misinterpret in ways that guarantee the prophecy's fulfillment—the gods' knowledge remains hidden until it is too late.
- The archetype of futile resistance to prophecy appears in *Oedipus*, whose parents tried to prevent his fated crimes by abandoning him as an infant, yet their very action set in motion the events the prophecy foretold.
- In Egyptian and Mesopotamian religions, priests and ritual specialists interpreted omens (animal entrails, star patterns, dreams) as divine messages revealing the will of the gods.
- The Fates themselves were thought to speak through oracles, making prophecy the mortal means of learning what even the gods cannot change.
- Hebrew prophets like *Isaiah* and *Jeremiah* claimed direct communication from the God of Israel, delivering messages of judgment, redemption, and divine will to the people.
- Norse mythology features the völva, a seeress whose prophecies foretold even the gods' doom at *Ragnarök*, binding all beings to an inescapable destiny.
- Prophecy's central irony: foreknowledge does not prevent fate but often becomes the mechanism through which fate is fulfilled.
