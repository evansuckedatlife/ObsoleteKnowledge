## summary

**Jacob Grimm** (1785–1863) and **Wilhelm Grimm** (1786–1859) were two German brothers whose collection and curation of European fairy tales became the canonical foundation for the Western fairy-tale tradition. Their *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* ("Children's and Household Tales"), first published in 1812, transformed oral folktales into a standardized, curated collection that shaped literature, theater, ballet, and music for centuries. The Brothers Grimm demonstrated that folk narratives were worthy of serious scholarly and artistic attention.

## you gotta know

- Born in Hanau, Germany, in the wake of Enlightenment intellectual ferment; studied law but shifted to philology and folklore.
- Collected tales from peasants, friends, and other oral sources across the Harz region and beyond—not pure folklore studies but curated literary texts.
- Published *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* in 1812 with just 86 tales; the collection grew to over 200 stories across multiple editions and translations throughout their lives.
- Each new edition was revised to make stories more literary and "proper"—softening violence, adding morals, Germanizing names—a practice that shaped how fairy tales were received across Europe.
- Included now-iconic tales: "Hansel and Gretel," "Snow White," "Cinderella," "Rapunzel," "Little Red Riding Hood," "Sleeping Beauty," and "Rumpelstiltskin."
- Jacob was the primary linguist and academic scholar; Wilhelm the more empathetic editor and storyteller—their partnership balanced intellectual rigor with narrative grace.
- Influenced virtually every later fairy-tale adaptation, from ballet libretti to musical theater, because their standardized texts became the reference versions taught in schools.
