## summary

The **Imperial Ballet** was the state ballet company of Imperial Russia, centered at the Mariinsky Theatre in St. Petersburg (and later Moscow), that became the epicenter of classical ballet development in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Under choreographers like *Marius Petipa* and in collaboration with composer *Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky*, it created the canonical full-length ballets—*Swan Lake*, *The Sleeping Beauty*, *The Nutcracker*—that remain the foundation of the global classical ballet repertoire. The institution's aesthetic principles, techniques, and repertoire defined what classical ballet would become worldwide.

## you gotta know

- Established as Russia's court ballet in the 18th century, it evolved into a state institution with formal training, hierarchy, and canonical repertoire by the 19th century.
- Located primarily at the Mariinsky Theatre (originally known as the Imperial Theatre) in St. Petersburg, it became synonymous with classicism and technical perfection.
- *Marius Petipa*, serving as Ballet Master for over 60 years, created the choreographic vocabulary and full-length ballet template that still governs classical ballet today.
- Collaborated with *Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky* on three masterworks: *Swan Lake* (1895, revised after Petipa's initial 1877 contribution), *The Sleeping Beauty* (1890), and *The Nutcracker* (1892)—the holy trinity of ballet repertoire.
- Trained dancers using the *Cecchetti method*, a rigorous codification of technique that standardized classical ballet training across Russia and influenced schools worldwide.
- The company's aesthetic emphasized *verticality*, *turnout*, *extension*, and aristocratic restraint—an ideal of the human body as instrument of precision and grace that contrasted with the emerging modern dance aesthetic.
- Dancers at the Imperial Ballet achieved god-like status in Russian society; the company was a symbol of imperial prestige and artistic achievement.
- Though dissolved during the Russian Revolution, its legacy persisted in the Kirov Ballet (Soviet successor) and continues in Mariinsky Ballet productions today.
