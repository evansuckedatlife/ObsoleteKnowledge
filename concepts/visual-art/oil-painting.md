## summary

**Oil painting** is the technique of applying pigments mixed with linseed or other drying oils to canvas, wood panel, or other surfaces. Developed in early Renaissance Flanders and perfected across Europe, oil became the dominant medium of Western art for over five centuries. The medium's slow drying time allows for blending, layering, glazing, and revisions impossible with tempera or fresco; its richness of color and luminosity lends itself to subtle modeling and psychological depth. From *Jan van Eyck* through the old masters to contemporary practice, oil painting remains synonymous with fine art itself, valued for its material permanence, expressive range, and capacity to render both precise detail and atmospheric effect.

## you gotta know

- Developed in Flanders in the early 15th century; *Jan van Eyck* is often credited as a pioneer, though the technique likely evolved gradually.
- Replaces tempera (egg-based) as the dominant medium by the 16th century due to its superior handling, blending, and luminosity.
- Composed of pigment suspended in drying oil (typically linseed); the slow drying enables reworking, layering, and glazing.
- Allows for unprecedented color saturation, subtle transitions (like [[sfumato]]), and the modeling of light and shadow ([[chiaroscuro]]).
- Technical mastery required: understanding of underpainting, layering (fat over lean), glazing, and the chemical properties of drying oils and pigments.
- The material medium of choice for the Renaissance, Baroque, Romantic, and much of Modernism; associated with high art and fine craftsmanship.
- Oil's environmental toxicity and long drying time have led to increasing use of acrylics and other synthetic media in contemporary practice, yet oil remains culturally prestigious.
