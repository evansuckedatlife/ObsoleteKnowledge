## summary

The **cinématographe** was an ingenious three-in-one apparatus patented in 1895 by the French brothers *Auguste and Louis Lumière* that operated as a camera, film printer, and projection device. Unlike *Thomas Edison's* bulky and solitary *Kinetoscope* peephole viewer, this lightweight, hand-cranked mechanism projected moving images onto a large screen before a shared collective audience. Its successful public debut in Paris inaugurated commercial cinema as a communal medium and launched the historical development of projected screen motion pictures.

## you gotta know

- Patented in *France* by *Auguste and Louis Lumière* in February 1895, utilizing an intermittent claw mechanism inspired by the feed mechanism of sewing machines.
- Hosted its legendary first commercial public screening on December 28, 1895, in the *Salon Indien du Grand Café* on the *Boulevard des Capucines* in Paris before thirty-three paying spectators.
- Weighed approximately sixteen pounds and required no electricity, allowing the *Lumière* operators to transport it outdoors worldwide and establish the earliest form of documentary film production.
- Operated at an economical exposure rate of sixteen frames per second on thirty-five-millimeter perforated celluloid film, which became an early international motion picture standard.
- Screened foundational actualities at the debut presentation, including *Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory*, *The Waterer Watered* (an early narrative gag comedy), and *Baby's Breakfast*.
- Screened *Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat* in early 1896, creating an enduring cultural legend that spectators recoiled in panic fearing the oncoming locomotive would burst through the screen.
- Attracted spectator *Georges Méliès*, who offered to purchase the machine on opening night; when the brothers refused because they considered moving images a passing scientific novelty, *Méliès* built his own camera to invent cinematic illusionism.
- Served directly as the conceptual ancestor for all subsequent screen-based projection, transitioning visual storytelling from private parlor novelties to public mass spectacle.

## connections

- [[cinematic-technique]] — originated from the fixed camera perspectives, natural lighting, and staging in depth captured by the apparatus.
- [[france]] — served as the country of origin where the invention was patented, developed, and first exhibited commercially.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — yielded the chemical celluloid stock, precision gear manufacturing, and rapid urban transit depicted in early actualities.
- [[modernism]] — found in the projected moving image its defining technological medium for capturing temporal fragmentation and kinetic velocity.
- [[realism]] — embodied the machine's initial aesthetic commitment to documenting unstaged workers, arriving trains, and street life without artifice.
- [[chocolat]] — stands in a French cinematic lineage that began with the Lumière actualities and evolved into contemplative postcolonial screen narratives.
