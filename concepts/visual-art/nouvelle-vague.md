## summary

The **Nouvelle Vague** (French New Wave) was a revolutionary film movement of the late 1950s and 1960s that rejected studio-bound convention and dramatic narrative artifice in favor of improvisation, handheld cinematography, and philosophical inquiry. Directors like *François Truffaut* and *Jean-Luc Godard* pioneered *cinéma vérité* (cinema truth)—using location shooting, long takes, jump cuts, and direct address to the camera to blur boundaries between documentary and fiction. The New Wave reasserted cinema as artistic expression and transformed filmmaking globally, proving that intellectual rigor and aesthetic innovation could emerge from low budgets and skepticism of industry conventions.

## you gotta know

- Emerged in Paris, driven by young critics at *Cahiers du cinéma* who became filmmakers, theorizing their own practice.
- Rejected studio conventions: no elaborate sets, no big budgets, no famous stars—handheld cameras and real locations sufficed.
- Used innovative editing: jump cuts, discontinuous sound, direct address to camera broke narrative illusion deliberately.
- Often plotless or anti-narrative: long philosophical digressions, conversations about love and politics, mood over plot.
- *François Truffaut's* *The 400 Blows* (1959) and *Jean-Luc Godard's* *Breathless* (1960) were canonical works.
- Valorized director as auteur—the theory that a film's style and vision should bear the director's unmistakable signature.
- Influenced filmmakers worldwide: the New Wave became a model for artistic cinema outside Hollywood's industrial system.
- Pioneered the concept of the director as auteur—the theory that a film's artistic vision and technical signature bear the unmistakable mark of one controlling consciousness.

## connections

- [[jean-luc-godard]] — the movement's most intellectually provocative figure, pushing cinema toward pure abstraction and politics.
- [[francois-truffaut]] — the humanist co-founder whose lyrical films celebrated memory, loss, and romantic yearning.
- [[breathless]] — Godard's breakthrough film, defining New Wave style with jump cuts and location shooting.
- [[silent-film]] — the artistic cinema tradition from which the New Wave drew theoretical inspiration.
- [[france]] — the postwar nation whose intellectual culture gave the movement philosophical seriousness.
- [[auteur-theory]] — the critical framework that elevated directors as artists rather than studio employees.
- [[world-war-ii]] — the postwar moment of French cultural renewal and skepticism toward authority that motivated the movement.
