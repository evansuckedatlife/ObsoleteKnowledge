## summary

**Idealization** is the artistic practice of depicting subjects according to preconceived aesthetic standards of perfection, harmony, and proportion rather than replicating the idiosyncratic flaws of nature. First systematically theorized in *Classical Antiquity* across [[ancient-greece]] and the [[roman-empire]], it emerged as the guiding visual philosophy of Western representation, establishing canonical proportions for the human figure in [[sculpture]] and [[painting]]. Its resurgence during the [[italian-renaissance]] championed intellectual beauty over raw observation, profoundly shaping academic art theory until the modern era.

## you gotta know

- *Michelangelo* embodied High Renaissance idealization in his marble [[sculpture]] *David*, choosing heroic, oversized proportions, classical restraint, and anatomically perfect musculature over a realistic depiction of an adolescent shepherd.
- When criticized for portraying the Virgin Mary as remarkably youthful beside the adult *Christ* in the *Pietà*, *Michelangelo* defended his choice by asserting that divine purity and chastity preserved eternal youth, subordinating physical realism to spiritual perfection.
- *Raphael* codified visual harmony in paintings such as *The School of Athens*, famously declaring in an epistle to *Baldassare Castiglione* that because truly beautiful women were rare in nature, he relied upon a preconceived mental ideal (*un'idea che mi viene alla mente*).
- The classical standard originated in fifth-century BCE [[ancient-greece]] when the sculptor *Polykleitos* formulated the *Canon*, a mathematical treatise and accompanying bronze statue (*Doryphoros*) demonstrating *symmetria*, where every bodily segment maintained an exact harmonic ratio to the whole.
- The [[roman-empire]] appropriated Greek idealism for political iconography in works like the *Augustus of Prima Porta*, deliberate departures from the wrinkled, unsparing *verism* of Republican portraiture designed to cast the emperor as an ageless, godlike commander.
- Classical art theory drew heavily upon the legendary anecdote recorded by *Cicero* and *Pliny the Elder* concerning the Greek painter *Zeuxis*, who created his ideal portrait of *Helen of Troy* by selecting and synthesising the finest individual features from five maidens of *Croton*.
- Academic devotion to ideal beauty was fiercely challenged during the seventeenth century by the raw naturalism of *Caravaggio*, whose depictions of dirt-caked feet and wrinkled saints scandalized patrons, and was definitively dismantled by nineteenth-century movements leading into [[modernism]].

## connections

- [[ancient-greece]] — established the mathematical systems of proportion and philosophical foundations for depicting the perfected human physique.
- [[sculpture]] — served as the primary classical medium through which canonical anatomical perfection and balanced physical harmony were developed.
- [[italian-renaissance]] — revived antique humanist aesthetics, celebrating the divine reflected through harmonized human proportions.
- [[michelangelo]] — sculpted monumental bodies that prioritized expressive heroic perfection and divine youth over naturalistic verisimilitude.
- [[raphael]] — mastered serene pictorial balance and idealized human grace, defining the visual doctrine of the High Renaissance.
- [[leonardo-da-vinci]] — synthesized anatomical observation with geometric proportion, epitomized by his iconic study of the *Vitruvian Man*.
- [[painting]] — adapted classical sculptural ideals into two-dimensional compositions using perspective, sfumato, and balanced figural grouping.
- [[modernism]] — systematically dismantled centuries of academic adherence to classical harmony in favor of raw expression and abstraction.
