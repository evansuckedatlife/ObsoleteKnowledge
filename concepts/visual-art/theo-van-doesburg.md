## summary

**Theo van Doesburg** (1883–1931) was a Dutch painter, architect, and theorist who co-founded *De Stijl*, one of modernism's most radical movements. Through both his artwork and his editorial work on the *De Stijl* journal, van Doesburg became the movement's primary theorist and ambassador, defining its geometrical aesthetics and utopian vision. His influence extended across painting, graphic design, and architecture, making him a pivotal figure in early 20th-century modernism.

## you gotta know

- Born Christiaan Emil Marie Küpper in 1883, adopting the pseudonym "Theo van Doesburg" for his artistic career.
- Founded *De Stijl* in 1917 with *Piet Mondrian* and others, establishing the journal as the movement's intellectual center.
- Developed *De Stijl's* core aesthetic principles: reduction to primary colors (red, blue, yellow) plus black, white, and gray; geometric abstraction; and the elimination of perspective.
- Served as editor of the *De Stijl* journal from its inception, commissioning and promoting the works of artists, architects, and designers aligned with the movement's vision.
- Created the "Elementarism" variation of *De Stijl* in the mid-1920s, introducing diagonal lines to challenge the purely horizontal-vertical orthogonal aesthetic.
- Designed graphics, paintings, and architectural concepts that unified art and practical design, embodying the modernist belief that geometric abstraction could transform society.
- Collaborated with the Bauhaus and other avant-garde institutions, spreading *De Stijl's* influence across Europe during the 1920s.
- Died unexpectedly at age 47, just as *De Stijl* was fragmenting, but left an intellectual legacy that shaped abstract art, graphic design, and architecture for decades.
- His writings and manifestos were as influential as his art; he articulated the philosophical basis for geometric abstraction and the belief that radical formal reduction could serve human liberation.
- Van Doesburg's work bridged multiple avant-garde traditions, connecting Dutch modernism with the Bauhaus, Russian Constructivism, and Dadaist movements.
