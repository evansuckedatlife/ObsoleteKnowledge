## summary

**Neoclassical architecture** is the application of Enlightenment ideals and classical Greek and Roman architectural principles to building design, principally from the mid-18th century through the 19th century. Reacting against the ornate Baroque and Rococo styles, neoclassical architects—including *Jacques-Germain Soufflot*, *Robert Adam*, and later American architects—returned to the orders (Doric, Ionic, Corinthian), symmetry, proportion, and restraint of antiquity. The style became the visual embodiment of Enlightenment rationality, democratic virtue, and national identity, shaping government buildings, public monuments, and civic spaces from Paris to Washington, D.C. Neoclassical architecture asserts that beauty derives from reason, clarity, and adherence to universal principles rather than ornamental excess.

## you gotta know

- Emerged in the mid-18th century as a conscious reaction against Baroque and Rococo excess and frivolity.
- Grounded in archaeological interest in actual Greek and Roman buildings, not the Renaissance's imaginative revival of classicism.
- Emphasized the three Greek orders (Doric, Ionic, Corinthian) as the fundamental vocabulary of design; symmetry, proportion, and geometric clarity.
- Favored simple, rational plans: rectangular façades, colonnaded porticoes, pediments, clean lines, minimal ornament.
- Became the official style of revolutionary governments and new democracies: France, United States, and later across Europe and the Americas.
- Major examples include the *Panthéon* in Paris, the *U.S. Capitol*, *Lincoln Memorial*, and countless state houses and civic buildings.
- Later evolved into *Greek Revival* (c. 1825–1860), especially in America and Britain, which more narrowly adhered to Greek rather than Roman forms.
