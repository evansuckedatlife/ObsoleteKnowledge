## summary

**Photography** emerged in the 1820s–40s as a mechanical process for capturing images through light and chemistry, transforming visual representation from drawn, painted, or sculpted craft into technical reproduction. By fixing light on paper or glass, photography challenged painting's monopoly on depicting reality and established a new artistic medium with its own aesthetic and conceptual possibilities. The technology became foundational to modern visual culture and raised enduring questions about the relationship between mechanical reproduction and artistic truth.

## you gotta know

- Photography was invented in the 1820s–30s through experiments by multiple figures (*Nicéphore Niépce*, *Louis Daguerre*, *William Fox Talbot*) working independently to capture light chemically on surfaces.
- The *daguerreotype* (1839) was the first commercially viable photographic process—producing sharp, detailed silver-copper plates that made portrait photography accessible and popular almost immediately.
- Photography displaced painting as the primary vehicle for literal representation and portraiture; painters responded by abandoning strict realism and exploring subjective color, form, and emotion.
- The medium united opposites: mechanical objectivity (the camera records what is there) and artistic subjectivity (the photographer frames, lights, and composes); this tension became central to photographic aesthetics.
- Early photographers adopted painting's compositional language, framing, and subjects to claim artistic legitimacy; gradual acceptance came as photographers developed distinctly photographic techniques (unusual angles, cropping, depth of field).
- Photography enabled new ways of seeing—freeze-framing motion, revealing detail invisible to the naked eye, flattening three-dimensional space into two dimensions—that artists like *Degas* directly learned from and incorporated into painting.
- The medium fragmented into separate traditions: commercial (portraiture, documentation), scientific (medical, astronomical, archaeological), artistic (fine art photography as modernism).
- Photography's indexical nature (it *records* light rays from the real world rather than representing it) gave the medium philosophical weight—it was literal trace, not interpretation, yet paradoxically subject to manipulation at every stage (exposure, development, printing).

## connections

- [[painting]] — the tradition photography displaced and challenged, but also learned compositional language from.
- [[realism]] — photography's aesthetic; its emergence coincided with and enabled realist art movements.
- [[impressionism]] — painters like [[degas]] adopted photographic cropping, unusual perspectives, and compositional strategies.
- [[alfred-stieglitz]] — early 20th-century photographer who fought for photography's recognition as fine art through aesthetic innovation.
- [[ansel-adams]] — landscape photographer who demonstrated photography's capacity for expressive vision and technical mastery.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — photography is a direct product of industrial technology and optical chemistry.
- [[perspective]] — photography mechanically enforces single-point perspective, raising questions about objectivity and seeing.
