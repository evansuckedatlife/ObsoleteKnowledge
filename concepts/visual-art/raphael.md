## summary

**Raphael** (Raffaello Sanzio, 1483–1520) was an Italian Renaissance master painter and architect whose harmonious compositions, graceful figures, and mastery of perspective and color made him one of the three greatest figures of the *High Renaissance*, alongside *Leonardo da Vinci* and *Michelangelo*. Working primarily in Rome under papal patronage, Raphael decorated the Vatican Palace with frescoes including the iconic *School of Athens*, revolutionized portrait painting, and demonstrated that emotional and intellectual depth could be achieved through clarity and balance rather than Leonardo's sfumato or Michelangelo's dramatic power.

## you gotta know

- Raphael was born in Urbino, a center of Renaissance learning; his father was a painter and court official, and Raphael trained under *Perugino*, absorbing the Umbrian school's emphasis on clear forms and serene composition.
- Pope *Julius II* hired Raphael in 1508 to decorate the papal apartments (the *Stanze di Raffaello*) in the Vatican; over the next 12 years, Raphael and his workshop painted vast frescoes covering the walls and ceilings, the most famous being the *School of Athens*.
- *The School of Athens* (1511) depicts *Plato* and *Aristotle* at the center of a gathering of ancient philosophers arranged in perfect geometric harmony; the fresco showcases Raphael's mastery of linear perspective, foreshortening, and psychological portraiture—each philosopher has a distinct, believable character.
- Raphael's approach balanced *Leonardo*'s empirical precision and sfumato (soft, smoky transitions) with *Michelangelo*'s sculptural musculature and dramatic energy; his style favored clarity, symmetry, and grace—a synthesis rather than an idiosyncratic genius.
- He revolutionized *portrait painting*, creating dignified, psychologically penetrating likenesses that influenced portraiture for centuries; his *Portrait of a Young Woman* (often called *La Fornarina*) is celebrated for its delicate rendering and human presence.
- Raphael was also an architect: he redesigned the façade of the Palazzo Madama, served as architect of St. Peter's Basilica after *Donato Bramante*'s death, and planned urban spaces in Rome—his architectural vision emphasized classical proportion and harmony.
- He died unexpectedly at age 37, likely from a fever; his early death ended a career that, though brief, had already established him as Christendom's leading artistic authority—his pupils and followers spread his influence across Europe for generations.
- Raphael's workshop (bottega) was enormous and highly organized; his ability to delegate while maintaining a unified aesthetic made him prolific—he managed multiple major commissions simultaneously while training dozens of assistants, establishing the model of the Renaissance workshop as a creative factory.
