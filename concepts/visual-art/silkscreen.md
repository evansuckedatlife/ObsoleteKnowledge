## summary

**Silkscreen**, also termed **screenprinting** or **serigraphy**, is a stenciling printmaking method in which ink is forced through a fine woven fabric mesh onto a paper, canvas, or textile substrate. Originating from ancient stenciling traditions and mechanized during the [[industrial-revolution]], the technique transitioned from commercial sign-painting into a prominent fine-art medium during the twentieth century in the [[united-states]]. By facilitating serial repetition, mechanical uniformity, and bold expanses of saturated color, it collapsed traditional boundaries between industrial fabrication and original artwork, serving as the foundational printing technology for [[pop-art]].

## you gotta know

- *Andy Warhol* made the commercial technique his defining artistic signature in [[new-york-city]], opening [[the-factory]] to manufacture serialized, grid-based repetitions of mass-market consumer commodities like [[campbell-soup-cans]] and celebrity portraits of [[marilyn-monroe]].
- Operates by forcing viscous ink across a porous screen fabric—traditionally woven silk and later synthetic polyester or nylon mesh—using a rubber-bladed squeegee to push pigment through stencil apertures while impermeable emulsion shields non-image areas.
- *Robert Rauschenberg* introduced photographic silkscreens into avant-garde studio art in the early 1960s, layering found newsprint imagery and painterly brushwork to synthesize [[abstract-expressionism]] with the media-saturated landscape of the [[cold-war]].
- Emerged into modern industrial use during the early twentieth century when commercial sign-painters in [[england]] and the United States patented squeegee apparatuses and multicolor registration techniques for retail advertisements.
- The designation *serigraphy* was coined in the 1930s by art historian *Carl Zigrosser* and artists of the Works Progress Administration to differentiate original, aesthetic printmaking from commercial industrial sign production.
- Served as an essential graphic medium for countercultural political movements, enabling rapid, low-cost dissemination of protest posters during the May 1968 worker-student strikes in [[france]] and rally placards for the [[civil-rights-movement]].
- Adopted photochemical stencils sensitized with bichromate or diazo compounds, permitting the direct transfer of photographic negatives onto mesh and allowing halftone dot patterns to replicate photographic imagery without manual engraving.

## connections

- [[pop-art]] — the mid-twentieth-century aesthetic movement that elevated commercial screenprinting into a central fine-art medium.
- [[the-factory]] — the industrial Manhattan workspace where Warhol operated mass-production silkscreen printing lines.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — the historical era of technological manufacturing that spurred modern squeegee stenciling, synthetic pigments, and mechanized production.
- [[cold-war]] — the geopolitical era whose consumer abundance, space race rivalries, and mass-media distribution provided key silkscreen motifs.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — the historic struggle that leveraged rapid, low-cost screenprinted placards and posters for grassroots mobilization across America.
- [[new-york-city]] — the postwar cultural capital where commercial print shops directly intersected with the avant-garde gallery scene.
- [[abstract-expressionism]] — the dominant painterly school of expressive gesture that silkscreen artists challenged with flat, mechanized surfaces.
- [[painting]] — the classical fine-art discipline radically transformed when artists integrated manual brushwork with mechanical ink transfer.
