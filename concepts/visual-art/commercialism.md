## summary

Catalyzed by the rapid expansion of mechanized production, mass media, and corporate capitalism following the [[industrial-revolution]], **commercialism** in art reflects both the economic commodification of aesthetic objects and the artistic appropriation of advertising, branding, and consumer culture. Historically derided by academic institutions as utilitarian **commercial art**, the mechanics of consumerism were directly embraced by postwar avant-gardes to confront everyday postwar reality. By simultaneously mimicking, exalting, and critiquing mass marketing, artists permanently dismantled the traditional hierarchy dividing high culture from commercial low culture.

## you gotta know

- Andy Warhol eliminated the divide between corporate marketing and fine art by naming his studio *The Factory*, where he employed silkscreen printing to replicate consumer goods including *Campbell's Soup Cans*, *Coca-Cola* bottles, and *Brillo Boxes*.
- Pop painter Roy Lichtenstein appropriated comic strips and commercial print ads, mimicking industrial *Ben-Day dots* through meticulous hand-painting to scrutinize mechanical reproduction and romantic notions of artistic touch.
- Cultural theorist Walter Benjamin provided a foundational diagnosis of modern commercial culture in his 1935 essay *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction*, arguing that industrial replication degrades an artwork's singular, authentic "aura."
- Many prominent twentieth-century innovators began their careers in applied commercial design, including Warhol in department store shoe advertising, James Rosenquist painting towering outdoor billboards, and Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec creating lithographic advertising posters in [[france]].
- Claes Oldenburg engaged directly with commercial salesmanship in 1961 by opening *The Store* in [[new-york-city]], selling plaster, paint-spattered sculptures of cheap consumer items like diner food and clothing directly to gallery patrons.
- During the 1980s art market boom, Neo-Geo sculptors like Jeff Koons took consumer fascination to its apex, sealing brand-new *Hoover* vacuum cleaners inside pristine illuminated display cases and manufacturing luxury stainless-steel casts like *Rabbit*.
- Japanese artist Takashi Murakami codified the synthesis of fine art and consumer merchandising under his *Superflat* movement, creating high-profile retail collaborations with fashion houses like Louis Vuitton alongside major international museum exhibitions.
- Despite early hopes within [[conceptual-art]] to escape corporate commercialism by generating ephemeral, uncollectible ideas, art dealers promptly developed market systems to price, trade, and authenticate conceptual certificates and photo documentation.

## connections

- [[pop-art]] — postwar movement that placed supermarket commodities, mass media celebrities, and commercial advertisement formats at the center of fine art.
- [[conceptual-art]] — artistic tendency that tried to bypass market commercialism by dematerializing physical works into pure proposals and temporary actions.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — historical era that instituted modern mass manufacturing, urban consumer markets, and mechanized print media.
- [[modernism]] — cultural period defined by intense friction between the pursuit of pure aesthetic autonomy and the rise of commercial mass culture.
- [[new-york-city]] — financial and cultural capital housing both the global advertising industry of Madison Avenue and the dominant blue-chip commercial gallery market.
- [[american-realism]] — early twentieth-century style whose painters observed department store displays, cinema houses, and commercial streetscapes across American cities.
- [[color-in-art]] — formal discipline directly altered by commercial enterprise through synthetic chemical pigment development, neon lighting, and industrial CMYK process printing.
