## summary

**Geometric Abstraction** is a modernist approach that abandons figuration entirely in favor of pure geometric forms—lines, planes, circles, and grids—organized according to mathematical relationships or rational principles. Emerging in the early 20th century, it expressed faith in reason, universal order, and the spiritual power of non-objective form, becoming foundational to both fine art and applied design.

## you gotta know

- Emerged independently across Europe (*Mondrian* in the Netherlands, *Kandinsky* in Russia, *Malevich* in the Soviet Union) around 1910–1920, suggesting a collective spiritual hunger.
- Rooted in the belief that abstract geometric forms communicate universal meaning more directly than representation of visible reality; geometry was spiritually pure and transcendent.
- *Malevich's* *Black Square* (1915) is the radical gesture: the complete reduction to a single form, renouncing all narrative and illusion.
- Employs primary colors (red, yellow, blue), black, and white arranged in grids, asymmetrical balance, and carefully proportioned compositions reflecting cosmic order.
- Influenced by Cubism's formal reduction but pushing further—not fragmenting observed reality but creating entirely non-representational worlds with internal mathematical logic.
- Central to the Constructivist and De Stijl movements, which saw geometric abstraction as the visual language of revolutionary modernism and universal human harmony.
- Extended into architecture, typography, and industrial design, where geometric rationality promised functional efficiency and democratic accessibility; *Mondrian* saw his grids as formal solutions to chaos.
- Aspiration to universal truth through abstraction: geometric forms were seen as a language beyond culture, nationality, or linguistic contingency; the purest art possible.

## connections

- [[abstract-art]] — the parent category of all non-objective art.
- [[kandinsky]] — pioneer of pure abstraction through color and geometric form.
- [[piet-mondrian]] — the master of geometric abstraction who perfected reduction to primary colors and orthogonal lines.
- [[de-stijl]] — the Dutch movement that systematized geometric abstraction as utopian design philosophy.
- [[constructivism]] — the Soviet equivalent that weaponized geometric forms for revolutionary propaganda.
- [[art-deco]] — adopted geometric vocabulary for commercial and decorative purposes.
- [[bauhaus]] — school that unified geometric abstraction with functionalist design principles.
