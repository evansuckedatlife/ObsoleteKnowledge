## summary

**Ernst Ludwig Kirchner** (1880–1938) was a German Expressionist painter and printmaker who founded Die Brücke and became the movement's most celebrated and innovative figure. His angular, distorted figures rendered in acidic, emotionally charged colors became the visual signature of German Expressionism itself. Kirchner's work obsessively depicted urban life, street scenes, and psychological alienation—themes he knew intimately through his own mental anguish, which led to a breakdown in 1915 and lifelong psychiatric struggles. His paintings and woodcuts merge formal innovation with psychological intensity, making inner turbulence visible through fractured forms and clashing hues. Despite Nazi persecution of his work as "degenerate," Kirchner's legacy remains the exemplar of Expressionist power and pathos.

## you gotta know

- Founded Die Brücke in 1905 in Dresden, gathering like-minded young artists around a program of modernist revolution and emotional intensity.
- Developed a signature style of elongated, angular figures rendered in non-naturalistic, emotionally saturated color.
- Obsessively painted Berlin street scenes, dancers, circus performers, and prostitutes—subjects conveying urban alienation and psychological unease.
- Suffered a severe mental breakdown in 1915 (possibly trauma-related) and spent much of his later life in psychiatric treatment and isolation.
- Mastered both painting and printmaking (woodcut, lithography), using the expressive potential of each medium to convey emotional truths.
- His work was branded "degenerate" by the Nazis, who removed his paintings from German museums and burned some pieces.
- Emigrated to Switzerland after Nazi persecutions; his legacy was rehabilitated post-WWII as central to Modernism's emotional and formal revolutions.
