## summary

**American Regionalism** was a major realist modern art movement that flourished across the *[[united-states]]* during the 1930s, specifically responding to the economic devastation of the *[[great-depression]]*. Rejecting European abstraction and urban elitism, Regionalist painters celebrated indigenous rural landscapes, agrarian labor, folklore, and small-town American heritage. Its iconic imagery forged an accessible, reassuring national visual culture that dominated public imagination until the rise of *[[abstract-expressionism]]* following *[[world-war-ii]]*.

## you gotta know

- Spearheaded by the celebrated "Regionalist Triumvirate" of Midwestern painters: *[[grant-wood]]* of Iowa, *Thomas Hart Benton* of Missouri, and *John Steuart Curry* of Kansas.
- *[[grant-wood]]*'s 1930 masterpiece *American Gothic*, depicting a stern farmer and his daughter before a Carpenter Gothic farmhouse, stands as one of the most recognized icons in American art history.
- Directly resisted European *[[modernism]]*, *[[cubism]]*, and avant-garde abstraction in favor of naturalistic figuration, crisp draftsmanship, and localized narrative storytelling.
- *Thomas Hart Benton* championed dynamic, swirling figurative murals depicting muscular laborers, industrial progress, and American folklore, notably in murals such as *A Social History of the State of Missouri*.
- Flourished under federal patronage programs of the New Deal, especially the Works Progress Administration (WPA) and the Federal Art Project, which commissioned post office murals nationwide.
- Formed part of the broader *American Scene* painting tendency, which also encompassed urban social realists who depicted city street life and labor struggles.
- Faded from critical favor in the late 1940s as Cold War cultural policy embraced *[[abstract-expressionism]]* and the avant-garde centered in *[[new-york-city]]*.

## connections

- [[american-realism]] — the broader realist tradition in American painting from which Regionalism developed.
- [[grant-wood]] — the key painter whose rural Iowan canvases epitomized Regionalist themes and composition.
- [[great-depression]] — the economic crisis that catalyzed artists to seek solace and solidarity in rural American virtues.
- [[painting]] — the primary fine-art medium through which Regionalist landscapes and portraits were created.
- [[united-states]] — the nation whose vernacular heritage, agrarian culture, and landscapes formed the movement's sole subject.
- [[modernism]] — the international avant-garde movement that Regionalist artists consciously opposed and critiqued.
- [[american-symbols]] — Regionalist canvases generated enduring visual emblems of national perseverance and rural identity.
- [[american-monuments]] — shared the public New Deal impulse to monumentalize American history and regional workers.
