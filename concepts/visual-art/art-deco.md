# Art Deco

## summary

**Art Deco** was a design movement that flourished in the 1920s and 1930s, blending modern industrial aesthetics with geometric ornamentation and luxurious materials. It embraced bold symmetry, streamlined forms, and vivid colours, appearing in architecture, furniture, fashion, and graphic design. Art Deco embodied the optimism of the machine age while maintaining a decorative richness that distinguished it from austere Modernism.

## you gotta know

- Emerged after World War I as a celebration of progress, speed, technological advancement, and the optimism of the modern age.
- Named after the 1925 Exposition Internationale des Arts Décoratifs et Industriels Modernes in Paris, the seminal showcase that gave the movement its identity.
- Signature visual elements: chevrons, stepped and stacked forms, sunburst and sunray motifs, geometric borders, chrome, lacquer, ebony, and exotic tropical woods.
- Reached its architectural zenith in American skyscrapers, notably the *Chrysler Building* (1930) with its iconic tiered crown, and the *Empire State Building* (1931).
- Drew inspiration from multiple avant-garde sources: Cubism's geometric fragmentation, Futurism's dynamism, Egyptian and Aztec ornament, and machine-age industrial aesthetics.
- Declined after the Great Depression and World War II disrupted manufacture and luxury consumption, though it remains influential in contemporary design and retro aesthetics.
- Distinguished from Modernism by its embrace of decoration and luxury; while Modernists rejected ornament as superfluous, Art Deco integrated it as essential to form.
- Appeared not only in architecture but across the decorative arts: furniture, graphics, textiles, ceramics, jewelry, and everyday objects like radio sets and cigarette cases.
- Art Deco represented a middle path: modern in its engagement with machine aesthetics and mass production, yet luxurious and decorative in ways strict Modernists found contradictory.
- The style served as a visual language for the emerging middle and professional classes, asserting modernity, progress, and cosmopolitan sophistication through design.
