## summary

**Walker Evans** (1903–1975) was an American photographer whose formally composed, unflinching documentation of Depression-era American life became foundational to documentary *photography* as an art form. Working for the Farm Security Administration and independently, Evans captured the dignity and complexity of ordinary Americans facing economic hardship, creating images of stark visual clarity that transcended mere social documentation to achieve universal artistic resonance. His rigorous aesthetic approach redefined how photography could function as both witness and art.

## you gotta know

- Born in 1903, Evans studied literature and art history before turning to photography in the early 1930s, bringing intellectual rigor to the medium.
- Worked for the *Farm Security Administration* (1935–1937), photographing rural poverty, tenant farmers, and Depression-era communities across the American South and Midwest.
- Created meticulously composed photographs of vernacular architecture, signs, objects, and people, treating mundane subjects with formal precision and compositional clarity.
- Published *Let Us Now Praise Famous Men* (1941) with writer James Agee, combining photographs with lyrical prose to document sharecropper families in Alabama—considered one of the great photobooks.
- Adopted a distinctive aesthetic approach emphasizing sharp focus, balanced composition, and direct observation, avoiding the more emotionally dramatic style of contemporaries like *Dorothea Lange*.
- Later worked as a photographer for Fortune magazine and taught at Yale, influencing generations of photographers through both his images and his artistic philosophy.
- Remained committed to photography as a rigorous art form rather than instrumental social documentation, insisting on aesthetic autonomy.
- His work influenced documentary photography worldwide and elevated photography's status as an art form, not merely a recording tool.
- Evans's aesthetic philosophy—that clear, direct observation combined with formal precision could achieve artistic truth—became foundational to modernist photography.
