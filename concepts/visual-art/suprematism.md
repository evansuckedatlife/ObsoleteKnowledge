## summary

**Suprematism** was an avant-garde movement founded by *Kazimir Malevich* around 1913–1915, pursuing pure abstraction through geometric form—circles, squares, lines—divorced from representation, emotion, or utility. Suprematism asserted the "supremacy of pure artistic feeling" over objective reality, treating color and geometric shape as sufficient for profound aesthetic experience. Though short-lived as a unified movement, Suprematism became foundational to twentieth-century abstraction, geometric art, and the idea that painting could abandon mimesis entirely.

## you gotta know

- *Malevich* ([[kazimir-malevich]]) declared that art need not depict anything; a black square on white ground could be a complete philosophical and aesthetic statement.
- Suprematism emerged in Russia during the revolutionary fervor of the 1910s, aligned with utopian ideals of a new society freed from bourgeois convention.
- The movement operated in tension with *Constructivism* ([[constructivism]]): both embraced geometric abstraction, but Suprematism prioritized pure feeling while Constructivism emphasized utility and social function.
- Suprematist compositions typically consisted of simple geometric forms—squares, circles, rectangles, crosses—arranged in dynamic asymmetrical balance.
- Malevich's *Black Square* (1915) became the movement's icon: radical reduction, philosophical audacity, and a claim that art could transcend representation altogether.
- The movement influenced the *Bauhaus* ([[bauhaus]]) and *De Stijl*, spreading its geometric abstraction philosophy across Europe.
- Malevich's late work returned toward figuration, complicating the movement's narrative of pure abstraction.
- Soviet authorities eventually suppressed Suprematism as too individualist; Malevich's late career was constrained by political pressure.

## connections

- [[kazimir-malevich]] — founder and primary theorist of Suprematism.
