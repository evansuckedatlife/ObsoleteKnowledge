## summary

*A Trip to the Moon* (1902) was a 14-minute silent film by French director and magician *Georges Méliès*, considered the founding work of narrative cinema and science-fiction film. Adapted loosely from Jules Verne's *From the Earth to the Moon*, the film depicts a fantastical journey by astronomers to the lunar surface, featuring hand-painted sets, trick photography, and imaginative special effects. Though elementary by modern standards, it crystallized cinema's potential for storytelling, visual fantasy, and technological wonder.

## you gotta know

- Directed and produced by *Georges Méliès*, a theater magician who pioneered the use of camera tricks and practical effects to create illusions on film.
- One of the earliest narrative films with a coherent plot, as opposed to earlier cinema's focus on singular actions or events.
- Featured elaborate hand-painted sets, elaborate costumes, and a large cast of performers, making it one of the most expensive productions of its era.
- Used numerous optical and practical tricks: dissolves, multiple exposures, stop-motion animation, and miniatures to depict fantasy elements.
- The iconic image of a spacecraft crashing into the Man in the Moon's face remains one of cinema's most recognizable moments.
- Distributed internationally and became hugely popular, making Méliès wealthy and famous; it was copied and remade throughout Europe and America.
- Demonstrates early cinema's debt to theater, magic, and spectacle, even as it points toward film's future as a distinct medium.
- Later lost and suppressed; the hand-colored version was rediscovered in the 1990s, allowing modern audiences to see Méliès's intended chromatic vision.
