## summary

The **Chicago School** refers to the pioneering late-nineteenth-century architectural movement centered in Chicago that revolutionized commercial building through the invention and refinement of the steel-frame skyscraper. Emerging in the wake of the Great Chicago Fire of 1871, architects such as *Louis Sullivan*, *Dankmar Adler*, *William Le Baron Jenney*, *John Wellborn Root*, and *Daniel Burnham* abandoned load-bearing masonry in favor of fireproof structural steel, plate-glass curtain walls, and expressive vertical articulation. Their insistence on structural honesty laid the conceptual bedrock for architectural *modernism* and produced the functionalist maxim that form should ever follow function.

## you gotta know

- *Louis Sullivan*, often hailed as the father of modern skyscrapers and mentor to *Frank Lloyd Wright*, coined the celebrated architectural principle that form ever follows function in his 1896 treatise *The Tall Office Building Artistically Considered*.
- *William Le Baron Jenney* engineered the *Home Insurance Building* in 1885, universally recognized as the world's first true steel-frame skyscraper, which supported its height through a fireproof metal skeleton rather than heavy exterior masonry.
- *Sullivan* and *Adler* designed seminal masterpieces including the *Auditorium Building*, the *Guaranty Building* in Buffalo, and the *Wainwright Building* in St. Louis, which established the tripartite vertical schema of base, shaft, and decorative terra-cotta cornice modeled on classical columns.
- The prominent firm *Burnham and Root* created the *Rookery Building* with its iron-framed internal light court and the *Monadnock Building*, celebrated as the tallest load-bearing masonry commercial tower ever constructed.
- Structural innovations pioneered by the movement include the *Chicago window*, a tripartite aperture consisting of a large fixed central pane flanked by two operable double-hung sashes, optimizing natural lighting and ventilation in commercial workspaces.
- Deep, unstable swamp soil beneath the city prompted the invention of the spread foundation or *Chicago caisson*, utilizing grillages of steel rails encased in concrete to evenly distribute immense weight.
- The movement clashed ideologically with the neoclassical *Beaux-Arts* revival promoted by *Daniel Burnham* at the 1893 *World's Columbian Exposition*, an event *Sullivan* bitterly lamented would set back American architectural progress for fifty years.
- The First Chicago School directly nurtured the subsequent development of the *Prairie School* residential architecture championed by *Frank Lloyd Wright*.

## connections

- [[frank-lloyd-wright]] — began his career working in the office of *Adler and Sullivan*, adapting their philosophy of organic design into residential architecture.
- [[modernism]] — adopted the structural clarity, functional expression, and rejection of historicist pastiche formulated by the movement.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — provided the cheap rolled steel, mass-produced plate glass, and steam passenger elevators essential for high-rise construction.
- [[united-states]] — served as the nation whose rapid post-Civil War urbanization and commercial expansion fueled high-density urban skylines.
- [[bauhaus]] — extended the movement's fusion of industrial materials, functional efficiency, and structural transparency into twentieth-century design pedagogy.
- [[le-corbusier]] — drew upon the steel grid and open floor plans pioneered in Chicago when formulating his five points of modern architecture.
- [[cinematic-technique]] — evolved alongside the vertical visual geometry of the modern metropolis, capturing early skyscraper facades with upward camera tilts.
