## summary

**American Consumer Culture** (also known as **consumerism in the United States** or **postwar consumer culture**) refers to the socioeconomic ethos and lifestyle in the *[[united-states]]* where personal identity, leisure, and social standing became organized around the acquisition of mass-produced commodities. Sparked by the technological advances of the *[[industrial-revolution]]* and reaching unprecedented heights following *[[world-war-ii]]*, it flooded daily life with branded packaging, television advertising, and planned obsolescence. This commercial ubiquity provided the critical subject matter and commercial techniques that sparked *[[pop-art]]*, fundamentally transforming global visual arts.

## you gotta know

- Reached an explosive zenith during the 1950s economic boom, propelled by suburban tract housing developments, nationwide highway construction, the creation of revolving credit cards, and ubiquitous television advertising during the *[[cold-war]]*.
- Provided the central iconography for *[[pop-art]]*, most famously elevated by *Andy Warhol*, who repurposed mass-market commodities like Campbell's soup cans, Brillo soap pads, and Coca-Cola bottles into elite gallery spectacles.
- Inspired *Roy Lichtenstein* to mechanically simulate industrial printing methods, specifically commercial Ben-Day dots, magnifying the melodrama of mass-circulation comic strips and marketing circulars.
- Anticipated decades earlier by painters of *[[american-realism]]* such as *Edward Hopper*, whose sparse depictions of department stores, diners, and gas stations illuminated the alienation of urban commercial spaces.
- Directly undermined the introspective, heroic ethos of *[[abstract-expressionism]]* by celebrating the superficial, mass-reproduced, and disposable artifacts of modern department stores and supermarkets.
- Fueled the postwar expansion of *[[american-art-collecting]]*, as corporate collections and newly enriched collectors traded art like speculative commercial commodities.
- Diagnosed by mid-century sociologists and economists such as *John Kenneth Galbraith* in *The Affluent Society* and *Vance Packard* in *The Hidden Persuaders*, who dissected corporate manipulation of public desire.

## connections

- [[pop-art]] — appropriated its commercial iconography, silkscreen mass reproduction, and consumer branding directly into fine art.
- [[united-states]] — provided the capitalist industrial apparatus and suburban wealth that established mass consumption as cultural hegemony.
- [[cold-war]] — utilized consumer abundance and kitchen appliances as ideological proof of Western democratic superiority over communism.
- [[american-art-collecting]] — generated the private wealth and corporate fortunes that financed vast acquisitions of contemporary art.
- [[american-film-noir]] — depicted the corrosive moral rot and criminal tragedy stemming from unbridled material ambition.
- [[american-realism]] — documented early twentieth-century storefronts, advertising billboards, and consumer isolation in metropolitan centers.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — established the mechanized production lines, transportation networks, and retail structures essential for mass consumerism.
