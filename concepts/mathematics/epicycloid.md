## summary

An **epicycloid** is a curve traced by a point on the circumference of a circle rolling around the outside of another fixed circle. When the rolling circle has a diameter equal to the fixed circle's radius, the resulting epicycloid is called a *cardioid* (heart-shaped); other radius ratios produce roses and other spectacular patterns. Epicycloids are essential examples in classical geometry and appear in mechanical systems, orbital dynamics, and decorative mathematics.

## you gotta know

- A special case of a *roulette* — a curve generated by rolling one shape along another.
- The parametric equations depend on the ratio of the rolling circle's radius (denoted r) to the fixed circle's radius (denoted R). If R = r, a *cardioid* results; if R = 2r, a *nephroid* emerges.
- For an epicycloid generated by rolling a circle of radius r outside a fixed circle of radius R, the parametric form is x(θ) = (R + r)cos(θ) − r cos((R + r)θ/r) and y(θ) = (R + r)sin(θ) − r sin((R + r)θ/r).
- The *cardioid* (ratio 1:1) is the most famous epicycloid, resembling an actual heart shape and possessing exactly one cusp.
- A ratio of 2:1 produces a *nephroid*, with exactly two cusps arranged symmetrically.
- Ratios of small integers p:q produce epicycloids with p cusps when traced completely (e.g., 3:1 gives three cusps, 4:1 gives four cusps).
- Used historically by astronomers to explain planetary motion before Newton's gravitational model and Kepler's laws revolutionized orbital mechanics.
- The envelope of cardioids produces further curves exploited in gear design, mechanical cam systems, and optical focusing applications.
- The curvature, arc length, and area enclosed by epicycloids have been studied since ancient times, with applications in mechanical engineering, harmonic motion analysis, and decorative mathematics.
