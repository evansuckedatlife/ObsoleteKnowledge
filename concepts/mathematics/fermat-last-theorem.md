## summary

**Fermat's Last Theorem** states that for any integer n > 2, there exist no three positive integers x, y, z such that x^n + y^n = z^n. *Pierre de Fermat* claimed (without proof) to have solved this around 1637, triggering a 358-year mathematical odyssey. *Andrew Wiles* finally proved it in 1995 using *elliptic curves* and *modular forms*, a deep connection no one anticipated. The theorem exemplifies how a simple-to-state problem can unlock vast new mathematics.

## you gotta know

- Fermat claimed a proof in the margin of Diophantus' *Arithmetica* but left no details ("I have a truly marvelous proof, but this margin is too small").
- For n = 2, the equation x² + y² = z² has infinitely many solutions (*Pythagorean triples* like 3-4-5, 5-12-13); no integer solutions exist for n ≥ 3, making the jump qualitative and profound.
- Leonhard Euler proved the case n = 3 in the 18th century, using infinite descent; he also proved n = 4; Sophie Germain proved infinitely many cases through deep number-theoretic arguments in the 19th century.
- Wiles' 1995 proof (after 7 years of secret work) established a link between elliptic curves and modular forms, proving the *Taniyama-Shimura conjecture*, which immediately implied Fermat's Last Theorem.
- The *Taniyama-Shimura conjecture* (proposed by *Yutaka Taniyama*, *Goro Shimura*, and others) stated that every rational elliptic curve is modular; proving this seemed impossibly hard before Wiles' insight.
- Wiles' proof required over 100 pages of dense algebraic geometry and introduced techniques (especially the modularity lifting theorems) that revolutionized arithmetic geometry and number theory.
- The proof uses deformation theory, Galois representations, and deep results from the theory of automorphic forms—machinery that only became available in the late 20th century.
- Fermat's Last Theorem stands as a monument to mathematical obstinacy: the simplest-to-state hard problem in number theory, solved only through techniques that post-date Fermat by 350+ years.
- The theorem's resolution illustrates how seemingly isolated problems can connect deep mathematical structures, opening entire new fields of research.
