## summary

**David Hilbert** (1862–1943) was a German mathematician whose influence shaped virtually every major area of 20th-century mathematics. He championed rigorous axiomatic foundations, posed the famous 23 Hilbert Problems that guided mathematical research for decades, and created *Hilbert spaces*—a bridge between linear algebra and infinite-dimensional analysis essential to quantum mechanics.

## you gotta know

- Emphasized the *formalist* program: all mathematics should be built on explicit axioms and formal logical rules, avoiding hidden assumptions; this vision shaped how mathematics is practiced today.
- Proposed 23 open mathematical problems (1900) whose solutions drove much of early 20th-century mathematics; many remain relevant, prestigious, and unsolved—some worth million-dollar prizes today.
- Developed invariant theory and showed that every ideal in a polynomial ring is finitely generated (Hilbert basis theorem), unifying a scattered field and establishing the foundation for modern algebra.
- Created Hilbert space (infinite-dimensional inner product spaces) as a rigorous framework for functional analysis and quantum mechanics; Hilbert spaces are indispensable to modern physics and analysis.
- Famously declared "We must know. We will know."—asserting that mathematical truth is knowable despite [[kurt-godel]]'s later incompleteness theorems, reflecting his optimistic philosophical stance.
- Worked across pure mathematics (algebra, number theory, geometry) and applied areas (physics, logic), embodying the unity of mathematical knowledge and inspiring interdisciplinary approaches.
- His mathematical papers and courses profoundly influenced entire generations of mathematicians; his methods and perspectives became standard practice in modern mathematics.
- Hilbert's optimism about mathematics' potential and knowability contrasts with 20th-century developments (Gödel's incompleteness), yet his axiomatic approach remains the foundation of rigorous mathematics despite its limits.
- His contributions span nearly every major field—he contributed to invariant theory, algebraic geometry, number theory, logic, and the foundations of geometry—making him perhaps the last true universal mathematician.
