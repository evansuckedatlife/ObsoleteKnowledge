## summary

**Buoyancy**, mathematically formalized through **Archimedes' principle**, is the net upward force exerted by a static fluid on an object submerged or partially immersed within it, equal in magnitude to the weight of the displaced fluid. First established during the third century BCE in Syracuse in the landmark treatise *On Floating Bodies*, the concept represents the historical foundation of mathematical hydrostatics and the earliest rigorous application of deductive geometry to physical matter. The principle serves as a critical bridge between ancient Hellenistic [[geometry]] and modern continuum [[calculus]], providing the theoretical framework for naval architecture, meteorology, and astrophysics.

## you gotta know

- States that any object wholly or partially immersed in a fluid is buoyed upward by a **buoyant force** equal to the weight of the fluid that the body displaces, expressed compactly as $F_b = \rho g V$.
- Famously popularized in an anecdote recorded by the Roman architect *Vitruvius*, who recounted that *Archimedes* realized water displacement while stepping into a bathing pool to test the gold purity of a votive crown for King *Hiero II*, prompting him to run through Syracuse shouting "*Eureka!*"
- Formulated rigorously without modern algebraic notation in *Archimedes*' two-volume treatise *On Floating Bodies*, which established hydrostatic equilibrium as a consequence of contiguous fluid elements seeking uniform compression.
- Analyzes the rotational stability of paraboloids of revolution in its demanding second book, executing an intricate geometric study of righting moments that anticipated modern marine engineering by nearly two millennia.
- Formally proven in modern vector [[calculus]] by taking the surface integral of pressure over an arbitrary closed boundary and invoking the divergence theorem, reducing the surface traction to a volume integral of the hydrostatic pressure gradient.
- Dictates static equilibrium based on the geometric alignment of the center of gravity and the center of buoyancy—the centroid of the displaced fluid volume—where rotational stability of a floating vessel requires the metacenter to sit above the center of gravity.
- Spurred the invention of the hydrometer to measure the relative density of liquids, an apparatus famously described in letters between the Hellenistic mathematician *Synesius* and his teacher *Hypatia* of Alexandria.

## connections

- [[calculus]] — provides the mathematical apparatus of surface integrals and the divergence theorem required to generalize Archimedean flotation to nonuniform pressure fields.
- [[ancient-greece]] — the cultural and intellectual setting where deductive Hellenistic mathematics flourished and generated the axiomatic foundations of fluid mechanics.
- [[euclid]] — established the synthetic geometric proofs and Euclidean proportion theory that *Archimedes* extended to calculate the volumes and centroids of immersed bodies.
- [[isaac-newton]] — integrated Archimedean hydrostatic buoyancy into classical mechanics within the *Principia*, linking fluid displacement to universal gravitation.
- [[derivative]] — represents the spatial pressure gradient in the Navier-Stokes equations, where hydrostatic balance simplifies to the vertical derivative of pressure balancing fluid weight density.
- [[geometry]] — supplies the spatial formulas used to locate geometric centroids and compute displaced volume segments for arbitrary solids of revolution.
- [[catenary]] — a classic physical curve of statics that, like floating paraboloids, occupied mathematicians transitioning from classical geometry to analytic mechanics.
