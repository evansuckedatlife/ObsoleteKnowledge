## summary

The **Argand plane**, widely known as the **complex plane** or the **Argand diagram**, is a two-dimensional geometric representation that maps complex numbers directly onto a continuous Cartesian coordinate system. In this planar construction, the horizontal axis represents the real component while the perpendicular vertical axis represents the imaginary component of any complex value. Although first formulated in 1799 by the Norwegian cartographer *Caspar Wessel*, it was independently rediscovered and published in 1806 by *Jean-Robert Argand* and later popularized across Europe by *Carl Friedrich Gauss*. By giving geometric reality to imaginary quantities, it provides an indispensable conceptual foundation connecting [[linear-algebra]], the [[exponential-function]], and modern complex analysis.

## you gotta know

- Plots each complex number $z = x + iy$ as a unique point or directed position vector $(x, y)$, establishing the real values along the horizontal axis and the purely imaginary values along the vertical axis.
- Expresses points in polar form using the modulus $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ as radial distance and the argument $\theta$ as the directed angle measured counterclockwise from the positive real axis.
- Interprets complex multiplication geometrically through *Euler's formula*, demonstrating that multiplying two values multiplies their moduli while adding their arguments to produce a combined dilation and rotation.
- Distributes the $n$-th roots of unity symmetrically along the unit circle centered at the origin, forming the vertices of a regular $n$-sided polygon with one vertex situated at the real coordinate $1 + 0i$.
- Forms a two-dimensional real [[vector-space]] that identifies complex addition with standard vector translation according to the classical parallelogram law of Euclidean geometry.
- Published anonymously in 1806 by Parisian bookkeeper *Jean-Robert Argand* in his treatise *Essai sur une manière de représenter les quantités imaginaires*, despite earlier neglected work by *Caspar Wessel* in 1799.
- Undergoes one-point compactification into the extended plane or *Riemann sphere*, where the entire plane is mapped onto a three-dimensional sphere using stereographic projection from the north pole.
- Provides the fundamental geometric setting for contour integration in complex [[calculus]], allowing path integrals along closed planar curves to evaluate real integrals using *Cauchy's integral theorem* and the residue theorem.

## connections

- [[vector-space]] — provides the two-dimensional real linear structure that governs complex addition and scalar multiplication.
- [[trigonometric-function]] — parameterizes the coordinate axes through cosine and sine to convert Cartesian coordinates into polar form.
- [[exponential-function]] — powers Euler's identity to express complex rotation and scaling concisely as exponentials.
- [[linear-algebra]] — models multiplication by the imaginary unit as an orthogonal rotational transformation on planar vectors.
- [[polynomial-function]] — provides the topological canvas on which the fundamental theorem of algebra guarantees roots for all non-constant polynomials.
- [[fourier-series]] — describes periodic functions as superpositions of rotating vectors tracing circular orbits in the plane.
- [[calculus]] — establishes the framework for complex differentiation, conformal mappings, and contour integrals along planar paths.
