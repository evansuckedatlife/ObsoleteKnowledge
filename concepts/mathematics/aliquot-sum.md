## summary

The **aliquot sum**, also termed the **proper divisor sum**, represents the sum of all positive divisors of an integer strictly excluding the number itself. Expressed symbolically as $s(n) = \sigma(n) - n$, where $\sigma(n)$ is the divisor function, the operation originated in the mathematical inquiries of [[ancient-greece]] and remains a foundational concept within modern [[number-theory]]. It provides the classical mechanism for classifying integers as perfect, abundant, or deficient, and serves as the iterative rule for generating aliquot sequences whose long-term asymptotic behavior forms one of the most famous open problems in computational arithmetic.

## you gotta know

- Establishes the classical tripartite classification of integers first codified by *Nicomachus*: an integer $n$ is classified as *perfect* if its aliquot sum equals itself, *abundant* if the sum exceeds the integer, and *deficient* if the sum is strictly less than the integer.
- Maps every prime number to an aliquot sum of exactly 1, making primes the most basic deficient numbers because their only positive proper divisor is unity; prime powers $p^k$ yield proper divisor sums determined by the geometric series formula $(p^k - 1) / (p - 1) - p^k$.
- Forms periodic cycles under repeated functional iteration, generating *amicable numbers* at period two where two distinct integers each equal the aliquot sum of the other, such as the ancient pair 220 and 284 attributed to *Pythagoras*.
- Governs the celebrated *Euclid-Euler theorem*, which demonstrates that an even integer is perfect if and only if it is generated by a Mersenne prime $2^p - 1$ through the explicit [[prime-factorization]] construction $2^{p-1}(2^p - 1)$, establishing a direct link to the geometry of [[euclid]].
- Produces dynamical orbits known as *aliquot sequences* when iterated iteratively as $n, s(n), s(s(n)), \dots$; cycles of length three or greater define *sociable numbers*, first identified in 1918 by the Belgian mathematician *Paul Poulet*.
- Anchors the unsolved *Catalan-Dickson conjecture*, which asserts that every aliquot sequence eventually terminates at a prime or enters a repeating cycle, though computational searches on base 276 remain unresolved after millions of iterations.
- Defines the set of *untouchable numbers*, which are positive integers that never appear as the aliquot sum of any number; the Hungarian mathematician *Paul Erdős* proved via analytic [[number-theory]] that infinitely many such untouchable values exist.
- Relies on the unique decomposition guaranteed by the [[fundamental-theorem-of-arithmetic]] to calculate the total divisor sum $\sigma(n)$ multiplicatively, although the aliquot sum function $s(n)$ itself is strictly non-multiplicative.

## connections

- [[number-theory]] — provides the core theoretical framework for studying divisor functions, prime distributions, and integer sequences.
- [[prime-factorization]] — supplies the prime powers and canonical factors required to determine all proper divisors of a given integer.
- [[fundamental-theorem-of-arithmetic]] — ensures the unique factorization that allows the sum-of-divisors function $\sigma(n)$ to be evaluated as a multiplicative function.
- [[euclid]] — established the foundational proof in Book IX of the *Elements* identifying how Mersenne primes yield integers that equal their aliquot sum.
- [[ancient-greece]] — the historical culture where early arithmetic investigations into perfect, abundant, and amicable numbers originated.
- [[modular-arithmetic]] — provides congruences and residue classes used to establish parity constraints on potential odd perfect numbers and untouchable integers.
- [[fermat-little-theorem]] — assists in primality testing and factor discovery for extraordinarily large intermediate terms generated in expanding aliquot sequences.
