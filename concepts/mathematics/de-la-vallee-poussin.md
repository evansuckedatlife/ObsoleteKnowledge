## summary

**Charles Jean de la Vallée Poussin** was a Belgian mathematician whose landmark work in analytic [[number-theory]] solved one of the central problems of nineteenth-century mathematics. In 1896, he independently proved the [[prime-number-theorem]], establishing the asymptotic distribution of prime numbers simultaneously with French mathematician *Jacques Hadamard*. Beyond his prime distribution proof, he made profound contributions to approximation theory, potential theory, and the summability of trigonometric series.

## you gotta know

- Independently proved the [[prime-number-theorem]] in 1896, demonstrating that the prime counting function is asymptotically equivalent to the logarithmic integral.
- Proved along with *Jacques Hadamard* that the *Riemann* zeta function has no complex zeros along the vertical line where the real part equals one.
- Refined the prime distribution result in 1899 by establishing the first explicit zero-free region for the zeta function, providing a quantitative asymptotic error bound.
- Introduced the *de la Vallée Poussin* kernel in approximation theory, forming intermediate trigonometric sums that ensure uniform [[convergence]] for continuous functions.
- Formulated a classic characterization of uniform integrability in measure theory via the existence of an increasing convex function that dominates expectations.
- Proved an alternation theorem in minimax polynomial approximation, giving a necessary and sufficient criterion for the best polynomial approximation in the *Chebyshev* norm.
- Spent nearly six decades as a professor at the Catholic University of Leuven, training generations of Belgian analysts.
- Endured the destruction of his university and personal library during the German invasion in [[world-war-i]], subsequently delivering guest lectures at Harvard and Paris.

## connections

- [[prime-number-theorem]] — the landmark asymptotic law regarding the density of prime numbers that he famously proved in 1896.
- [[number-theory]] — the mathematical field in which his complex-analytic methods resolved long-standing questions about prime distribution.
- [[convergence]] — studied extensively in his work on the summability of Fourier expansions and the uniform convergence of polynomial approximations.
- [[limit]] — essential to evaluating the asymptotic ratio between the prime counting function and logarithmic integral.
- [[calculus]] — utilized via complex contour integration along the critical strip of the zeta function.
- [[fourier-series]] — investigated through his smoothing kernels, which resolved convergence pathologies of partial sums.
