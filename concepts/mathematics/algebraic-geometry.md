## summary

**Algebraic geometry** is the classical and modern branch of mathematics dedicated to investigating the geometric properties of solutions to systems of multivariate polynomial equations. Emerging from Cartesian coordinate methods and projective geometry in [[france]] and [[germany]], the field underwent an immense twentieth-century transformation that turned it into one of the most conceptually sophisticated domains of pure mathematics. By uniting geometric intuition with abstract ring theory, sheaf cohomology, and scheme theory, it serves as the primary language for studying geometric spaces across arbitrary fields. It plays a decisive role in resolving historic questions in [[number-theory]], driving advances in theoretical physics and arithmetic geometry.

## you gotta know

- Defines its fundamental objects as algebraic varieties, which are geometric loci formed by the mutual vanishing sets of collections of [[polynomial-function]] equations over an algebraically closed field.
- Bridges geometric spaces and commutative algebra through *David Hilbert*'s celebrated *Nullstellensatz*, establishing a one-to-one correspondence between radical polynomial ideals in coordinate rings and affine algebraic sets.
- Investigates elliptic curves as smooth cubic projective curves of genus one equipped with an algebraic group law, creating the foundational bridge between algebraic geometry and [[number-theory]].
- Reconstructed upon an abstract axiomatic foundation by *Alexander Grothendieck* and *Jean-Pierre Serre*, who introduced sheaves and schemes to extend geometric analysis to arbitrary commutative rings and arithmetic schemes.
- Resolves intersection problems through projective space, as exemplified by *Bézout's theorem*, which guarantees that two plane projective curves of degree *m* and degree *n* without common components intersect at precisely *m* times *n* points counted with multiplicity.
- Provided the breakthrough framework for solving the *Weil conjectures*, an analogue of the *Riemann hypothesis* for varieties over finite fields that was formulated by *André Weil* and proven by *Pierre Deligne*.
- Classifies higher-dimensional algebraic varieties up to birational equivalence via the *Minimal Model Program*, a major field-defining initiative championed by *Shigefumi Mori* that constructs canonical representatives within birational equivalence classes.
- Supplied the core machinery of modular curves, deformation theory, and elliptic curve schemes that enabled *Andrew Wiles* and *Richard Taylor* to prove the *modularity theorem*, establishing the truth of *Fermat's Last Theorem*.

## connections

- [[polynomial-function]] — provides the core algebraic equations whose simultaneous roots define affine and projective varieties.
- [[number-theory]] — interfaces deeply through arithmetic geometry, which investigates the existence and distribution of rational and integer solutions on algebraic varieties.
- [[linear-algebra]] — supplies the vector techniques used to construct tangent spaces, compute differentials, and transform projective coordinate systems.
- [[vector-space]] — formalizes algebraic vector bundles, sheaf cohomology modules, and linear systems of divisors defined over geometric spaces.
- [[modular-arithmetic]] — provides the arithmetic of finite fields required to analyze varieties in positive characteristic and study zeta functions.
- [[calculus]] — historically inspired the concepts of tangent lines and differential forms that algebraic geometry translated into purely algebraic language.
- [[euclid]] — established the classical geometric tradition of figures and curves that coordinate geometry eventually represented as algebraic zero loci.
