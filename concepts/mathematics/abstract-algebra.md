## summary

**Abstract algebra**, historically developed under the name **modern algebra**, is the foundational branch of pure mathematics that investigates abstract algebraic structures—principally groups, rings, fields, modules, vector spaces, and lattices—defined by formal axiomatic systems rather than arithmetic operations on numerical quantities. Emerging throughout the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries through landmark breakthroughs in `[[france]]` and `[[germany]]`, the discipline fundamentally transformed algebra from the classical art of solving polynomial equations into the structural analysis of symmetries, mappings, and invariance. Today, it serves as a central organizing language across contemporary mathematics, underpinning disciplines from algebraic geometry and algebraic `[[number-theory]]` to cryptography, coding theory, and the group-theoretic formulations of modern quantum mechanics.

## you gotta know

- Shifted mathematical inquiry from solving for unknown quantities in a `[[polynomial-function]]` to classifying abstract structures governed by explicit sets of operational axioms.
- Crystallized through the study of polynomial roots by the French mathematician *Évariste Galois*, whose Galois theory linked `[[field-extension]]` structures to permutation groups to analyze algebraic solvability.
- Proved the Abel-Ruffini theorem and the insolvability of the quintic by radicals, showing that symmetry properties in `[[group-theory]]` prevent general degree-five polynomials from possessing root formulas analogous to the quadratic equation.
- Defined central algebraic structures that categorize mathematical symmetry, notably groups whose elements satisfy associativity, identity, and invertibility under a specified binary operation.
- Generalized traditional integer arithmetic from *[[modular-arithmetic]]* and the *[[fundamental-theorem-of-arithmetic]]* into broader abstract domains, including quotient rings, principal ideal domains, and unique factorization domains.
- Solved classical compass-and-straightedge geometric construction problems inherited from *[[ancient-greece]]*, demonstrating that doubling the cube, trisecting an arbitrary angle, and squaring the circle are algebraically impossible due to degree constraints on constructible field extensions.
- Codified the discipline into a modern university curriculum through *B. L. van der Waerden's* influential 1930 textbook *Modern Algebra*, which synthesized the groundbreaking seminar lectures of *Emmy Noether* and *Emil Artin*.
- Revolutionized ring and module theory through *Emmy Noether's* structural axiomatization, introducing the ascending chain condition on ideals to establish the finite generation of Noetherian rings without relying on tedious coordinate computations.

## connections

- `[[polynomial-function]]` — investigating radical formulas for the roots of higher-degree polynomial equations served as the primary historical catalyst for group and field theory.
- `[[modular-arithmetic]]` — provides standard concrete examples of finite cyclic groups, quotient rings, and finite fields that ground abstract axiomatic definitions.
- `[[vector-space]]` — represents an algebraic structure defined over a field, which abstract algebra generalizes to modules defined over arbitrary rings.
- `[[linear-algebra]]` — the structural study of linear maps and matrices, reinterpreted abstractly through module homomorphisms and `[[eigenvalue]]` decompositions.
- `[[fundamental-theorem-of-arithmetic]]` — establishes prime factorization for integers, serving as the archetype for unique factorization domains and Dedekind domains in ring theory.
- `[[fermat-little-theorem]]` — a classical result in number theory that abstract algebra explains as an immediate consequence of *Lagrange's theorem* applied to multiplicative groups of units.
- `[[france]]` — homeland of *Évariste Galois*, whose brief life and revolutionary papers on permutation groups established the foundations of structural algebra.
- `[[germany]]` — home of the University of *Göttingen*, the leading center of the early twentieth-century axiomatic movement spearheaded by *Emmy Noether*.
