## summary

A **hunter-gatherer** society relies primarily on the direct foraging of wild edible plants and the hunting or fishing of undomesticated animals for subsistence. Representing the universal mode of survival for *[[homo-sapiens]]* throughout the Paleolithic era until roughly twelve thousand years ago, this lifestyle shaped human physiology, social organization, and environmental interactions across the globe. Studying ancient and surviving foraging communities provides foundational social science insights into the origins of human [[culture]], egalitarian band dynamics, and the economic transitions of prehistory.

## you gotta know

- Subsistence based on wild flora and fauna was the universal economic model of the human genus for hundreds of thousands of years, dominating human prehistory until the advent of plant and animal domestication during the [[neolithic-revolution]].
- Anthropologist *Marshall Sahlins* challenged the traditional view of prehistoric life as nasty, brutish, and short in his landmark essay *The Original Affluent Society*, arguing that foragers limited their material desires and worked only three to five hours daily to satisfy their subsistence needs.
- The landmark 1966 *Man the Hunter* symposium organized by *Richard Lee* and *Irven DeVore* revolutionized anthropological perspectives by demonstrating that plant gathering, primarily conducted by women, supplied the vast majority of caloric intake across temperate and tropical foraging societies.
- Social organization among mobile foragers typically took the form of small, kinship-based bands characterized by flexible group membership, shared food resources, and deliberate cultural mechanisms to prevent the emergence of centralized [[authority]] or material inequality.
- Bioarchaeological analysis of human skeletal remains indicates that early foragers were often taller, possessed better nutrition, and suffered fewer chronic infectious diseases and dental caries than the early sedentary farmers who succeeded them.
- Anthropologist *Christopher Boehm* identified "reverse dominance hierarchies" among nomadic foragers, in which the collective group used gossip, ridicule, ostracism, and execution to sanction overbearing or aggressive individuals who threatened egalitarian social order.
- Not all foragers were nomadic or strictly egalitarian; affluent complex hunter-gatherers, such as several maritime [[native-americans]] of the Pacific Northwest, built permanent wooden villages, preserved surplus salmon, and established hereditary social rank and potlatch [[ritual]] systems.

## connections

- [[neolithic-revolution]] — the profound global transition from foraging to agriculture and sedentary village life that began in the Fertile Crescent.
- [[human-evolution]] — the biological and behavioral lineage of hominins shaped by bipedal endurance hunting, tool manufacture, and dietary breadth.
- [[culture]] — the learned systems of ecological knowledge, tool technologies, and social customs essential for surviving in varied environments.
- [[ritual]] — communal ceremonies, sacred storytelling, and artistic expressions such as cave paintings that forged collective solidarity among nomadic bands.
- [[stone-tools]] — the enduring material artifacts, spanning the Paleolithic to the Mesolithic, engineered for hunting, scraping hides, and processing foraged roots.
- [[native-americans]] — diverse indigenous cultures whose historical subsistence strategies ranged from nomadic Great Plains bison hunting to stratified Northwest Coast maritime fishing.
- [[karl-marx]] — philosopher whose concept of "primitive communism" was inspired by the classless, shared property relations observed in prehistoric foraging bands.
