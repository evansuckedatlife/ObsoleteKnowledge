## summary

**Economic growth** is the sustained increase in the total productive output of an economy over time, typically measured by real [[gross-domestic-product]] growth. It reflects rising living standards, expanded employment, and increasing wealth creation in a society.

## you gotta know

- Measured as the percentage change in real GDP (adjusted for inflation) over a period, usually annually; sustained growth of 2-3% annually is typical for developed economies; growth rates below 0% define recession.
- *Factors of production* — labour, capital, land, and entrepreneurship — are the engines of growth; increases in quantity or quality drive expansion; a growing population and workforce fuel labour supply growth.
- Growth requires *capital accumulation* — investment in factories, infrastructure, education, and technology that raise future productive capacity; countries must save to invest, creating trade-offs between present consumption and future growth.
- *Productivity gains* (output per worker) are the ultimate driver of long-term growth; technological innovation and improved processes multiply worker output; without productivity improvement, growth stalls even with more workers.
- The [[industrial-revolution]] demonstrated that technological and organizational innovation could sustain rapid growth; pre-industrial economies grew very slowly.
- Different economists emphasize different sources: classical economists stressed capital and labour; neoclassicals added technological change; modern endogenous growth theory emphasizes R&D and knowledge.
- Growth comes with trade-offs: rapid growth may cause environmental damage, inequality, or social disruption; sustainable growth requires balancing expansion with equity and ecology.
- Institutions matter deeply for growth — secure property rights, rule of law, and low corruption enable investment; weak institutions trap poor countries in low-growth equilibrium despite potential resources.
- Some economists emphasize *catch-up growth* — poor countries can grow faster initially by adopting proven technologies; but convergence slows as they approach the frontier, requiring original innovation for further advance.

## connections

- [[gross-domestic-product]] — the primary measure of economic growth; growth is defined as expansion in real GDP.
- [[factors-of-production]] — the inputs that, when combined and improved, generate growth.
- [[capital-accumulation]] — the investment process that expands productive capacity for future growth.
- [[productivity]] — output per unit of input; the deepest driver of long-term growth.
- [[innovation]] — technological and organizational breakthroughs that dramatically expand what an economy can produce.
- [[inflation]] — nominal growth may reflect inflation rather than real growth; distinguishing the two is critical for policy.
- [[business-cycle]] — growth fluctuates around a long-term trend; booms and recessions are deviations.
