## summary

**Language contact** occurs when speakers of different languages interact over time, leading to mutual linguistic influence and innovation. Through contact, languages borrow vocabulary, grammar, and sounds; multilingual speakers develop mixed codes and creoles. Language contact is a primary driver of linguistic diversity and change, reshaping languages and creating entirely new ones throughout history and in the modern world.

## you gotta know

- *Borrowing* is the most common contact phenomenon — words, sounds, or structures from one language enter another (English *safari* from Swahili, *restaurant* from French).
- *Bilingual speakers* mediate contact; their code-switching and interference patterns influence both languages over generations.
- *Creoles* are new languages that emerge from intense multilingual contact, typically combining grammar from a substrate language with vocabulary from a dominant colonial language.
- *Pidgins* are simplified contact languages used for trade or administration; creoles form when pidgins become native languages to new generations.
- Contact can *accelerate language change*, disrupting normal patterns; structural simplification often occurs in contact settings.
- *Substratum effects* — features from suppressed indigenous languages persist in the dominant language of a region (e.g., French influence on English after 1066).
- *Prestige asymmetry* matters; borrowing typically flows from high-prestige to low-prestige languages.
- Threatens *endangered languages* — dominant languages often displace smaller ones; many languages are lost through contact displacement.
- *Mesolectal* forms emerge as speakers of different language backgrounds develop intermediate varieties (neither pure source language nor creole).
- *Phonetic accommodation* happens in real time: speakers subtly shift pronunciation toward conversational partners during bilingual interactions.
- Historical examples: English's enormous French vocabulary after 1066, Chinese place names absorbed into Japanese, and Hindi–Urdu convergence.
