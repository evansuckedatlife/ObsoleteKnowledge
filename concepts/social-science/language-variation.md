## summary

**Language variation** encompasses the observable differences in how people speak within a single language—pronunciation, vocabulary, grammar, and pragmatics vary systematically by region, class, age, gender, and social context. Variation is not corruption or incompetence but rather the fundamental nature of living languages; every language is a collection of overlapping dialects and registers. Sociolinguistics reveals that variation patterns reflect social structures and historical forces, making language a archive of social inequality.

## you gotta know

- Geographical dialects emerge from historical settlement patterns and linguistic drift; English speakers in Scotland, America, India, and Nigeria speak recognizably different Englishes shaped by local history and contact with other languages.
- Class-based variation is robust and systematic; working-class speakers typically use non-standard grammar and pronunciation more frequently than middle-class speakers, and these differences are stable across generations, suggesting deeper social causes than mere education.
- Gender variation is pervasive; women typically use more prestige forms (standard pronunciations, formal grammar) than men in the same social class, and initiate language change toward higher-prestige forms—a phenomenon called the *gender paradox*.
- Age-based variation reflects both lifespan changes (speakers adopt more formal speech as they age) and real-time language change (each generation speaks slightly differently from its parents, accumulating into visible historical shifts).
- Register variation (formal vs. casual speech) shows that the same speaker commands multiple styles, code-switching between them based on audience and topic; formality is not a property of the language but a property of social context.
- Phonemic variation (differences in which sounds exist in a dialect) is rare; most variation consists of different realizations of the same phoneme—e.g., /r/ pronounced as a retroflex [ɹ] in English vs. a trill [r] in Spanish.
- Network effects shape variation; language spreads through social networks, and speakers who bridge multiple social groups (more cosmopolitan, educated, geographically mobile) tend to acquire more variable speech patterns.
- Variation often correlates with stigma: non-standard dialects are devalued by institutions (schools, media, courts) even though linguistically they are equally complex and rule-governed; this stigmatization reflects and reproduces social inequality.

## connections

- [[sociolinguistics]] — the discipline that maps and explains language variation systematically.
- [[dialect]] — the most recognized form of variation; regionally-bounded speech varieties.
- [[language-change]] — variation is the mechanism through which language evolves over time; variations today are languages tomorrow.
- [[register]] — stylistic variation by context and formality level.
- [[code-switching]] — the deployment of variation strategically by multilingual speakers.
- [[linguistics]] — the broader science of language; variation is fundamental to linguistic theory.
- [[language-contact]] — often the source of new variations when languages influence each other.
