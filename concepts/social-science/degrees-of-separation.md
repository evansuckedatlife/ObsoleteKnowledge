## summary

**degrees of separation** is a mathematical and sociological concept positing that any two individuals on Earth can be linked through a short chain of intermediate acquaintances. Popularized through social psychologist *Stanley Milgram*'s small-world experiments in the late 1960s, the phrase "six degrees of separation" entered widespread cultural consciousness through *John Guare*'s 1990 play. The concept sparked modern computational network theory, demonstrating that real-world social matrices exhibit high clustering combined with surprisingly brief average path lengths.

## you gotta know

- The fundamental premise was originally introduced in Hungarian author *Frigyes Karinthy*'s 1929 short story "Chains" (*Láncszemek*), which hypothesized that human interconnectedness shrunk rapidly with modernization.
- *Stanley Milgram* empirically tested the small-world phenomenon in 1967 by asking target participants in Kansas and Nebraska to forward postal packets toward an unfamiliar stockbroker in Boston.
- The average chain length among successfully delivered letters in Milgram's experiment was approximately 5.5 intermediates, crystallizing into the popular moniker "six degrees of separation."
- Sociologist *Mark Granovetter* expanded the underlying structural mechanics in 1973 with his theory on the "strength of weak ties," showing that casual acquaintances bridge distant social clusters.
- Mathematicians *Duncan Watts* and *Steven Strogatz* mathematically formalized the small-world model in 1998, explaining how adding rare random links to a regular lattice drastically reduces average distance.
- Modern digital analyses of platforms like Facebook, Twitter, and email networks demonstrated global path lengths ranging between 3.5 and 4.7, proving the world is even smaller online.
- The concept inspired playful cultural benchmarks, including the Kevin Bacon Game and the Erdős number, measuring collaborative distance in cinema and mathematical research.
