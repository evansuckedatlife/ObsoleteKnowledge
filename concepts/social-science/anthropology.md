## summary

**Anthropology** is the systematic study of human societies and cultures in all their diversity—past and present. Anthropologists examine how people organize themselves, what they believe, how they communicate, and how they make meaning. Rooted in comparative observation and detailed fieldwork, anthropology bridges the natural and social sciences, revealing both universal human patterns and the profound variation in how societies solve fundamental problems.

## you gotta know

- Anthropology's core method is ethnography: long-term immersive observation and participant engagement with a community's daily life, typically lasting months to years.
- Franz Boas, the discipline's founding figure in America, argued that culture must be understood on its own terms rather than judged by Western standards—a principle called *cultural relativism* that rejected evolutionary hierarchies.
- Four main subfields: cultural/social anthropology (norms and meaning), biological anthropology (human evolution and genetics), archaeology (material culture and history), and linguistic anthropology (language and communication).
- Kinship systems, gift exchange, taboo, and ritual are central topics; understanding these reveals how societies structure identity, obligation, and social hierarchy in ways radically different from Western assumptions.
- Anthropology emerged partly as a response to colonialism and racism, later becoming a tool for critiquing those systems by revealing the constructed nature of cultural categories like "race" and "civilization."
- Malinowski's concept of *functionalism*—the idea that cultural practices serve identifiable social functions maintaining group cohesion—remains influential in understanding culture even as later anthropologists critiqued its teleology.
- Postcolonial and feminist anthropology have challenged the discipline's historical association with colonialism and male-dominated fieldwork, advocating for collaborative research and attention to power dynamics.
- Applied anthropology uses anthropological knowledge to address real-world problems: development, public health, environmental sustainability, and organizational culture change—moving beyond pure academic observation.
