## summary

**Introspection** is the systematic self-observation and analysis of one's own mental states, thoughts, and feelings. Pioneered by *Wilhelm Wundt* as the primary method of experimental psychology in the 19th century, introspection attempted to make consciousness scientifically measurable. While its limitations led to its eclipse by behaviorism, introspection remains important to contemporary cognitive science and phenomenology.

## you gotta know

- *Wilhelm Wundt* developed *introspection as an experimental method* in his psychological laboratory at Leipzig University.
- Involved trained observers systematically reporting their immediate conscious experience under controlled stimulus conditions.
- Wundt believed introspection could measure the *elemental contents of consciousness* — sensations, feelings, and their combinations.
- Represented an attempt to make psychology a rigorous *natural science* by measuring mental phenomena the way physics measures matter.
- Suffered from *poor reliability* — observers' reports could not be easily verified or replicated across individuals.
- Criticized by *Behaviorists* like *John B. Watson*, who argued that subjective mental reports had no place in science.
- *Lost primacy* in psychology but never disappeared entirely; cognitive psychology later rehabilitated careful introspection combined with objective measurement.
- Differs from casual self-reflection by requiring systematic training, standard conditions, and careful reporting of immediate experience.
- *Structuralism*, the psychological school built on Wundt's introspection, sought to decompose consciousness into basic units like atoms in physics.
- Modern neuroscience has partially rehabilitated introspective reports as data points that complement fMRI and other objective measures of brain activity.
- *Phenomenology*, a philosophical movement, adopted introspection as a rigorous method for studying the structure of conscious experience.
