## summary

*Philip Zimbardo* is an American social psychologist best known for conducting the Stanford Prison Experiment in 1971, a shocking study that demonstrated how quickly ordinary people adopt authoritarian and abusive behaviors when placed in roles within institutional power structures. His work became a landmark in understanding the psychology of obedience, institutional violence, and the malleability of human nature under social pressure. His findings profoundly influenced psychological and ethical thinking about authority and individual agency.

## you gotta know

- Conducted the Stanford Prison Experiment in 1971, assigning Stanford students randomly to play guards or prisoners in a simulated prison that lasted only 6 days before escalating abuses forced its termination.
- The experiment revealed that guards rapidly became authoritarian and cruel, while prisoners became passive, dehumanized, and emotionally distressed—contradicting expectations that personality determined behavior and suggesting situational power overwhelmed dispositional traits.
- Zimbardo later became the situational psychologist par excellence, arguing that context and social roles shape behavior more powerfully than individual traits—a position supported by his experiment and later challenged by revisionist critiques.
- The experiment raised major ethical concerns about psychological research, including informed consent, researcher responsibility for harm inflicted on participants, and the duty to stop harmful studies immediately.
- Subsequent decades brought reexamination and criticism, with allegations that Zimbardo actively encouraged guard abuses and coached participants, essentially creating rather than discovering the behavior he claimed to observe.
- He later developed the Lucifer Effect framework to explain how ordinary people commit evil, extending insights from the prison experiment to broader social phenomena like torture, genocides, and institutional violence.
- His personality theory work addressed the "demonic other syndrome"—the tendency to see evil actors as inherently different from ourselves rather than recognizing our own capacity for situational evil.
- The Stanford Prison Experiment remains foundational in social psychology curricula despite methodological and ethical criticisms, illustrating the power and peril of dramatic psychological demonstrations.
