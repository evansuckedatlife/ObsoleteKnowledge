## summary

**Psychosocial development** refers to the emotional and social aspects of human growth across the lifespan, emphasizing how individuals navigate relationships, identity, and social roles as they mature. Centralized in developmental psychology by Erik Erikson's framework, psychosocial development complements cognitive theories by focusing on how the social world shapes emotional life and how individuals construct identity through social interaction.

## you gotta know

- Erik Erikson proposed eight psychosocial stages, each defined by a central conflict or developmental task (trust vs. mistrust in infancy, identity vs. role confusion in adolescence, generativity vs. stagnation in middle adulthood).
- A key innovation in Erikson's theory is extending development across the entire lifespan; earlier theories focused on childhood, but psychosocial development continues throughout adulthood and old age.
- Each stage requires resolution of its central conflict to develop specific virtues; successful navigation builds strengths (hope, autonomy, purpose) that support later development.
- Psychosocial development emphasizes that psychological growth is inseparable from social context; peers, family, cultural values, and institutions shape how individuals develop through life.
- Identity formation, central to adolescent psychosocial development, involves exploring different roles and ideologies before committing to an adult identity—a process influenced by family dynamics, peer culture, and broader social opportunity.
- Erikson's framework accounts for cultural variation in development; different societies may value different virtues and stage durations, though the basic progression is more universal.
- Psychosocial development integrates emotional regulation (managing feelings), social skills (relating to others), and meaning-making (understanding one's place in society)—dimensions often separated in pure cognitive theories.
- Successful passage through early stages (trust, autonomy, initiative) builds competence and self-esteem; failure to resolve these stages can create persistent anxiety, shame, or feelings of inadequacy.
- Middle adulthood focuses on *generativity*—concern for the next generation and leaving a legacy; individuals who achieve this feel purposeful and connected; stagnation leads to self-absorption and dissatisfaction.
- Old age culminates in *ego integrity*—acceptance of one's life and mortality—versus despair; the ability to review one's life with acceptance rather than regret is the final psychosocial achievement.
