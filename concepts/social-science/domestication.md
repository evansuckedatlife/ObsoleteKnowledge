## summary

**Domestication** is the process of selective breeding and behavioral modification through which wild plants and animals are adapted to human use over many generations. Domestication represents one of humanity's most consequential technologies; it enabled the transition from mobile hunter-gatherer societies to settled agricultural communities, triggering the Neolithic Revolution. Domestication is evolutionary change directed by human choice rather than natural selection; over centuries, humans unconsciously (and sometimes consciously) selected for traits beneficial to themselves, reshaping plant genetics and animal behavior irreversibly.

## you gotta know

- Plant domestication involved selecting seeds from plants with larger seeds, more nutritious forms, or better survival in cultivation, unconsciously selecting for dependence on human care; over generations, wild grains became crops unable to survive without human planting and harvesting.
- Animal domestication required selecting for tameness and reduced flight response; wild animals with extreme fight-or-flight reactions were unsuitable for human control, so only docile individuals bred successfully under human management, making behavioral change the primary driver of domestication.
- Domesticated species often undergo parallel changes in multiple traits (neoteny—retention of juvenile features into adulthood, floppy ears, varied coat colors, reduced brain size)—side effects of selecting for tameness, suggesting that behavioral change drives broader genetic shifts.
- Domestication is irreversible; domesticated plants are often dependent on human care and cannot survive without it; domesticated animals, if released into the wild, typically cannot survive without human shelter and feeding, though some feral populations adapt over generations.
- Climate and geography shaped which species could be domesticated and where; the species available for domestication (sheep, goats, cattle, wheat, barley in the Fertile Crescent; corn, beans, squash in Mesoamerica) varied regionally, influencing which civilizations could rise.
- Domestication enabled population growth but also introduced new vulnerabilities: human-animal contact transmitted zoonotic diseases (smallpox, measles); monoculture agriculture reduced dietary diversity; dependence on specific crops created vulnerability to famine if crops failed.
- The transition from foraging to farming was not unambiguously progressive; archaeological evidence (bone health, disease markers, labor requirements) suggests that early farmers were often less healthy and worked harder than contemporaneous foragers, though farming did allow denser settlement.
- Domestication continues; modern agriculture constantly selects for traits deemed desirable (disease resistance, yield, shelf life), creating crop varieties and livestock breeds that are highly specialized and dependent on human management and technology.

## connections

- [[early-agriculture]] — the practice that domestication enables; farming depends on domesticated crops and animals.
- [[neolithic-revolution]] — the transformation from hunter-gatherer to agricultural society, fundamentally dependent on domestication.
- [[settlement-patterns]] — domestication enabled sedentary communities; agriculture supports denser, more permanent settlements than foraging.
- [[bronze-age]] — civilizations dependent on domesticated plants and animals; the Bronze Age could not have existed without Neolithic domestication.
- [[agriculture]] — the contemporary practice rooted in ancient domestication; modern farming maintains and extends domestication.
- [[human-ecology]] — the study of how humans interact with their environment; domestication is the deliberate reshaping of species for human benefit.
- [[civilization]] — complex societies dependent on agricultural surplus enabled by domestication.
