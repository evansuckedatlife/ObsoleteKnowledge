## summary

**Motivation** is the force that initiates, directs, and sustains behavior toward achieving goals or satisfying needs. Psychologists conceptualize it as the internal or external drivers that compel action, ranging from biological drives like hunger to psychological goals like achievement and belonging. Understanding motivation has become central to psychology, education, and organizational behavior, with major theories explaining everything from survival-level needs to self-fulfillment.

## you gotta know

- Biological drives (hunger, thirst, sexual reproduction) represent the most fundamental motivators, shared across species. These drive-reduction mechanisms were central to early behavioral psychology.
- *Abraham Maslow* theorized a hierarchy of needs, proposing that lower needs (physiological, safety) must be satisfied before higher psychological needs (belonging, esteem, self-actualization) become motivating forces.
- Extrinsic motivation (external rewards or punishments) can be more immediate and effective for simple tasks but often proves less sustainable than intrinsic motivation (inherent satisfaction, autonomy, mastery, purpose).
- *Martin Seligman's* concept of learned helplessness revealed that repeated uncontrollable failure can eliminate motivation, demonstrating the psychological basis of depression and apathy even when escape becomes possible.
- Herzberg's two-factor theory separates motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility) from hygiene factors (salary, security, working conditions), arguing the two operate independently and both matter.
- Goal-setting theory emphasizes that clear, specific, challenging objectives generate stronger motivation than vague aspirations, but only when feedback and commitment are present.
- Cultural variation exists in what motivates individuals—individualistic societies emphasize personal achievement and autonomy, while collectivist societies emphasize group harmony and relational obligations.
- Self-determination theory emphasizes three core needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—arguing intrinsic motivation flourishes when all three are satisfied.
