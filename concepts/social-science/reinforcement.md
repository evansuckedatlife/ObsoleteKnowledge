## summary

**Reinforcement** is any consequence that increases the likelihood of a behavior being repeated. In behavioral psychology, *B.F. Skinner* distinguished between *positive reinforcement* (adding something desirable, like food or praise) and *negative reinforcement* (removing something aversive, like pain or discomfort). This simple principle—that behavior followed by a reinforcing consequence becomes more probable—explains how organisms learn and adapt to their environments across species and contexts.

## you gotta know

- Positive reinforcement strengthens behavior by adding a desirable consequence (a rat gets food for pressing a lever, a student gets praise for answering correctly).
- Negative reinforcement strengthens behavior by removing or avoiding an aversive stimulus (a driver buckles a seatbelt to stop the alarm from beeping; an animal escapes an electric shock by performing the desired action).
- Crucially, both types *increase* behavior; they are distinct from *punishment*, which *decreases* behavior regardless of whether something is added or removed.
- The strength and pattern of reinforcement matters: continuous reinforcement (reward every time) produces rapid learning but quick extinction when reinforcement stops; intermittent schedules produce slower learning but greater persistence.
- Variable-ratio schedules (reward after a random number of behaviors) are especially powerful—they create the "gambling effect," where unpredictability sustains effort indefinitely.
- Reinforcement is species-universal: the same principle works on pigeons, rats, dogs, humans, and most creatures capable of learning, suggesting it reflects a fundamental property of nervous systems.
- The concept underpins applied behavior analysis, animal training, education, workplace motivation systems, and even video game design, which deliberately exploit reinforcement schedules to create compulsive engagement.
- Reinforcement must be *immediate* (or very soon after the behavior) to be effective; delayed reinforcement loses its power—which is why instant feedback loops in games and apps are so potent at shaping behavior and addiction.
- *Satiation* (repetition of reinforcement until it no longer motivates) can reduce a reinforcer's power, while *deprivation* (removing access to the reinforcer) restores its strength.

## connections

- [[b-f-skinner]] — the psychologist who systematized reinforcement theory and demonstrated its power through rigorous experiments.
- [[operant-conditioning]] — the broader learning framework of which reinforcement is the central mechanism.
- [[punishment]] — the complementary concept that decreases behavior; often confused with negative reinforcement.
- [[behaviorism]] — the psychological school treating reinforcement as the fundamental driver of behavior.
- [[learning-theory]] — broader field exploring how organisms acquire knowledge and habit.
- [[behavior-modification]] — the practical application of reinforcement principles to change unwanted behaviors.
