## summary

**Innateness**—most prominently formulated in [[linguistics]] and [[cognitive-science]] as the **innateness hypothesis**—is the doctrine that human beings possess an inborn, biologically predetermined capacity for acquiring language and organizing complex mental representations. Articulated in the mid-twentieth century by *[[noam-chomsky]]* as a cornerstone of generative grammar, the concept argues that human children do not learn syntax merely through environmental conditioning or passive imitation, but instead rely on genetically encoded structural constraints. In quiz bowl and the social sciences, the debate over innateness is essential for understanding the decline of radical [[behaviorism]] and the dawn of the cognitive revolution, framing enduring controversies across [[developmental-psychology]], cognitive modularity, and the classical nature-versus-nurture divide.

## you gotta know

- *Poverty of the stimulus*: *[[noam-chomsky]]* argued that children acquire language rapidly and uniformly despite environmental input that is degenerate, finite, and lacking in negative evidence, making an innate grammatical predisposition necessary to solve the induction problem.
- *Language Acquisition Device* and *universal grammar*: Chomsky posited a specialized mental organ, the *Language Acquisition Device* (*LAD*), later formalized into *[[universal-grammar]]*, which asserts that all natural languages share invariant structural principles encoded in human biology.
- *Critique of behaviorism*: The hypothesis gained widespread prominence through Chomsky's 1959 review of *[[b-f-skinner]]*'s book *Verbal Behavior*, showing that operant conditioning cannot account for the unbounded creativity and recursive structure of human speech.
- *The Language Instinct*: Cognitive scientist *Steven Pinker* popularized the nativist paradigm in his 1994 book *The Language Instinct*, synthesizing generative linguistics with evolutionary psychology to argue that language is a specialized biological adaptation.
- *Creolization and sign language*: Primary empirical support stems from pidgin-to-creole transitions and *Nicaraguan Sign Language*, where children exposed to rudimentary, unstructured communication spontaneously invented fully formed, grammatically regularized languages within a single generation.
- *Critical period hypothesis*: Formulated by *Eric Lenneberg* in his 1967 work *Biological Foundations of Language*, this corollary posits that innate language acquisition machinery depends on neural maturation and operates within a biological window closing around puberty.
- *Royaumont debate with constructivism*: In a famous 1975 debate in *[[france]]*, developmental psychologist *[[jean-piaget]]* contested Chomsky's strict nativism, asserting that linguistic competence develops gradually through general sensorimotor intelligence rather than a preformed, domain-specific mental faculty.

## connections

- [[noam-chomsky]] — formulated the poverty of the stimulus argument and universal grammar to establish the modern nativist paradigm.
- [[behaviorism]] — the dominant mid-century empiricist paradigm whose tabula rasa learning model was overturned by nativist linguistics.
- [[b-f-skinner]] — radical behaviorist whose operant conditioning account of verbal learning provoked Chomsky's field-defining 1959 critique.
- [[cognitive-science]] — interdisciplinary field galvanized by nativist arguments for internal mental representations, modularity, and cognitive architecture.
- [[linguistics]] — academic discipline revolutionized by the generative program, which reoriented the field toward studying the mind's internal grammar.
- [[developmental-psychology]] — discipline investigating childhood growth, where nativist and empiricist frameworks clash over how cognitive faculties mature.
- [[jean-piaget]] — developmental psychologist who championed cognitive constructivism against Chomsky's innate modularity during their 1975 debate.
