## summary

The **indigenous languages of the Americas** comprise the languages spoken by the diverse pre-Columbian civilizations and their descendants, ranging from large language families like [[Quechua]] (8+ million speakers) and [[Navajo]] (180,000+ speakers) to dozens of undocumented or nearly-extinct language isolates. These languages exhibit remarkable structural diversity—polysynthetic verb systems, ergative-absolutive alignment, complex tone systems—and encode unique knowledge about botany, astronomy, and oral history that is lost when speakers shift to Spanish, Portuguese, or English.

## you gotta know

- The Americas contain unprecedented linguistic diversity; pre-Columbian populations spoke thousands of distinct languages organized into hundreds of language families—far more diversity than Europe.
- Major language families include *Quechuan* (Andes), *Aymaran* (southern Andes), *Oto-Manguean* (Mexico), *Oto-Pamean*, *Athabaskan* (North America), *Salishan* (Pacific Northwest), and hundreds of isolated languages with no documented relatives.
- [[Franz Boas]] pioneered documentation of indigenous North American languages (Kwakwaka'wakw, Bella Coola, Chinook); his work established that non-European languages possessed complex grammar and disproved racist hierarchies of linguistic "primitiveness."
- Spanish and Portuguese conquest triggered rapid language death; colonial language policy suppressed indigenous languages in favor of European languages, literacy, and Christian education.
- [[Navajo]] and [[Quechua]] are exceptional successes: both maintained large speaker bases and achieved official or co-official status in their nations, partly through deliberate cultural revitalization and educational policies.
- Indigenous American languages exhibit typological extremes: [[Navajo]] is highly polysynthetic (one verb can express an entire sentence), while [[Pirahã]] is renowned for minimal numerals and alleged absence of recursion (disputed by many linguists).
- Many indigenous language communities lack orthographies, educational materials, or institutional support; documentation by linguists is often the only record preventing complete extinction.
