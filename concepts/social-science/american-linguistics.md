## summary

**American linguistics** designates the distinct theoretical and methodological traditions of language study that developed in the [[united-states]] from the late nineteenth century to the present. Initially founded upon anthropological documentation of unwritten indigenous tongues by figures like [[franz-boas]] and *Edward Sapir*, the tradition evolved through the distributionalist rigor of *Leonard Bloomfield* before being transformed by *Noam Chomsky*'s generative grammar. This intellectual trajectory moved the field from empiricist structural fieldwork toward formal mentalist theories of human cognition.

## you gotta know

- Began under [[franz-boas]], whose *Handbook of American Indian Languages* established descriptive linguistics by insisting that languages must be documented on their own structural terms without forcing them into Indo-European grammatical templates.
- Developed the foundational Sapir-Whorf hypothesis through *Edward Sapir* and *Benjamin Lee Whorf*, exploring how grammatical patterns influence habitual thought and cultural categorization.
- Formalized into American structuralism through *Leonard Bloomfield*'s seminal 1933 textbook *Language*, which married structural descriptive methods with anti-mentalist [[behaviorism]].
- Institutionalized through the founding of the *Linguistic Society of America* (*LSA*) in 1924 and its prestigious journal *Language*.
- Revolutionized in 1957 by [[noam-chomsky]]'s *Syntactic Structures*, which rejected structuralism and behavioral learning in favor of generative grammar and an innate language acquisition device, launching the cognitive revolution.
- Extended through ongoing field studies of indigenous American languages, including [[amazonian-languages]] and [[arawakan-languages]], which continually provide crucial empirical checks against universal formal models.

## connections

- [[franz-boas]] — the father of American anthropology who inaugurated the empirical tradition of documenting indigenous North American languages.
- [[noam-chomsky]] — the Massachusetts Institute of Technology scholar whose generative enterprise remade the discipline from the late 1950s onward.
- [[linguistics]] — the worldwide academic discipline of language study whose modern direction was largely guided by American developments.
- [[behaviorism]] — the psychological doctrine that deeply influenced Bloomfieldian structuralism's rejection of mentalism.
- [[cognitive-science]] — the multidisciplinary field co-founded by generative linguists rejecting behavioral models of verbal behavior.
- [[amazonian-languages]] — field study sites in South America that continue to test and challenge theoretical frameworks of American linguistic theory.
- [[arawakan-languages]] — native language families of the Americas that supplied essential empirical material for American structuralists.
- [[united-states]] — the institutional and academic home of universities, societies, and grant bodies that sponsored these theoretical paradigms.
