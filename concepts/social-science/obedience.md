## summary

**Obedience** is the behavioral tendency to comply with the directives of authority figures, a fundamental aspect of social organization and group functioning. While often adaptive—allowing coordination and rule-following—obedience can be destructive when authorities demand harmful acts, as demonstrated by historical atrocities and modern psychological experiments that probe the boundaries between self-direction and submission to authority.

## you gotta know

- Obedience appears across all human societies in some form; without it, coordinated group action and institutional order would be nearly impossible; it is often adaptive for survival and functioning.
- The concept gained particular prominence through Stanley Milgram's experiments in the 1960s, which showed that ordinary people would obey experimenters' orders to deliver seemingly fatal electric shocks to innocent subjects; 65% of participants went to maximum voltage.
- Obedience differs from conformity: conformity is alignment with peer group norms (following what others do), while obedience is compliance with explicit directives from a hierarchical superior.
- Milgram's research suggested that proximity to authority, removal of personal responsibility, and institutional legitimacy can drive individuals to violate their own conscience; when authority was distant, compliance dropped sharply.
- Authority need not be physically present; symbolic authority (uniforms, titles, institutional settings) powerfully triggers obedience, as did the laboratory setting in Milgram's experiments.
- Cultural variation in obedience levels exists; societies with different power-distance values show varying degrees of automatic deference to authority; some cultures emphasize obedience as a virtue more than others.
- Ethical concerns about Milgram's methods led to major revisions in research ethics and the need for informed consent and protection from psychological harm in psychological studies.
- Obedience to authority can escalate destructively: the Stanford Prison Experiment, Nazi atrocities, and modern organizational crimes reveal how obedience systems can enable mass harm.
- Psychological distance (physical, temporal, or social) from those harmed reduces obedience; direct observation of victims' suffering increases resistance to harmful orders.
- Disobedience increases when group members coordinate resistance or when authority's legitimacy is questioned; moral courage and social support both enable defiance of unjust authority.
