## summary

**dating methods** comprise the scientific and analytical techniques utilized by archaeologists, physical anthropologists, and geologists to establish the chronological age of artifacts, biological remains, and geological strata. Developed across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, these procedures are broadly divided into relative techniques, which arrange materials in sequential order, and absolute or chronometric techniques, which assign calendar dates based on physical constants. Mastering these frameworks enables researchers to sequence human cultural adaptations, trace migrations, and coordinate global prehistoric chronologies.

## you gotta know

- Radiocarbon dating, developed by *Willard Libby* in the late 1940s, measures the radioactive decay of carbon-14 in organic materials up to roughly 50,000 years old.
- Stratigraphy relies on the geological law of superposition formulated by *Nicolas Steno*, establishing relative sequences where lower undisturbed sedimentary layers precede upper ones.
- Dendrochronology, pioneered by *A. E. Douglass*, determines precise annual calendar years by measuring variations in tree-ring growth patterns and cross-referencing regional master sequences.
- Potassium-argon (K-Ar) and argon-argon dating measure isotopic decay in volcanic rock, making them indispensable for establishing the deep chronological timescale of hominin fossils in East Africa.
- Seriation arranges assemblages of artifacts, notably ceramic pottery styles, along a relative timeline based on changes in frequency and aesthetic evolution over time.
- Thermoluminescence (TL) and optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) measure trapped electrons released upon heating or light exposure to date fired ceramics, burnt flint, and windblown sediment.
- Accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) revolutionized radiocarbon analysis by directly counting carbon atoms, thereby requiring microscopic sample sizes and protecting invaluable specimens from destructive analysis.
