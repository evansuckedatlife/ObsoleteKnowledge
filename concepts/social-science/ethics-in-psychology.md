## summary

**Ethics in psychology** is the field of moral and professional standards governing research on human and animal subjects, including requirements for informed consent, protection from harm, confidentiality, and institutional oversight. The field emerged from landmark violations—notably the [[Little Albert]] experiment (1920), [[Milgram's obedience experiments]] (1961–1962), and the [[Stanford Prison Experiment]] (1971)—which exposed the psychological damage researchers could inflict and prompted the development of ethics boards and formal research protocols.

## you gotta know

- Early psychology lacked ethical safeguards; researchers conducted experiments on vulnerable populations (infants, prisoners, psychiatric patients) without informed consent or regard for psychological harm.
- The [[Little Albert]] experiment traumatized an 11-month-old by conditioning a fear of white rats; Albert suffered lasting phobia and was never desensitized, highlighting the failure to protect research subjects.
- [[Stanley Milgram]]'s obedience experiments led participants to believe they were delivering lethal electric shocks; participants experienced severe psychological distress, and many emerged believing they had harmed another human.
- The [[Stanford Prison Experiment]] induced psychological breakdown in student participants randomly assigned to "guard" and "prisoner" roles; the study was halted early because conditions became abusive and dehumanizing.
- The [[Nuremberg Code]] (1947), developed after Nazi medical atrocities, first established the principle of informed consent; psychology adopted similar standards through the [[American Psychological Association]]'s ethics code.
- Modern institutional review boards (IRBs) now require risk-benefit analysis, informed consent, confidentiality protections, and the right to withdraw; violations can result in funding loss and professional sanctions.
- Ongoing debates remain about whether certain landmark studies could ever be approved today, and whether the knowledge gained (about human obedience and conformity) justifies the psychological harm inflicted on participants.

## connections

- [[stanley-milgram]] — his obedience experiments catalyzed modern ethical standards for human research.
- [[behaviorism]] — early behaviorists like [[John B. Watson]] experimented on infants with minimal ethical restraint.
- [[cold-war]] — ethical violations often escalated under Cold War competition; fear of falling behind drove risky research.
- [[world-war-ii]] — Nazi medical experimentation exposed the dangers of unregulated research and prompted the first formal ethics codes.
- [[ivan-pavlov]] — pioneered experimental psychology on animals and humans; his methods influenced later psychology but lacked ethical oversight.
- [[william-james]] — promoted empirical psychology but was cautious about mechanistic reduction and harm to subjects.
