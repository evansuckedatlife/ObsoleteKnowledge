## summary

**Code-switching** is the practice of alternating between two or more languages or dialects within a single conversation or even a single sentence. Multilingual speakers employ code-switching strategically to navigate different social contexts, express identity, or convey meaning that a single code cannot capture. Rather than a deficit or confusion, code-switching is a sophisticated linguistic resource and a window into how language intertwines with identity and community.

## you gotta know

- Bilingual and multilingual speakers code-switch routinely; it is not a sign of linguistic confusion but rather evidence of linguistic competence—the ability to deploy multiple codes appropriately.
- Situational code-switching shifts which language is used based on context: speakers might use the heritage language at home, English at school, and Spanish with friends, selecting the appropriate code for the audience and setting.
- Intra-sentential code-switching (switching within a single sentence) requires deep grammatical knowledge of both languages; linguists initially thought such mixing was impossible, but research proved it follows systematic rules.
- Code-switching serves social functions: marking group identity, creating solidarity with others who share multilingualism, or establishing social distance; it signals "us" and "them" linguistically.
- Prestige hierarchies shape code-switching: speakers often shift toward higher-prestige codes (colonial languages, standard varieties) in formal contexts and toward vernacular codes in casual settings, reflecting broader power structures.
- Heritage language speakers often code-switch to accommodate monolingual listeners or because they are more fluent in one language for certain domains (e.g., technical terms in English, family vocabulary in heritage language).
- Colonial and postcolonial contexts intensify code-switching; formerly colonized peoples navigate languages imposed by colonizers alongside ancestral languages, with code-switching becoming a daily linguistic reality.
- Language contact itself drives code-switching; when communities speak multiple languages, speakers develop code-switching norms that become part of the group's linguistic identity (e.g., Spanglish in US Hispanic communities).
