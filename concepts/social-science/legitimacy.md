## summary

**Legitimacy** is the socially recognized right to exercise power, transforming raw coercion or physical domination into acknowledged [[authority]]. Within political philosophy and the social sciences, it explains why populations willingly accept institutional hierarchies and comply with legal rules without the constant application of force. First rigorously systematized during the [[enlightenment]] and later expanded by nineteenth- and twentieth-century sociologists, **legitimate authority** forms the bedrock of political stability, meaning that governance loses its power to command obedience whenever its moral or procedural foundations are successfully questioned.

## you gotta know

- *Max Weber*'s tripartite classification of legitimate rule in *Economy and Society* distinguishes between traditional authority (derived from longstanding custom and heredity), charismatic authority (flowing from extraordinary personal magnetism or heroism), and rational-legal authority (rooted in codified statutes, administrative regulations, and official competence).
- In the landmark obedience experiments conducted by *[[stanley-milgram]]*, subjects complied with harmful orders when delivered by a perceived scientific figure, but defiance spiked dramatically when that experimenter lacked institutional prestige or was contradicted by another authority figure.
- During the [[enlightenment]], theorists like *John Locke* and *Jean-Jacques Rousseau* argued that state legitimacy rests upon a social contract and the consent of the governed, asserting that citizens retain the moral right to alter or overthrow any government that fails to secure their natural rights.
- The collapse of regime legitimacy has historically served as the catalyst for revolutionary upheavals, as seen when fiscal insolvency and perceived administrative corruption precipitated the [[french-revolution]] and shattered monarchical absolutism.
- Social psychologist *Tom Tyler* demonstrated in *Why People Obey the Law* that institutional legitimacy depends heavily on procedural justice—the perceived neutrality, respectfulness, and fairness of legal processes—rather than on the severity of criminal sanctions or personal favorability of outcomes.
- German philosopher and sociologist *Jürgen Habermas* formulated the concept of a *legitimation crisis*, positing that late-capitalist democracies struggle when administrative bureaucracies fail to fulfill economic demands while generating the cultural consensus required to justify state interventions.
- Legal and political scholars distinguish between normative legitimacy (whether a political system satisfies objective moral standards to justify its rule) and empirical legitimacy (the sociological reality of whether subjects internally believe in and defer to the ruling authority).

## connections

- [[authority]] — the institutionalized form of power that depends on perceived legitimacy to secure voluntary compliance rather than relying on coercion.
- [[stanley-milgram]] — social psychologist whose obedience experiments demonstrated that deference to harmful commands requires an apparently legitimate scientific authority.
- [[conformity]] — social process wherein individuals align behaviors with group expectations, often reinforced by legitimate structural hierarchies.
- [[asch-conformity]] — foundational experiment demonstrating social compliance to peer consensus, highlighting the distinction between peer conformity and hierarchical obedience.
- [[enlightenment]] — philosophical movement that redefined political legitimacy around rational-legal constitutions, natural rights, and social contracts.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — legal framework establishing rational-legal legitimacy through codified checks, balances, and popular sovereignty in the [[united-states]].
- [[karl-marx]] — philosopher who criticized bourgeois state legitimacy as an ideological superstructure designed to legitimize class exploitation.
