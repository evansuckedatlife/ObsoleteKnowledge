## summary

**Linguistic typology** is the classification of languages by their recurrent structural features—how they form words (morphology), arrange them (syntax), organize meaning (semantics), and handle grammatical information. Typologists ask questions like: Does this language use prefixes, suffixes, or infixes? Does it express tense before the verb or after? Is its baseline word order subject-verb-object or subject-object-verb? By mapping such features across the world's languages, linguists identify universal patterns, rare structures, and the boundaries of human linguistic possibility.

## you gotta know

- The foundational distinction is *morphological type*: *isolating* languages (Chinese, English) use few inflections and rely on word order; *agglutinative* languages (Turkish, [[Basque]]) stack morphemes linearly and transparently; *fusional* languages (Latin, Russian) combine multiple grammatical meanings into single morphemes; *polysynthetic* languages ([[Navajo]], Inuit) encode entire clauses within single words.
- Word order typology reveals correlations: languages with verb-initial order often use postpositions and possess other rare features together, as if core grammatical decisions cascade through the system.
- [[Basque]] is a typological outlier: an agglutinative language with ergative-absolutive alignment and postpositions, surviving in a region surrounded by Indo-European accusative-nominative languages.
- [[Hungarian]] exemplifies agglutinative structure and postpositional alignment; its vowel harmony and extensive case system make it a standard textbook example of an agglutinative language.
- [[Navajo]] is the canonical example of a polysynthetic language; its verb system encodes subject, object, aspect, mood, and direction within a single word, allowing complete thoughts in a single word.
- Language universals emerge from typology: all languages have nouns and verbs, all languages express negation, all languages can form questions—suggesting deep constraints on human language.
- Typological research has practical value: understanding grammatical features aids language documentation, second-language teaching, and machine translation; it also constrains theories of human cognition and universal grammar.

## connections

- [[linguistics]] — the broader science of language; typology is one major subfield.
- [[noam-chomsky]] — universal grammar theory overlaps with typology; Chomsky asks what features are universal versus parametric.
- [[structural-linguistics]] — typology emerged from structuralist approaches to systematic comparison across languages.
- [[basque]] — a crucial typological outlier exhibiting rare combinations of features.
- [[hungarian]] — a standard example of agglutinative morphology and postpositional syntax in typological surveys.
- [[navajo-language]] — the canonical example of polysynthetic verbal morphology.
- [[linguistic-relativity]] — typological differences correlate with (though do not determine) conceptual differences across cultures.
