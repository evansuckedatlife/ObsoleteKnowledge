## summary

The **Azande** (singular **Zande**, historically also referred to as the **Zande people** or **Asande**) are a Central African ethnic and cultural group inhabiting the border regions of northeastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, South Sudan, and the Central African Republic. In social anthropology and epistemology, they attained global renown through the classic fieldwork of *E. E. Evans-Pritchard*, whose 1937 masterpiece *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic Among the Azande* demonstrated the internal logic and intellectual coherence of indigenous metaphysical systems. Historically governed by an aristocratic *Avongara* nobility, Azande society integrated poison oracles, herbal magic, and witchcraft beliefs into an elegant institutional framework for resolving social conflict and explaining everyday misfortune.

## you gotta know

- British social anthropologist *E. E. Evans-Pritchard* conducted intensive ethnographic fieldwork among the Azande between 1926 and 1930, establishing participant observation as an analytical benchmark in British structural functionalism.
- In Zande thought, witchcraft (*mangu*) is considered a physical substance located beneath the liver, which grows along with the individual and can act psychically and involuntarily to bring harm to rivals.
- Evans-Pritchard famously demonstrated that witchcraft beliefs do not contradict physical cause-and-effect, but rather account for the "second spear" of coincidence, explaining why two independent chains of causation converge on a specific person at a tragic moment (such as termites causing a granary to collapse).
- The poison oracle (*benge*) serves as the supreme judicial authority, wherein an expert administers a strychnine-like forest vine paste to young domestic fowls while posing solemn questions, interpreting the bird's survival or death as an infallible verdict.
- Minor daily divination rites include the rubbing-board oracle (*iwa*), an easily portable carved wooden instrument, and the termite oracle (*dakpa*), which relies on overnight feeding patterns on two sticks placed into an active mound.
- Historically, the Azande developed expansive, militarized tributary kingdoms dominated by the *Avongara* royal dynasty, who retained exclusive judicial monopoly over legal accusations by owning the state's poison supplies.
- Unlike their pastoral Nilotic neighbors the [[nuer]], the Azande are traditionally sedentary hoe farmers living in dispersed homesteads across wooded savanna, cultivating eleusine millet, cassava, maize, and sweet potatoes.
- Evans-Pritchard's ethnographic account triggered major philosophical discussions through thinkers like *Peter Winch*, serving as a foundational case study in cultural relativism, rationality, and translation between distinct worldviews.

## connections

- [[culture]] — the total complex of shared customs, kinship values, and cognitive models that structure everyday Azande communal existence.
- [[ritual]] — the strictly codified performance of divination protocols, magical medicines, and royal court judgments.
- [[symbolism]] — the rich metaphorical and material idioms, such as *benge* and *mangu*, used to interpret causality and morality.
- [[authority]] — the centralized political and juridical power exercised by the *Avongara* ruling aristocracy over commoner subjects.
- [[colonialism]] — British and Belgian imperial policies that forcibly resettled dispersed Azande families into concentrated roadside hamlets to enforce sleeping sickness controls.
- [[nuer]] — neighboring South Sudanese cattle pastoralists studied by Evans-Pritchard in his subsequent structuralist ethnography.
- [[bantu-languages]] — neighboring linguistic family whose historical northern forest boundary abuts the Zande language area in the Congo basin.
- [[bronislaw-malinowski]] — functionalist anthropological pioneer whose participant observation methodology directly informed Evans-Pritchard's early field investigations.
