## summary

**Grammar** comprises the system of rules governing how words combine to form sentences and how meanings are conveyed within a language. It encompasses phonology (sound systems), morphology (word structure), and syntax (sentence structure)—the formal architecture that allows speakers to produce and understand infinite utterances from finite resources. Grammar is universal across human languages, though its specific forms vary dramatically.

## you gotta know

- Grammar includes *phonology* (which sounds matter), *morphology* (how words are built from smaller meaningful units), and *syntax* (how words combine into phrases and sentences).
- Speakers possess *implicit* grammatical knowledge—they follow rules without conscious awareness; linguists infer grammar by examining patterns in language use.
- *Generative grammar*, pioneered by *Noam Chomsky*, proposes that grammar is a finite set of rules producing infinite sentences; this transformed linguistics from cataloguing to theory-building.
- *Panini*, an ancient Sanskrit grammarian, produced the first systematic grammar around 400 BCE—one of history's most precise linguistic descriptions, with over 4,000 rules.
- Grammar varies across languages: some have elaborate case systems, others rely on word order; some fuse meaning into single words while others spread it across many; yet all languages have grammar.
- Children acquire grammar implicitly through exposure in infancy, without formal instruction—a process so robust that linguists debate whether core grammar is innate.
- *Prescriptive* grammar (rules about "correct" language) differs from *descriptive* grammar (rules speakers actually follow); linguists focus on description, not prescription, recognizing that living languages change and evolve.
- *Universal grammar* hypothesizes that despite surface differences, all human grammars share deep structural principles—constraints on word order, argument structure, and transformations that appear across all language families.
