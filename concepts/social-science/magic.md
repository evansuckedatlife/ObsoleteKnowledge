## summary

**Magic** is the belief and practice that thoughts, words, or ritual actions can directly influence the physical world through supernatural means—a universal human category that anthropologists and historians identify across cultures, historical periods, and social strata. [[James Frazer]] famously distinguished *sympathetic magic* (the belief that similar or connected things affect each other) from *contagious magic* (the belief that things once in contact remain mysteriously linked), and argued that magical thinking represented a stage in human intellectual development preceding religious and scientific thought. Though Frazer's evolutionary framework has been critiqued, magic remains central to understanding ritual practice, literary imagination, and the persistent boundaries between rational and irrational belief.

## you gotta know

- *Sympathetic magic* operates on the principle "like produces like"—voodoo dolls, wax figurines, or effigies are believed to affect the person they represent through imitative correspondence.
- *Contagious magic* assumes that things once in contact remain mystically linked; a lock of hair, fingernail, or article of clothing allows magic to harm or heal the original person.
- [[James Frazer]]'s *The Golden Bough* analyzed magic cross-culturally, proposing that pre-modern societies moved from magical thinking through religious propitiation to scientific empiricism—a thesis later critiqued as ethnocentrically evolutionary.
- Magic is not merely prescientific superstition; it serves social functions: creating community identity, managing anxiety, ritualizing transitions, and processing trauma when empirical explanation fails.
- Literary magic—in [[The Tempest]], [[Morgan le Fay]]'s enchantments, and fantasy traditions—often encodes philosophical questions about power, knowledge, agency, and the limits of human will.
- Medieval and early modern [[Europe]] prosecuted thousands of women and men as witches, often conflating folk healing, herbalism, and unauthorized knowledge with demonic pacts—demonstrating how magical accusations served political and gender violence.
- Anthropologists distinguish between *folk magic* (village healers, lucky charms, protective rituals), *religious magic* (ritually sanctioned sacred acts), and *literary magic* (invented for narrative effect); these categories overlap and shift across time and place.

## connections

- [[ritual]] — magic and ritual are closely intertwined; ritual actions are believed to have supernatural efficacy in magical worldviews.
- [[mythology]] — magic is a recurrent theme in mythological narratives; gods, heroes, and supernatural beings wield magical power.
- [[religion]] — [[James Frazer]] argued magic preceded religion evolutionarily; modern practice shows they coexist and blend constantly.
- [[james-frazer]] — anthropologist whose comparative analysis of magic across cultures defined the modern academic study of magical thinking.
- [[enlightenment]] — the intellectual movement that declared magic irrational and sought to replace it with empirical science; yet magical belief persists.
- [[the-tempest]] — [[William Shakespeare]]'s drama in which [[Prospero]]'s magic drives the entire plot and raises questions about power, knowledge, and colonialism.
- [[morgan-le-fay]] — the enchantress whose magical mastery defines her agency and complexity in [[Arthurian legend]].
