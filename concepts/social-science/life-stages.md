## summary

**Life stages** are the age-graded periods that human development passes through, each characterized by distinctive biological, psychological, and social challenges and achievements. From infancy through old age, individuals confront predictable developmental tasks—establishing autonomy, forming identity, building relationships, pursuing achievement, and integrating life experience. Life-stage frameworks help psychologists and educators understand development as a structured process rather than a simple progression of years.

## you gotta know

- *Erik Erikson* proposed eight psychosocial stages spanning infancy to old age, each defined by a central conflict between opposing impulses (e.g., trust vs. mistrust in infancy, autonomy vs. shame in toddlerhood).
- *Childhood* is marked by rapid physical growth, emerging autonomy, and socialization into cultural roles; the period involves dependency on caregivers and vulnerability to attachment disruption.
- *Adolescence* involves puberty, identity formation, and peer orientation; the period is characterized by questioning authority, experimenting with roles, and shifting from parental dependence to peer interdependence.
- *Early adulthood* centers on autonomy, career establishment, and intimate relationships; tasks include choosing occupation, forming partnerships, and establishing independence from family of origin.
- *Middle adulthood* often involves mentoring younger generations, maintaining relationships and careers, and confronting mortality; this generative phase may involve midlife reassessment and renewed purpose.
- *Late adulthood* brings integration of life experience and confrontation with mortality; successful aging involves reflecting on accomplishments, maintaining relationships and activities, and finding meaning in retrospect.
- *Continuity theory* suggests that personality and coping styles remain relatively stable across life stages; people continue engaging in familiar activities and roles even as bodies and social circumstances change.
- Each life stage presents distinctive *developmental tasks*: infants must form secure attachments, adolescents must establish identity, middle-aged adults often confront questions of legacy and meaning, while older adults work toward ego integrity and acceptance.
