## summary

**Archaeology** is the scientific study of human societies through the analysis of their material remains—buildings, tools, art, bones, seeds, and artifacts buried or preserved by time. Archaeologists reconstruct how people lived, what they valued, what they ate, and how they organized themselves, using evidence that written records often ignore (the lives of women, slaves, the poor, the marginalized). Archaeology reveals civilizations without written histories and exposes assumptions about progress and inevitability that written records often encode.

## you gotta know

- Archaeology is fundamentally destructive; excavation removes artifacts from their stratigraphic context (the layers in which they are embedded), so meticulous documentation and careful technique are essential to preserve the interpretive value of dig sites.
- Stratigraphy (the analysis of layers) is archaeology's primary dating method; objects in lower layers are older than those in upper layers (the law of superposition), allowing chronological sequencing without absolute dates.
- Radiocarbon dating, tree-ring dating (dendrochronology), and thermoluminescence are scientific dating methods that archaeologists use to assign absolute ages to artifacts; these methods revolutionized chronology in the 20th century.
- Material culture (the objects people make and use) reveals aspects of society that texts often obscure; the distribution of pottery styles shows trade networks, the construction of monumental sites shows labor organization, the preservation of weapons shows conflict.
- Archaeology has revealed that civilizations thought to be "primitive" (the Indus Valley civilization, Cahokia, Zimbabwe) were actually sophisticated urban centers with monumental architecture, standardized weights, and extensive trade networks.
- Zooarchaeology (the study of animal remains) and paleoethnobotany (the study of ancient plants) reveal diets, agricultural practices, environmental changes, and human adaptation; these lines of evidence are often more reliable than written accounts.
- Colonialism deeply shaped early archaeology; 19th-century archaeologists often looted artifacts, destroyed sites, and constructed narratives justifying European superiority and colonial rule; postcolonial archaeology now works to decolonize interpretation and repatriate artifacts.
- Contemporary archaeology employs computer modeling, satellite imaging, ground-penetrating radar, and genetic analysis; these technological tools allow archaeologists to map sites without excavating and to extract information from degraded remains.
