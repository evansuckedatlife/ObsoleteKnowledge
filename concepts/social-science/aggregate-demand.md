## summary

**Aggregate demand**, frequently designated **AD**, represents the total planned expenditure on all final goods and services produced within an economy at a given overall price level and time period. Formulated comprehensively within macroeconomic theory, it combines four main components: consumer spending, gross domestic private investment, government purchases, and net exports. Understanding aggregate shifts in expenditure enables economists and policymakers to analyze macroeconomic fluctuations, involuntary unemployment, and inflationary or recessionary gaps.

## you gotta know

- Expressed mathematically as the national accounting identity *AD = C + I + G + (X - M)*, summing household consumption, private investment, government expenditure, and net exports.
- Rose to analytical dominance through *John Maynard Keynes* in his 1936 treatise *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, which disputed Say's Law and asserted that deficient total spending, rather than rigid wages alone, caused protracted economic slumps.
- Contrasted directly with classical political economy originating in *18th-century Britain*, where theorists like *Adam Smith* emphasized productive capacity, supply creation, and self-regulating market equilibria.
- Plotted as a downward-sloping curve against the aggregate price level due to the wealth effect, the interest rate effect, and the exchange rate effect, which collectively depress domestic purchases when domestic price indexes increase.
- Incorporated into the IS-LM apparatus developed by *John Hicks* and *Alvin Hansen*, modeling the interaction between the goods market and money market to derive the equilibrium expenditure curve.
- Subjected to intense debate during the monetarist counter-revolution led by *Milton Friedman*, who argued that variations in the money supply dominate total spending fluctuations over the business cycle.
- Serves as the principal target of countercyclical fiscal policy and monetary policy, designed to bridge output gaps during severe downturns like the Great Depression and modern financial crises.
