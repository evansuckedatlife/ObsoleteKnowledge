## summary

The **Paleolithic**, or Old Stone Age, is the longest period of human prehistory, spanning roughly 2.6 million years until about 10,000 years ago. Defined by the use of stone tools and subsistence through hunting and gathering, the Paleolithic encompasses the emergence of the human genus, the evolution of Homo sapiens, the colonization of the globe, and the development of language, art, and ritual—the foundations of human culture.

## you gotta know

- The Paleolithic is divided into three periods: Lower (2.6 million–300,000 years ago, associated with *Homo habilis* and *Homo erectus*), Middle (300,000–30,000 years ago, associated with Neanderthals and early modern humans), and Upper (50,000–10,000 years ago, defined by behavioral modernity and rapid innovation).
- Stone tool technology evolved from simple choppers and flakes to sophisticated hand axes, blades, and specialized implements, reflecting increasing cognitive sophistication and planning; typology of tools reveals cultural variation between regions.
- Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania preserves some of the earliest evidence of stone tool use, dating to roughly 2.6 million years ago, marking the boundary between the Paleolithic and earlier primate existence.
- Upper Paleolithic humans produced cave paintings, figurines, and decorated tools—the first unmistakable evidence of symbolic thought, artistic expression, and cultural meaning-making; cave art sites span from France to Indonesia.
- All Paleolithic societies were hunter-gatherers, organizing in small mobile bands and subsisting on game, fish, and plant foods gathered from the environment; population density was low but sophisticated.
- The Paleolithic witnessed human migration out of Africa to colonize all continents except Antarctica, an achievement requiring technological and social innovation; some scholars debate multiple waves of migration.
- Language almost certainly developed during the Paleolithic, though its emergence cannot be precisely dated; linguistic capacity is assumed in Upper Paleolithic humans given their symbolic and artistic achievements.
- Control of fire—documented by 500,000 years ago and possibly earlier—allowed cooking, warmth, tool-hardening, and social gathering; it was revolutionary for nutrition, predator defense, and social organization.
- Paleolithic art and burial practices suggest religious or spiritual beliefs; intentional burials with grave goods indicate concern for an afterlife or meaningful commemoration of the dead.
- Gender roles in Paleolithic societies likely reflected division of labor and reproductive demands; evidence from skeletal remains, tool marks, and ethnographic analogy suggests complex, varied social structures.
