## summary

**Language acquisition** is the process by which children learn their native language—or languages—through exposure to speech in their social environment. Despite beginning with minimal vocabulary and simple structures, children rapidly master the grammar, pronunciation, and usage conventions of their native language within a few years. This achievement is remarkably consistent across cultures and conditions, suggesting both environmental input and innate biological predispositions shape the process.

## you gotta know

- Children follow a *predictable developmental sequence*: cooing and babbling (0–6 months), first words (12–18 months), two-word combinations (18–24 months), rapid vocabulary expansion (2–3 years), and grammatical sophistication (3+ years).
- *Vocabulary explosion* occurs around age 18–24 months when children suddenly accelerate from hundreds to thousands of words; the mechanism triggering this remains debated but suggests a qualitative shift in learning mechanisms.
- Children produce *overgeneralizations*—applying grammatical rules too broadly (saying "goed" instead of "went", "two mouses" instead of "two mice")—revealing that they extract and apply rules rather than simply imitating speech.
- *Critical period* evidence suggests exposure during early childhood is crucial; children deprived of language input in early years face permanent difficulties acquiring full linguistic competence, even with later intensive instruction.
- *Universal grammar*, proposed by *Noam Chomsky*, posits that infants arrive with innate knowledge of grammatical principles, explaining how they acquire complex structures so rapidly from limited input.
- *Steven Pinker* and others argue children are innately prepared to learn language; the consistency of acquisition across cultures and the rapidity of learning suggest biological adaptation shaped by evolution.
- *Bilingual* children acquire multiple languages simultaneously during early childhood, demonstrating remarkable capacity; they keep languages partly separate (avoiding interference) while also showing cross-linguistic influence in specific features.
