## summary

**Stages of development** are discrete periods across the human lifespan characterized by distinct patterns of physical, cognitive, emotional, and social capabilities. [[jean-piaget]]'s theory of [[cognitive-development]] proposed four major stages (sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, formal operational) that fundamentally shaped developmental psychology. The stage approach assumes that development proceeds through qualitatively different phases, each with characteristic limitations and achievements.

## you gotta know

- Piaget's sensorimotor stage (0-2 years) emphasizes learning through physical interaction and movement; infants lack object permanence early in this stage.
- The preoperational stage (2-7 years) marks the emergence of symbolic thinking and language, but children remain egocentric and struggle with conservation tasks.
- In the concrete operational stage (7-11 years), children can perform logical operations on concrete objects but struggle with abstract hypothetical reasoning.
- The formal operational stage (12+ years) marks the capacity for abstract thinking, hypothesis testing, and formal logic—though not all adolescents or adults consistently use formal operations.
- [[erik-erikson]] proposed eight stages spanning the entire lifespan (not just childhood), each defined by a psychosocial crisis to be resolved.
- Stage-based models assume development is sequential (cannot skip stages), universal (all humans follow the same sequence), and qualitatively distinct (each stage involves different cognitive structures, not just more knowledge).
- Subsequent research has challenged pure stage models; development appears more continuous than stage theory suggests, and culture shapes developmental trajectories.
- Critics note that stage models can overemphasize discontinuity and overlook the gradual, context-dependent nature of development in real children.
- Individual variation within stages is substantial; not all children develop at the same pace, and some may demonstrate capacities from multiple stages simultaneously rather than fitting neatly into one.
- Cross-cultural research reveals that the age ranges Piaget identified are culturally variable; concrete operational thinking emerges at different ages depending on schooling, cultural practices, and exposure to formal logic.

## connections

- [[jean-piaget]] — the psychologist who developed the most influential stage theory of cognitive development.
- [[cognitive-development]] — the specific domain of development that stage theories most emphasize.
- [[developmental-psychology]] — the broader discipline encompassing all approaches to human development across the lifespan.
- [[erik-erikson]] — psychoanalytic theorist who extended stage thinking to the entire lifespan and emphasized social-emotional development.
- [[constructivism]] — the epistemological foundation underlying Piaget's stage theory; learners actively construct understanding.
- [[childhood]] — the period most extensively studied through stage-based developmental theories.
