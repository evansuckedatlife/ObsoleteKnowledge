## summary

**Dehumanization** is the psychological process by which individuals or groups come to view others as less than human, stripping them of moral status and individuality. This mental shift enables ordinary people to commit cruelty and abuse that they would normally find intolerable. The Stanford Prison Experiment famously demonstrated how dehumanization can emerge rapidly in an institutional setting. It is not primarily a pathology of evil people, but rather a common psychological mechanism activated under conditions of power asymmetry, social distance, and institutional authority.

## you gotta know

- A *cognitive shift* that reduces perceived humanity and moral worth in another person or group.
- Makes cruelty feel psychologically permissible by removing empathy and moral restraint.
- Occurs through language ("them," "those people"), visual markers (uniforms, stereotypes), and role assignment.
- Demonstrated dramatically in *Philip Zimbardo's* Stanford Prison Experiment, where guards dehumanized prisoners within days.
- Guards began using deindividuation tactics: removing prisoner names, assigning numbers, stripping dignity.
- Historical examples include slavery, genocide, war atrocities, and systemic discrimination.
- Operates through multiple pathways: physical distance, institutional authority, diffusion of responsibility, and ideological justification.
- Reversible — awareness of dehumanization and moral reframing can restore recognition of shared humanity.
- *Stanley Milgram's* obedience studies showed how participants could harm others when a dehumanizing institutional frame and authority figure removed personal responsibility.
- Dehumanization often begins with abstraction — referring to groups by numbers, biological terms, or metaphors ("vermin," "disease") rather than personal names.
- Modern contexts include online harassment and hate speech, where anonymity and distance reduce inhibitions against dehumanizing language.
