## summary

**Identity** is the set of characteristics, memberships, and narratives through which individuals and groups understand themselves and are understood by others. Identity is simultaneously psychological (internal sense of self) and social (recognized by others and embedded in institutions). Identity is constructed—not innate—through ongoing interaction with culture, history, relationships, and material conditions. Questions of identity are central to sociology, psychology, literature, and political struggle.

## you gotta know

- Identity encompasses multiple dimensions: gender, ethnicity, nationality, class, religion, sexuality, and profession—each negotiated differently across contexts and cultures.
- *Social identity theory* (Tajfel and Turner) shows that people derive self-esteem and meaning from group membership and often systematically favor in-groups over out-groups, even arbitrarily formed ones.
- Identity is performed and fluid; people emphasize different aspects in different settings (speaking differently at home vs. school vs. work) rather than possessing one fixed, unchanging identity.
- Postcolonial and diaspora studies highlight how colonialism, slavery, and forced migration fragment identity and create contested belonging—the search for roots amid fragmentation.
- Literary tradition of the *double* (*Dostoevsky's* *Crime and Punishment*, *Stevenson's* *Jekyll and Hyde*) explores psychological fragmentation and the instability of unified self-identity under stress.
- *Intersectionality*—Kimberlé Crenshaw's framework that identity operates along overlapping axes (race, gender, class, sexuality) creating distinct power positions—has become central to understanding inequality and justice.
- Identity formation is shaped not just by internal psychology but by external recognition: whether others acknowledge and validate one's claimed identity profoundly affects psychological wellbeing.
- Queer and transgender studies highlight how identity is both deeply personal and socially constructed; self-understanding requires both internal authenticity and social legibility—a tension that reveals identity's fundamentally relational nature.
