## summary

**Allocative efficiency** is a state of the economy in which production represents consumer preferences, occurring when goods and services are distributed such that marginal benefit equals marginal cost. When this condition holds, total social surplus is maximized and net deadweight loss is eliminated across competitive markets. It serves as a benchmark in welfare economics to evaluate whether resources are channeled into their highest-valued uses or distorted by market failures.

## you gotta know

- Defined mathematically at the output level where the price paid by consumers equals the marginal cost of production ($P = MC$).
- Maximizes the sum of consumer surplus and producer surplus, leaving no unrealized gains from trade or economic deadweight loss.
- Contrasts with *productive efficiency*, which requires producing goods at the lowest possible point on the average total cost curve ($P = \text{min } ATC$).
- Achieved in long-run equilibrium under *perfect competition*, where individual firms act as price-takers and market forces align private margins with social costs.
- Violated systematically by monopolies, cartels, and price-setting firms, which restrict equilibrium output to charge prices above marginal cost ($P > MC$).
- Undermined by externalities, public goods, and market failures where private calculations diverge from marginal social benefits or marginal social costs.
- Reduced directly by market interventions such as tariffs, price ceilings, price floors, and excise taxes that drive a wedge between consumer valuation and producer cost.

## connections

- [[allocation]] — the broader economic process of dividing scarce productive resources among competing uses and goods.
- [[supply-and-demand]] — the core analytical model whose market clearing equilibrium directly reflects allocative efficiency under competitive conditions.
- [[adam-smith]] — the Scottish philosopher whose classic concept of the "invisible hand" anticipated the self-regulating attainment of social welfare via competitive markets.
- [[karl-marx]] — the political theorist who critiqued capitalist price-driven allocation as fundamentally alienating, crisis-prone, and exploitative.
- [[john-maynard-keynes]] — the economist who demonstrated that aggregate demand shortfalls can cause persistent underemployment and inefficient resource utilization.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — the historical era that transformed mechanized production, compelling classical and neoclassical economists to formalize efficiency and distribution theory.
