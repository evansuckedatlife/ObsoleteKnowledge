## summary

**Productivity** measures the efficiency of economic production—the output generated per unit of input, typically measured as output per worker (labor productivity) or per unit of capital invested. Often considered the deepest driver of long-term economic growth, productivity increases when workers produce more goods or services in the same time, or produce the same output with less time or resources.

## you gotta know

- Labor productivity is typically calculated as real output divided by hours of labor; nations with higher labor productivity per hour generate greater wealth per worker.
- The Industrial Revolution's revolutionary impact on human prosperity stemmed fundamentally from massive productivity increases; mechanization allowed one worker to produce vastly more than before; this launched modern economic growth.
- Productivity improvements arise from multiple sources: technological innovation (better tools and machines), worker skill development (education and training), organizational efficiency (better management and processes), and capital investment (more and better equipment per worker).
- Total factor productivity (TFP) captures improvements beyond simply adding more labor or capital; it reflects the efficiency with which all inputs are combined, often attributed to technological progress and innovation.
- Paradoxes arise: computerization was expected to dramatically raise productivity, but measured improvements have been surprisingly modest—the "productivity paradox" suggesting measurement difficulties or lags in adoption; this puzzled economists in the 1990s.
- Productivity varies significantly across sectors and countries; service sectors typically show lower productivity growth than manufacturing; developing nations often have lower productivity levels than developed ones.
- Productivity growth tends to slow over time within technology cycles; rapid gains from a new technology eventually plateau as adoption becomes universal and further innovations become harder.
- Long-term economic growth is nearly synonymous with productivity growth; the difference between a rich and poor nation is fundamentally a difference in productivity per worker.
- Environmental and social costs sometimes go unmeasured in productivity metrics; an economy might boost output per worker while degrading the environment or exploiting workers, so productivity alone is an incomplete measure of progress.
- Productivity improvements can displace workers; automation raises output per worker but eliminates jobs, creating social tension; policy responses range from retraining programs to redistributive taxation.
