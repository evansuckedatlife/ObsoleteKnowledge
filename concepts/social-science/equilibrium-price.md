## summary

**Equilibrium price** is the price at which the quantity of a good that producers wish to supply exactly matches the quantity that consumers wish to demand. At this price, the market "clears" — there is neither surplus nor shortage. This concept forms the cornerstone of market economics, illustrating how prices coordinate production and consumption through the interaction of supply and demand.

## you gotta know

- Occurs at the *intersection of supply and demand curves* on an economic graph.
- Represents the price toward which a free market *naturally gravitates* absent artificial barriers.
- If the price is *too low*, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, creating shortage and upward pressure on price.
- If the price is *too high*, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, creating surplus and downward pressure on price.
- Assumes that price adjusts freely and that both buyers and sellers respond rationally to price signals.
- *Stable* when small deviations from equilibrium trigger forces that restore it (a characteristic of competitive markets).
- Varies across goods and across time; agricultural commodities may achieve equilibrium seasonally.
- *Fictional ideal* — real markets rarely achieve perfect equilibrium due to transaction costs, information gaps, and price rigidities.
- *Sticky prices* (especially wages) prevent markets from clearing instantly; adjustment may take weeks or months.
- *Price discrimination* and market segmentation mean different buyers face different prices for the same good, creating multiple local equilibria.
- Monopolistic and oligopolistic markets may have equilibria where producers deliberately maintain prices above competitive levels to extract surplus.
