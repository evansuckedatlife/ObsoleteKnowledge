## summary

**Language change** is the process by which languages evolve over time, altering their sounds, grammar, and vocabulary. Studied through diachronic linguistics, language change operates through mechanisms like sound shifts, analogy, and social factors. *William Labov* pioneered the modern understanding of how language changes, demonstrating that variation and change happen in real time through social forces, not in sudden leaps.

## you gotta know

- Languages are *always changing* — no language is static; this is a universal feature of human communication.
- *Sound change* occurs regularly: English final *-e* disappearing, vowel shifts (like the Great Vowel Shift in Middle English).
- *Grammatical change* involves shifts in morphology and syntax: English losing inflectional case endings, changes in word order patterns.
- *Lexical change* brings new words (borrowing, neologism) and removes old ones (obsolescence); rates vary by social prestige.
- *Variation precedes change* — *Labov* showed that linguistic change happens incrementally, visible in apparent-time studies of living speakers.
- *Social factors drive change*: younger speakers, women, lower-status groups often lead in adopting innovations.
- *Sound changes spread gradually* through a language area, not uniformly; geography and social networks matter.
- *Regularity* — systematic sound changes typically apply consistently across a class of words (the Neogrammarian principle).
- *Internal motivations* for change include analogical reanalysis (e.g., "pea" singular from "pease" collective) and phonetic simplification.
- *Frequency effects*: irregular verbs often resist change (go, went, gone remain irregular); high-frequency words stabilize while rare words shift faster.
- *Functional pressure* can drive grammar changes; loss of case endings increases reliance on word order, triggering syntactic reorganization.

- [[william-labov]] — the pioneer of variationist sociolinguistics and real-time language change studies.

- [[language-variation]] — the synchronic diversity underlying language change.
