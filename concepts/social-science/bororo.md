## summary

The **Bororo**, an Indigenous people of central South America who refer to themselves as the *Boe*, inhabit the Amazonian river basins and scrub plateaus of Mato Grosso in Brazil. They achieved world renown in twentieth-century structural anthropology through the field ethnographies of French anthropologist *Claude Lévi-Strauss*, particularly his classic accounts in *Tristes Tropiques* and *The Raw and the Cooked*. Their elaborate dualist kinship structures, spatial village geometry, bird iconography, and complex funerary rites remain foundational touchstones for the comparative study of cosmology, myth, and social [[belonging]].

## you gotta know

- Village architecture follows a famous circular layout where residential huts are arranged along the outer ring while the central communal men's house, known as the *baito*, serves as the hub of sacred [[ritual]] and civic assembly.
- Social life is organized around rigid structural dualism, dividing the community into two exogamous, matrilineal moieties named the *Exerae* and the *Tugarege*, which regulate marriage alliances, ceremonial responsibilities, and spatial distribution.
- Subject of the famous anthropological puzzle formulated by *Lucien Lévy-Bruhl* and later re-examined by *Claude Lévi-Strauss* and *J. Christopher Crocker*, concerning the native assertion "We are red macaws" (*araras*), illuminating totemic [[symbolism]] and metaphor.
- Featured extensively in the early chapters of *Claude Lévi-Strauss*'s 1955 autobiographical travelogue *Tristes Tropiques*, where their social layout was analyzed to illustrate structuralist principles of human mental architecture.
- Extended funerary ceremonies constitute their most elaborate ceremonial practice, lasting several weeks and requiring collective hunting expeditions, singing, feather headdresses, and secondary burial to guide ancestral spirits (*aroe*) to the afterlife.
- Traditional gender dynamics balance matrilineal kinship and matrilocal residential authority with male dominance in public oratorical debates, hunting rituals, and secret ceremonial fraternities within the *baito*.
- Faced devastating demographic collapse, territorial expropriation, and Christianization campaigns during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries due to Portuguese and Brazilian cattle ranching expansion and Salesian missions.

## connections

- [[native-americans]] — the indigenous peoples of the Western Hemisphere whose distinct lowland South American cultural traditions the Boe embody.
- [[culture]] — the integrated structural matrix of kinship, oral mythology, and material artifacts governing Bororo daily life.
- [[ritual]] — the intricate mortuary sequences, body painting conventions, and spirit invocations essential to cosmic stability.
- [[symbolism]] — the complex totemic and metaphorical associations linking clans to birds, animals, and cosmic moieties.
- [[colonialism]] — the historical encroachment of European frontiers, epidemic diseases, and forced missionization that threatened Bororo autonomy.
- [[belonging]] — the structural and psychological cohesion generated by lifelong membership in complementary moieties and maternal clans.
