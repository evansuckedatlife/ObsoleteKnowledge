# Deadweight Loss

## summary

**Deadweight loss** is the reduction in total economic surplus (the sum of consumer and producer benefit) caused by market inefficiencies. It represents goods and services that could have been produced but were not, or were produced and consumed at suboptimal levels, resulting in a net loss of value to society.

## you gotta know

- Arises when market quantity or price deviates from the *socially optimal level* found in perfectly competitive markets under [[supply-and-demand]].
- *Monopolies* create deadweight loss by restricting output below the competitive level; they charge higher prices and produce less, leaving some potential trades unrealized; consumers lose surplus and producers capture less than they would in perfect competition.
- *Tariffs* and import quotas restrict trade and create deadweight loss by raising domestic prices above world prices, reducing the gains from specialization; domestic consumers pay more, foreign producers sell less, and trades that would have benefited both parties do not occur.
- *Tax incidence* creates deadweight loss — the economic distortion caused by a tax exceeds the revenue collected, as consumers and producers alter behaviour to avoid taxation; the larger the tax rate, the larger the deadweight loss.
- In a perfectly competitive market, price equals marginal cost, and quantity is at the point where marginal benefit equals marginal cost — **no deadweight loss**.
- The size of deadweight loss depends on *price elasticity* — the more elastic the market (more responsive to price changes), the larger the loss from a given distortion.
- Deadweight loss is invisible in GDP accounting but represents real forgone value; it motivates arguments for deregulation, antitrust enforcement, and free trade.
- Pigouvian taxes (corrective taxes on externalities) can *reduce* deadweight loss by internalizing costs; however, most real-world taxes create deadweight loss because they distort production and consumption choices.

## connections

- [[monopoly]] — creates the classic deadweight loss case by restricting output to maximize profit.
- [[tariff]] — protectionist trade barriers generate deadweight loss by raising prices and reducing specialization.
- [[supply-and-demand]] — the intersection of these curves marks the efficient quantity; deviation creates deadweight loss.
- [[perfect-competition]] — the benchmark against which deadweight loss is measured.
- [[market-failure]] — deadweight loss is one symptom of markets failing to allocate resources efficiently.
- [[allocative-efficiency]] — the goal of resource allocation; deadweight loss measures deviation from it.
- [[price-controls]] — price floors and ceilings often create substantial deadweight losses.
