## summary

**Cave painting**, or parietal art, comprises painted, drawn, or stenciled images executed on natural rock walls in subterranean caves and rock shelters during the Upper Paleolithic epoch. Found prominently across western Europe, Southeast Asia, and southern Africa, these prehistoric compositions depict megafauna, abstract signs, and human handprints. In archaeology and paleoanthropology, the practice marks a revolutionary milestone in behavioral modernity, reflecting the emergence of complex figurative representation, mythic cosmology, and communal ritual.

## you gotta know

- Flourished during the *Aurignacian*, *Gravettian*, *Solutrean*, and *Magdalenian* cultural periods of the Late Pleistocene between roughly 40,000 and 12,000 years ago.
- Preserved in famous southwest European sites including *Chauvet Cave*, *Lascaux*, and *Altamira*, known colloquially as the Sistine Chapel of prehistoric art.
- Expanded in global antiquity through remarkable discoveries in *Maros-Pangkep* in Sulawesi, Indonesia, dating figurative depictions of animals back at least 45,000 years.
- Utilized mineral pigments like red and yellow ochre (iron oxides), black manganese dioxide, and charcoal ground into powder and bound with water, saliva, or animal fat.
- Applied pigments via blowing through hollow bird bones, daubing with fingers or moss pads, and sketching with burnt sticks along undulating cave surfaces to create three-dimensional relief.
- Featured negative hand stencils created by spitting pigment over a splayed hand pressed flat against subterranean limestone walls.
- Interpreted through prominent twentieth-century theories ranging from sympathetic hunting magic proposed by *Henri Breuil* to shamanistic trance states and structured cosmological structuralism suggested by *André Leroi-Gourhan*.
