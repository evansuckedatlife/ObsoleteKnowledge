## summary

The **business cycle** describes the alternating pattern of expansion and contraction in economic activity — periods of growth and rising confidence followed by slowdowns, recessions, or depressions. These cycles are inherent to market economies and drive key economic indicators like employment, production, and wealth creation.

## you gotta know

- Consists of four phases: *expansion* (growth accelerates), *peak* (growth reaches maximum), *contraction* (decline begins), and *trough* (minimum activity before recovery).
- *Expansion* periods see rising investment, employment, and consumer spending; firms are optimistic and hire aggressively; capacity utilization rises and inflation pressure builds.
- *Recessions* (two or more consecutive quarters of GDP decline) are contractions that trigger rising unemployment and falling profits; business confidence collapses.
- Interest rates are a primary policy tool — central banks *lower rates* during recessions to spur borrowing and spending, and *raise rates* during overheating to cool inflation.
- Psychological factors amplify cycles: during booms, animal spirits drive excessive investment; during busts, fear and pessimism trigger hoarding and retrenchment.
- The cycle's regularity was noted by 19th-century economists; [[john-maynard-keynes]] emphasized that market economies do not automatically self-correct without intervention.
- No two cycles are identical in length or severity, and predicting turns remains notoriously difficult — even for economists.
- Real asset prices (stocks, housing) tend to rise during expansions and crash during recessions; asset bubbles can exacerbate downturns.
- Lagged effects mean unemployment continues rising even after GDP stops falling; conversely, hiring lags recovery, creating jobless recoveries.
- Automatic stabilizers (unemployment insurance, progressive taxes) cushion cycles but do not eliminate them; fiscal and monetary policy debate centers on whether deliberate intervention smooths cycles or destabilizes them.

## connections

- [[gross-domestic-product]] — the primary measure of total economic output; cyclical fluctuations in GDP define the business cycle itself.
- [[interest-rate]] — the monetary lever central banks use to manage cyclical swings.
- [[inflation]] — typically rises during expansions and falls during contractions; a key concern in managing the cycle.
- [[unemployment]] — rises sharply during recessions, falls during expansions; lags the turning points.
- [[economic-growth]] — the underlying trend; the cycle represents deviations above and below this trend.
- [[supply-and-demand]] — imbalances between supply and demand amplify cyclical swings.
- [[john-maynard-keynes]] — revolutionized thinking about cycles by arguing they require deliberate policy intervention.
