## summary

A **dialect** is a regional or social variety of a language, distinguished by phonological, grammatical, and lexical differences from other varieties. Once dismissed as "substandard" speech, modern sociolinguistics recognizes dialects as linguistically legitimate systems that reflect identity, geography, and social position. No dialect is inherently superior; all are rule-governed variations of the same language. The boundary between "dialect" and "language" is political rather than linguistic—mutual intelligibility is a spectrum, not a binary.

## you gotta know

- Differs from a language chiefly in *mutual intelligibility* — speakers of different dialects can generally understand each other.
- Includes *regional dialects* (Southern American English, Cockney, Sicilian Italian) shaped by geography and isolation.
- Includes *social dialects* (African American Vernacular English, working-class speech) shaped by class, ethnicity, and community.
- *William Labov's* sociolinguistic research elevated dialects from stigmatized variants to proper objects of study.
- Demonstrates that *linguistic prestige is social, not inherent* — "standard" dialects have power because institutions endorse them.
- Often carries *social markers* — a single word choice can signal a speaker's origin, class, or education level.
- Evolves across generations as speakers incorporate new features and adapt to social pressures.
- Serves as a marker of *group identity* and belonging; speakers often code-switch between dialects depending on context.
- *Stigmatization* of non-standard dialects reflects social hierarchies, not linguistic deficiency; all dialects are equally complex rule systems.
- Network effects: speakers in close social networks maintain shared features, while weak ties and geographic mobility spread new variants.
- Youth and women often pioneer linguistic change within their communities, adopting new features that eventually spread more widely.
