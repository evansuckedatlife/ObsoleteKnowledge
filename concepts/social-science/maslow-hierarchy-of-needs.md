# Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs

## summary

**Maslow's hierarchy of needs** is a psychological model proposing that human motivation is organized into a pyramid of five levels, from physiological needs at the base through esteem needs to self-actualization at the apex. Developed by Abraham Maslow in 1943, the framework suggests that individuals must satisfy lower-level needs before pursuing higher ones, though the model's actual predictions have proven more complex than the popular pyramid suggests.

## you gotta know

- The five levels, from bottom to top: physiological needs (food, water, sleep), safety needs (security, stability), love and belonging (relationships, community), esteem needs (respect, confidence, achievement), and self-actualization (fulfilling one's potential).
- Maslow presented the hierarchy as a theory of human motivation, arguing that unsatisfied needs drive behavior and that satisfaction of lower needs creates space for higher pursuits.
- The pyramid has become ubiquitous in business, management, and psychology curricula, making it one of the most recognizable models in the social sciences.
- Maslow's original formulation was more flexible than the rigid pyramid suggests—he acknowledged that people sometimes pursue higher needs despite unmet lower ones (artists starving for their craft, for example).
- The hierarchy is culturally situated; research suggests different cultures prioritize needs in different orders—Western individualism emphasizes self-actualization while other cultures prioritize group belonging earlier.
- Empirical testing of the hierarchy has not strongly supported the strict five-level model or the idea that lower needs must be fully satisfied before higher ones emerge.
- The concept influenced humanistic psychology, organizational theory, and modern positive psychology, which extended his focus on human flourishing.
- Maslow later added a sixth level above self-actualization: *self-transcendence*, or the pursuit of meaning beyond oneself through spirituality, creativity, or service to others.
- Critiques note the model overlooks that deprivation (poverty, trauma) doesn't necessarily prevent higher-level pursuits, and that needs are not always arranged hierarchically but operate simultaneously.
- The hierarchy resonates because it offers an intuitive framework for understanding why people act as they do, yet it oversimplifies the complex, non-linear nature of actual human motivation.
