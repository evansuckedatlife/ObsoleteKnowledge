## summary

The *Montreal Canadiens* are one of ice hockey's most storied and successful franchises, founded in 1909 and competing continuously in the *National Hockey League*. Based in Montreal, Quebec, they represent the sport's most decorated championship tradition, with 24 *Stanley Cup* victories—more than any other franchise. The Canadiens embody French-Canadian hockey culture and have developed some of the sport's greatest players, making them inseparable from hockey history.

## you gotta know

- Founded in 1909, the Canadiens are one of the NHL's oldest franchises, with a continuous existence predating even the modern NHL.
- Won 24 Stanley Cups, the most in NHL history, with a particularly dominant five-year stretch (1956–1960) winning five consecutive championships.
- The 1970s Montreal Canadiens are often considered one of the greatest teams ever assembled, winning four consecutive Stanley Cups (1976–1979).
- *Ken Dryden*, an elite goaltender, anchored the 1970s dynasty with exceptional skill, intelligence, and leadership, becoming a symbol of the team's excellence.
- *Maurice Richard*, nicknamed "the Rocket," was the franchise's first iconic player, representing French-Canadian excellence and competitive passion in hockey.
- The Canadiens play at the Bell Centre (formerly the Montreal Forum), an arena integral to the franchise's identity and hockey history.
- The franchise represents French-Canadian cultural pride and has historically been a symbol of Quebec identity, making the team central to provincial culture.
- *Jean Béliveau*, a captain and forward, epitomized Montreal's combination of skill, elegance, and competitive excellence, playing his entire career with the franchise.
- The 1950s Canadiens dynasty featured a remarkable run of five consecutive Stanley Cups (1956–1960), establishing a winning culture that persisted for decades.
- *Guy Lafleur*, a superstar forward of the 1970s, combined offensive flair with intensity, becoming the face of the team during its greatest era.
- The Original Six era (1942–1967) saw fierce rivalries between Montreal and Toronto, with playoff matchups defining hockey culture across eastern Canada.
- The Canadiens' organizational excellence, scouting system, and development programs made them consistently competitive even during periods without championships.
