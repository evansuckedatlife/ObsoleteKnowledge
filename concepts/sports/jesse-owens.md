## summary

**Jesse Owens**, born **James Cleveland Owens**, was an American track and field athlete who achieved global immortality by winning four gold medals at the 1936 Summer *[[olympic-games]]* in Berlin, *[[germany]]*. His unprecedented victories in the 100 metres, 200 metres, long jump, and 4x100-metre relay dealt a crushing public blow to Adolf Hitler's propaganda of Aryan racial supremacy. Despite his international heroism, he returned home to an era of racial segregation in the *[[united-states]]*, becoming an enduring symbol of athletic excellence and the struggle that prefigured the modern *[[civil-rights-movement]]*.

## you gotta know

- Won four Olympic gold medals at the 1936 Berlin *[[olympic-games]]*, sweeping the 100 meters, 200 meters, long jump, and the 4x100-meter relay in front of Adolf Hitler and the Nazi leadership.
- Dismantled Nazi racial dogma on the world stage, refuting Hitler's claims that Black athletes were inferior and prompting lasting global admiration.
- Engineered what sports historians call the greatest forty-five minutes in sports history on May 25, 1935, at the Big Ten Championships in Ann Arbor, Michigan.
- Set three world records in the long jump, 220-yard sprint, and 220-yard low hurdles, and tied a fourth in the 100-yard dash, all while nursing a severely injured tailbone at that 1935 collegiate meet for Ohio State University.
- Befriended German long jumper *Luz Long* during the 1936 long jump competition after Long advised him to adjust his takeoff mark to avoid fouling out in qualifying.
- Faced entrenched Jim Crow segregation upon his triumphant return to the *[[united-states]]*, where President Franklin D. Roosevelt never invited him to the White House or sent congratulations.
- Forced into humiliating post-Olympic exhibition races against horses, dogs, and local sprint cars because mainstream commercial endorsements were denied to Black champions in the 1930s.
- Awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom by President Gerald Ford in 1976, finally receiving formal federal recognition for his sportsmanship and humanitarian impact.
