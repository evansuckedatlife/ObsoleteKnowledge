## summary

The **Algonquian language family** is a major subfamily of the Algic linguistic stock, encompassing dozens of indigenous languages historically spoken across North America from the Atlantic seaboard to the Rocky Mountains. Reconstructed by historical linguists back to *Proto-Algonquian*, the family includes widely spoken tongues such as *Cree*, *Ojibwe*, *Innu*, *Blackfoot*, and *Cheyenne*. It is celebrated in structural linguistics for its polysynthetic morphology, animate-inanimate grammatical gender distinction, and complex proximate-obviative hierarchies.

## you gotta know

- Subdivided geographically into three primary branches: *Plains Algonquian* (such as Blackfoot, Arapaho, and Cheyenne), *Central Algonquian* (such as Cree, Ojibwe, Menominee, and Miami-Illinois), and the extinct or endangered *Eastern Algonquian* branch.
- Provided dozens of foundational loanwords directly to colonial English, including everyday vocabulary such as *moccasin*, *wigwam*, *tomahawk*, *opossum*, *squash*, *pecan*, and *moose*.
- Characterized grammatically by *polysynthesis*, employing complex verb stems with inflectional prefixes and suffixes that encapsulate an entire clause within a single word.
- Employs a fundamental grammatical division between *animate* and *inanimate* noun genders rather than biological masculine-feminine distinctions.
- Distinguishes multiple third-person participants through *obviation*, marking the central third-person referent as *proximate* and all secondary third-person entities as *obviative* (often termed the "fourth person").
- Studied extensively by structural linguist *Leonard Bloomfield*, whose groundbreaking comparative work on *Proto-Algonquian* proved that the comparative method of historical linguistics applied rigorously to unwritten indigenous languages.
- Included *Massachusett*, the language into which Puritan missionary *John Eliot* translated the landmark 1663 *Eliot Indian Bible*, the first Bible printed anywhere in British North America.

## connections

- [[linguistics]] — scientific discipline that systematized the comparative phonology and morphological reconstruction of ancestral Proto-Algonquian.
- [[colonialism]] — historical expansion of European powers that precipitated violent displacement, linguistic assimilation, and language loss among native peoples.
- [[indian-removal]] — nineteenth-century United States federal policy that forcibly displaced speakers of Sauk-Fox, Kickapoo, Potawatomi, and Miami westward.
- [[united-states]] — republic whose early geography, colonial settlements, and state names (such as Massachusetts and Illinois) derive from Algonquian words.
- [[british-empire]] — imperial authority whose early encounters with Powhatan and Abenaki speakers generated the earliest printed lexicons of eastern coastal idioms.
- [[american-literature]] — literary tradition that frequently adapted, exoticized, or borrowed terms from northeastern tribal languages.
