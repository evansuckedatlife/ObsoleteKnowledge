## summary

The **kirpan** is a curved, single-edged dagger or sword that initiated Sikhs (*Khalsa*) are religiously mandated to wear at all times as an article of faith. Established in 1699 by the tenth Sikh guru, *Guru Gobind Singh*, its name derives from the Punjabi roots *kirpa* (mercy, grace, or kindness) and *aan* (honor, dignity, or self-respect). Far from an offensive weapon of aggression, the blade serves as a physical commitment to defend the weak, resist tyranny, and preserve universal human dignity.

## you gotta know

- Mandated as one of the sacred *Panj Kakar* (Five Ks) incumbent upon all baptized Sikhs, alongside *kesh* (uncut hair), *kara* (steel bangle), *kanga* (wooden comb), and *kachera* (specialized cotton undergarments).
- Encapsulates the profound linguistic and philosophical duality of *kirpa* (grace or compassion) and *aan* (dignity or honor), embodying the solemn vow that the blade may only ever be drawn as an absolute last resort to protect the vulnerable.
- Sheathed and secured within an ornate scabbard suspended from a specialized cotton or fabric harness called a *gatra*, worn diagonally across the torso over or beneath daily attire.
- Grounded doctrinally in the Sikh philosophical concept of *miri* and *piri*, representing the inseparable harmony of temporal political responsibility and spiritual righteousness established by *Guru Hargobind*.
- Formally accommodated under colonial jurisprudence when the *Indian Arms Act* of 1878 was amended to exempt Sikhs from prohibitions on carrying weapons, recognizing the blade as an inviolable religious duty.
- Generates significant legal and constitutional precedents in modern pluralistic societies regarding public carry rights in schools, courthouses, and commercial aircraft under religious freedom protections.
- Rejects usage as an instrument of coercion, intimidation, or conquest, standing strictly in Sikh ethics as a defensive reminder of personal discipline and moral courage.
