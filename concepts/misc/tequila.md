## summary

**Tequila** is a regionally specific, distilled alcoholic spirit produced primarily in the environs of the municipality of Tequila in the western Mexican state of Jalisco. Originating from indigenous Mesoamerican traditions of agave fermentation hybridized with copper-still distillation introduced from [[spain]] during the era of [[colonialism]], it is manufactured strictly from blue agave. Guarded by international Appellation of Origin agreements, this spirit occupies an iconic position in Mexican cultural heritage, international trade disputes, and modern beverage commerce.

## you gotta know

- Botanical exclusivity: Legally designated production requires the exclusive utilization of *Agave tequilana* Weber blue variety (commonly termed blue agave); premium spirits are labeled 100% agave, whereas blended *mixto* varieties must derive a minimum of 51% fermentable sugars from the blue agave plant.
- Appellation of Origin protection: Mexican federal law strictly confines official distillation to the state of Jalisco and specified municipalities in Guanajuato, Michoacán, Nayarit, and Tamaulipas, with compliance verified by the *Consejo Regulador del Tequila* (CRT).
- The Jimador harvest: Agricultural specialists called *jimadores* use an ancient, round-edged cutting instrument known as a *coa de jima* to strip away thorny outer leaves, extracting the dense, sugar-laden vegetative heart known as the *piña*.
- Cooking and extraction: Harvested hearts are slow-baked in traditional brick ovens (*hornos*) or rapid autoclaves to hydrolyze complex inulin fructans into fermentable simple sugars, after which the pulp is crushed using mechanical mills or a massive volcanic stone wheel (*tahona*).
- Regulatory aging tiers: The five official categories recognized by regulatory standards comprise *blanco* (or *plata*, unaged or rested under two months), *reposado* (aged between two and twelve months in oak), *añejo* (aged one to three years in small casks), and *extra añejo* (aged a minimum of three years).
- Early colonial commercialization: Following the depletion of imported European brandies in New Spain, *Don Pedro Sánchez de Tagle*, the Marquis of Altamira, established the first mass-scale commercial agave distillery in Jalisco around 1600.
- The worm fallacy: The cultural belief that this liquor traditionally includes an insect larva (*hypopta agavis*) in the bottle is an erroneous myth; the larva appears exclusively in specific artisanal mezcals from Oaxaca and is strictly prohibited in regulated production of this spirit.

## connections

- [[spain]] — introduced European alembic distillation technologies to the Americas, converting indigenous fermented agave beverages into concentrated hard liquor.
- [[colonialism]] — established the royal monopolies, hacienda systems, and transatlantic trade regulations that governed liquor distillation in New Spain.
- [[age-of-exploration]] — initiated the maritime trade circuits and cross-cultural exchanges that brought European distillation methods to the Americas.
- [[united-states]] — forms the single largest global export market for the spirit, a consumption boom vastly accelerated by twentieth-century cocktail trends.
- [[world-war-ii]] — precipitated a surge in North American consumer demand as wartime transatlantic shipping disruptions cut off supplies of European spirits.
- [[american-civil-war]] — era during which cross-border commerce and rail transportation began establishing international distribution networks for regional Mexican distillates.
