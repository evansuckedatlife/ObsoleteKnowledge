## summary

**maple syrup** is a sweet, viscous condiment made from the concentrated xylem sap of sugar maple, red maple, or black maple trees. Originating among the Indigenous peoples of northeastern North America, the practice was adopted by European settlers and transformed into a major agricultural industry centered in eastern Canada and northern New England. Celebrated for its complex caramel, woody, and vanilla tasting notes, it serves as an iconic cultural symbol of North American forest heritage.

## you gotta know

- Produced primarily from *Acer saccharum* (the sugar maple), whose sap contains the highest natural sucrose concentration among temperate forest trees.
- Originated through processing techniques developed by Indigenous nations—such as the *Haudenosaunee* and *Anishinaabe*—who made incisions in trunk bark and boiled sap using heated stones.
- Requires approximately forty liters of raw sap to yield a single liter of finished syrup through thermal evaporation, concentrating the sugar content to roughly 66 percent.
- Concentrated heavily in *Quebec*, Canada, which yields over seventy percent of total global output and maintains a strategic global syrup reserve managed by the *FPAQ*.
- Standardized under international grading regulations into four unified classes based on light transmission and flavor intensity: *Golden (Delicate)*, *Amber (Rich)*, *Dark (Robust)*, and *Very Dark (Strong)*.
- Harnessed industrial advancements during the nineteenth century, including reverse osmosis filtration, vacuum tubing networks, and evaporators.
- Suffers severe vulnerability to changing regional climate cycles, as sap flow depends on freezing nighttime temperatures alternating with daytime thawing in early spring.
