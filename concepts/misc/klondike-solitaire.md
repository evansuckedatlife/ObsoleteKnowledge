## summary

**Klondike Solitaire**, colloquially known simply as **Klondike** or **Classic Solitaire**, is a patience card game played with a standard fifty-two-card deck that rose to global ubiquity through its association with the late-nineteenth-century gold rush and its subsequent inclusion in personal computer operating systems. Players build seven tableau columns in descending sequence with alternating colors while transferring cards to four foundation piles ascending in suit from ace to king. Renowned for balancing deterministic forward planning with the probabilistic hazard of hidden cards, it remains the standard archetype against which all single-player card games are compared.

## you gotta know

- Bundled digitally with *Microsoft Windows 3.0* in 1990 by intern *Wes Cherry*, explicitly intended by the software publisher to teach personal computer users how to operate a graphical user interface and master the physical mechanics of mouse dragging and dropping.
- Named after the *Klondike Gold Rush* in the *Yukon* territory of northwestern Canada during the late 1890s, where prospectors and saloon-keepers played variations of patience during harsh subarctic winters.
- Setup consists of twenty-eight cards dealt into seven tableau piles from left to right, with each column containing one more card than the previous and only the uppermost card turned face-up.
- Foundation building requires separating cards into four discrete suit piles starting with an *Ace* and continuing through *Two*, *Three*, up to the terminal *King*.
- Tableau construction strictly follows alternating colors in descending rank, meaning a black *Seven* can only receive a red *Six*, while vacant tableau spaces can only be occupied by a *King* or a sequence headed by a *King*.
- Stock cards are typically flipped either one card at a time or in three-card packets known as turn-three draw, with the latter dramatically lowering the win probability and demanding strict tactical management of stock recycling.
- Mathematical analysis reveals that while roughly eighty percent of all possible deals are theoretically solvable when all card positions are fully known, practical win rates for human players facing face-down cards hover between ten and thirty percent due to unavoidable informational asymmetry.
- Frequently confused in nineteenth-century literature with *Canfield*, a distinct casino card game invented by Saratoga Springs gambling impresario *Richard Canfield* where players purchased decks and were paid per card placed on foundations.

## connections

- [[united-states]] — home of the commercial spread of the game through American frontier culture and its revolutionary digital packaging by *Microsoft* in Washington state.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — fostered the mass production of standardized, coated playing-card decks and the leisure time that fueled the late-nineteenth-century craze for patience games.
- [[perception]] — central to player decision-making, where visualizing hidden sequences and tracking unseen cards across stock passes dictates optimal play.
- [[geometry]] — mirrored in the rigid, triangular layout of the seven tableau columns and the strict spatial rules governing grid movement.
- [[modernism]] — reflected in the minimalist, pixelated card-back designs created by graphic designer *Susan Kare* for early computer interfaces.
