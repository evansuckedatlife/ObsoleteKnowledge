## summary

The **Cherokee syllabary** is an indigenous writing system completed around 1821 by the silversmith *Sequoyah* to represent the spoken Cherokee language. Rather than an alphabet where each letter stands for a phoneme, each glyph denotes an entire syllable typically composed of a consonant followed by a vowel. Its rapid adoption sparked widespread literacy, enabled native-language printing, and became an enduring symbol of national sovereignty during the pressures of *[[indian-removal]]* in the *[[united-states]]*.

## you gotta know

- Invented by the polymath *Sequoyah* (*George Guess* or *Gist*), an illiterate silversmith who recognized the communicative power of European "talking leaves" and labored for over a decade to systematically encode spoken syllables.
- Consists of eighty-five distinct characters representing vowel sounds and consonant-vowel pairings, drawing visual inspiration from modified Latin, Greek, and invented glyphs whose phonetic values diverge entirely from European scripts.
- Adopted officially by the *Cherokee Nation* in 1821 after a demonstration before tribal leaders, achieving an astonishingly rapid adult literacy rate that quickly surpassed contemporary English-speaking frontier settlers.
- Used to publish the *Cherokee Phoenix* (*Tsa-la-gi Tsu-le-hi-sa-nu-hi*) beginning in 1828 in New Echota, Georgia, edited by *Elias Boudinot* with lead type cast specifically for the syllabary.
- Provided a unified administrative and literary voice that helped the nation establish a written constitution modeled on the *[[united-states-constitution]]* and defend legal rights in the Supreme Court cases *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* and *Worcester v. Georgia*.
- Maintained active usage through the forced expulsion of the Trail of Tears under *[[indian-removal]]*, continuing in religious, legal, and secular documentation in Indian Territory and North Carolina.
- Adapted in the late twentieth and twenty-first centuries into digital typography, Unicode standards, and early mobile operating system interfaces to support ongoing language revitalization.
