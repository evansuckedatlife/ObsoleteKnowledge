## summary

**Pizza** is a baked culinary dish of Italian origin consisting of a flattened round disk of fermented wheat dough topped with tomatoes, cheese, and various savory garnishes, cooked rapidly in high-temperature ovens. Originating in Naples as an inexpensive and hearty street sustenance for the urban working-class poor, the food became an internationally celebrated icon following waves of transatlantic Italian migration and mid-twentieth-century military encounters. Today it embodies regional artisan traditions ranging from strictly regulated Neapolitan wood-fired specialties to diverse global and American styles.

## you gotta know

- Traditional Neapolitan preparation centers on hand-stretched dough garnished with San Marzano tomatoes, fresh mozzarella cheese (*fior di latte* or *mozzarella di bufala*), fresh basil leaves, and extra virgin olive oil.
- Popular tradition attributes the iconic *Margherita* style to Neapolitan pizzaiolo *Raffaele Esposito*, who allegedly crafted it in 1889 at Pizzeria Brandi to honor *Queen Margherita of Savoy* in the patriotic colors of the Italian flag.
- Inscribed in 2017 onto the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, recognizing the artisanal practice and cultural gestures of the Neapolitan *Pizzaiuolo*.
- Ancient culinary precursors include Mediterranean and Near Eastern flatbreads, such as the Roman army staple *panis focacius* and Greek seasoned breads, though the modern dish required the importation of the New World tomato.
- Transplanted to North America through late nineteenth-century Italian immigration, leading to the 1905 founding of *Lombardi's* in Manhattan, widely recognized as the first licensed pizzeria in the nation.
- Prompted distinct American regional variants, including deep-dish pies developed in Chicago, coal-fired thin-crust *apizza* in New Haven, and square pies baked in automotive drip pans in Detroit.
- Regulated within the European Union under the Traditional Specialities Guaranteed (TSG) designation, which strictly defines dough hydration, fermentation times, hand-stretching without rolling pins, and wood-fired oven heat.
- High-temperature baking at 450 to 500 degrees Celsius produces rapid steam expansion in the crust, leaving an airy, blistered perimeter known technically as the *cornicione*.

## connections

- [[italy]] — the Mediterranean nation where Neapolitan street vendors and bakeries transformed ancient flatbread customs into the modern dish.
- [[rome]] — the historic Italian capital and urban center of ancient Roman flatbread traditions like seasoned *focaccia*.
- [[new-york-city]] — the major immigrant gateway where Little Italy bakeries introduced coal-oven pizza to an American urban public.
- [[united-states]] — the destination where postwar commercial franchise expansion popularized pizza as a ubiquitous mass-market consumer dish.
- [[pierogi]] — a fellow European flour-based staple that underwent parallel immigrant preservation and commercialization in North American communities.
- [[world-war-ii]] — the military campaign during which Allied servicemen stationed across Southern Italy acquired a widespread taste for local bakeries.
- [[roman-empire]] — the classical empire whose culinary reliance on round grain cakes and communal baking ovens established the Mediterranean flatbread foundation.
