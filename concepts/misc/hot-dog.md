## summary

**Hot Dog** is an iconic street food comprising a cooked cured sausage—typically a frankfurter or wiener—cradled in the slit of a specially shaped, soft elongated wheat bun. Introduced to the *United States* by nineteenth-century German immigrants, the preparation became inextricably linked to American popular culture, outdoor leisure, and sporting events. Distinct regional traditions developed across urban centers, establishing specific topping combinations, meat compositions, and cultural rivalries across North America.

## you gotta know

- Traces its lineage to European cured pork and beef sausages developed in *Germany* and Austria, specifically the *Frankfurter Würstchen* and the Viennese *Wiener*.
- Popularized in the *United States* by nineteenth-century German immigrant street vendors, most famously *Charles Feltman*, who began selling hot sausages in grilled buns on *Coney Island* in *New York City* in the 1860s.
- Became an iconic national fixture at baseball stadiums, popularized by concessionaires like *Harry M. Stevens* at the Polo Grounds and Yankee Stadium.
- Immortalized commercially by Polish immigrant *Nathan Handwerker*, who opened *Nathan's Famous* at Coney Island in 1916 and instituted the famous annual *Fourth of July* hot dog eating contest.
- Developed distinct urban culinary variants, including the *Chicago-style* dog (steamed all-beef frank on a poppy seed bun, dragged through the garden with yellow mustard, neon green relish, chopped onions, tomato wedges, pickle spear, sport peppers, and celery salt, but strictly never ketchup).
- Spawned popular regional adaptations like the Detroit *Coney Island* dog smothered in beef chili, mustard, and diced onions, and the bacon-wrapped *Sonoran* hot dog of the American Southwest.
- Formed the subject of the high-profile diplomatic "Hot Dog Summit" in 1939, when President *Franklin D. Roosevelt* served them to King *George VI* of the *United Kingdom* at Hyde Park.
