## summary

The **infinitive** is a fundamental non-finite verb form in [[linguistics]] that expresses the core action or state of a verb without inflection for tense, person, or grammatical number. Across Indo-European grammatical traditions originating in [[ancient-greece]] and the [[roman-empire]], it serves as the conventional citation form or lemma in dictionaries and fulfills versatile syntactic roles as a nominal argument, adverbial adjunct, or verbal complement. In Modern English, it appears either periphrastically with the particle *to* or as a **bare infinitive** lacking any overt marker, remaining famous in prescriptive style guides for debates concerning the **split infinitive**.

## you gotta know

- Traditional English grammar distinguishes between the full or to-marked form and the **bare infinitive**, which appears without the particle *to* following auxiliary modal verbs like *can*, *must*, or *should*, as well as perception verbs like *see* and *hear*.
- Prescriptive grammarians during the eighteenth-century [[enlightenment]] attempted to ban the **split infinitive**—the insertion of an adverb between *to* and the verbal base—by modeling English rules on Latin, where the verb is a single synthetic word and morphologically indivisible.
- Modern descriptive [[linguistics]] recognizes split constructions as fully grammatical, famously popularized in twentieth-century science fiction by the opening motto of *Star Trek*, *to boldly go where no man has gone before*.
- In theoretical generative syntax, an unexpressed subject termed big *PRO* is posited to occupy the subject position of non-finite clauses, distinguishing control predicates like *persuade* from raising predicates like *seem*.
- Historical Old English formed non-finite citation verbs synthetically with the suffix *-an*, which eroded over centuries of contact into Middle English before adopting the directional preposition *to* as a functional head.
- Classical Latin and Greek inflected verbs through distinct synthetic morphological endings rather than analytical particles, distinguishing present, perfect, and future aspects in both active and passive voices.
- While European languages routinely employ nominalized non-finite complements, languages with different typological profiles, such as [[inuktitut]], rely on polysynthetic affixation or nominalizers rather than distinct non-finite lemmas.

## connections

- [[linguistics]] — the academic discipline analyzing non-finite verb categories, control structures, and complement clauses.
- [[infix]] — a contrasting morphological process that inserts affixes directly into roots, whereas Germanic non-finite markers operate externally or periphrastically.
- [[inuktitut]] — an Arctic polysynthetic tongue that conveys complex predication without traditional Western non-finite citation forms.
- [[roman-empire]] — civilization whose Latin grammar heavily shaped early prescriptive models of Western verbal morphology.
- [[ancient-greece]] — the birthplace of systematic Dionysian grammar, defining the *aparemphatos* mood lacking person marking.
- [[enlightenment]] — the intellectual era in which British grammarians attempted to codify rigid structural rules for vernacular syntax.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — the English playwright whose early modern plays frequently featured split verbal phrases and flexible syntax.
