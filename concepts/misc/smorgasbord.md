## summary

The **smorgasbord** (Swedish: **smörgåsbord**) is a traditional Scandinavian buffet-style feast featuring a lavish array of hot and cold dishes arranged together on a communal table for diners to serve themselves. Originating from eighteenth-century Swedish aristocratic gathering traditions, it spread internationally in the twentieth century and was assimilated into the English language as both an elaborate dining format and a figurative metaphor for any wide, diverse selection. In culinary history and academic quiz tournaments, it is celebrated for its rigid course-by-course consumption etiquette, its signature seafood spreads including [[smoked-salmon]], and its grand American introduction at the 1939 New York World's Fair.

## you gotta know

- Derived etymologically from the Swedish compound *smörgås* (literally meaning *"butter-goose,"* an archaic reference to lumps of butter floating on churned milk, later meaning an open sandwich) and *bord* (meaning table).
- Governed by an exacting traditional etiquette where guests visit the buffet multiple times using a clean plate for each course, beginning with pickled herring, progressing to cold fish like [[smoked-salmon]], then cold meats, warm dishes, and dessert.
- Evolved from the fourteenth-century to eighteenth-century Swedish *brännvinsbord*, an appetizer table loaded with bread, butter, cheese, preserved fish, and caraway-spiced aquavit served to guests before seated formal dinners.
- Introduced to mass public acclaim in the [[united-states]] during the 1939 New York World's Fair in [[new-york-city]], where the Swedish Pavilion operated a celebrated rotating restaurant known as the *Three Crowns*.
- Analyzed in [[linguistics]] as an emblematic loanword that entered mid-twentieth-century colloquial English, routinely functioning as an idiom signifying an eclectic or overwhelming assortment of ideas, objects, or opportunities.
- Celebrated during the winter holiday season as the *julbord*, an expanded Christmas iteration featuring regional specialties including spiced Christmas ham (*julskinka*), small meatballs, and liver pâté.
- Contrasted with traditional French table service (*service à la française*) and Russian service (*service à la russe*), providing a self-service dining framework that influenced modern banquet and hotel catering.
- Accompanied customarily by the communal singing of traditional drinking verses known as *snapsvisor*, recited immediately prior to downing small measures of chilled aquavit.

## connections

- [[smoked-salmon]] — classic cold seafood centerpiece served alongside gravlax and herring during the essential fish course of the banquet.
- [[new-york-city]] — metropolitan host of the 1939 World's Fair, where the rotating *Three Crowns* restaurant popularized the buffet format across North America.
- [[linguistics]] — academic field examining the borrowing of the Swedish compound into English as a popular metaphor for abundance and variety.
- [[united-states]] — nation where the culinary concept was widely embraced, inspiring mid-century American smorgasbord and buffet restaurant chains.
- [[world-war-ii]] — conflict erupting immediately after the 1939 World's Fair that reshaped European cultural diplomacy and international tourism.
- [[france]] — European gastronomic power whose rigid seated court service conventions contrasted sharply with the democratic self-service of Nordic buffets.
- [[five-senses]] — physiological faculties stimulated by the aromatic spices, vinegary marinades, smoky fish oils, and ornate visual displays of the communal spread.
