## summary

The **African diaspora** refers to the dispersal of African peoples across the globe, most significantly through the forced migration of enslaved Africans to the Americas between the 16th and 19th centuries, and subsequently through voluntary migration and colonization. This vast movement of peoples created enduring communities in the Caribbean, North and South America, and beyond, generating new cultural forms, identities, and traditions that blend African heritage with the experiences and influences of their adopted homelands. The African diaspora remains foundational to understanding Atlantic world history, cultural syncretism, and contemporary identity politics.

## you gotta know

- The transatlantic slave trade (16th–19th centuries) forcibly displaced approximately 12 million Africans to the Americas, with the overwhelming majority laboured to death in Caribbean and Brazilian sugar plantations.
- Enslaved Africans maintained cultural traditions—music, language, spiritual practices, and storytelling—that merged with Indigenous and European influences to create distinct diaspora cultures across different regions.
- The Caribbean became the primary destination for enslaved Africans, resulting in majority-Black societies where African cultural retentions are most pronounced in music, dance, religion, and cuisine.
- Diaspora communities developed new languages—Creoles, Gullah, Jamaican Patois—that blended African grammar and vocabulary with European languages, enabling communication across linguistic groups.
- Post-abolition migration, particularly from the Caribbean and Africa to North America, Britain, and Europe during the 19th–20th centuries, expanded the diaspora into metropolitan centers and created new forms of diaspora identity.
- Cultural practices including Carnival, Junkanoo, Voodoo, and blues music emerged from diaspora communities, synthesizing African rhythms and spiritual worldviews with New World environments and materials.
- The diaspora concept itself became central to African-American and Black British identity politics, scholarship, and social movements seeking to reclaim and celebrate African heritage despite displacement and oppression.
