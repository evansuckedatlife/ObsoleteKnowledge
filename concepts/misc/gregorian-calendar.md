## summary

The **Gregorian calendar** is the solar calendar adopted by the Catholic Church in 1582 and gradually adopted worldwide as the standard civil calendar. Designed by Pope Gregory XIII to correct accumulated drift in the *Julian calendar*, it rules most international timekeeping today and anchors the Western new year on January 1st.

## you gotta know

- Reformed the Julian calendar, which had drifted ten days out of alignment with the solar year by the 16th century due to an overestimated leap-year rule.
- The fix: omit leap years in century years (1700, 1800, 1900) unless divisible by 400—a more accurate correction than the Julian system's simple every-four-years rule.
- Adopted first by Catholic nations (Spain, Portugal, Italy) and slowly by Protestant and Orthodox regions; Britain and its colonies (including America) adopted it in 1752.
- Imposes a fixed solar year of approximately 365.24219 days, enabling precise long-term calendars without the month-drift inherent in purely lunar systems.
- The extra days shift Gregorian new year rituals (like the Times Square ball drop) to a uniform civil date rather than following lunar cycles.
- Coexists with religious and cultural calendars (Islamic, Hebrew, Chinese, Hindu) but dominates international commerce, law, and science.
- The leap-year exception (every 400 years, not 100) means the Gregorian year drifts only one day per ~3,300 years—an extraordinarily accurate long-term system.
- Adoption was politically fraught: Orthodox and some Protestant churches resisted what they saw as papal authority, delaying synchronization into the 20th century for some regions.
- The ten-day jump in October 1582 (October 4 followed by October 15) sparked urban legends of the "lost ten days" and briefly confused merchants and clergy worldwide.
- France, Spain, and Portugal adopted it immediately; Russia and Greece held out until the 20th century; some Orthodox communities use the Julian calendar still for religious purposes.
