## summary

**goat cheese**, internationally recognized by its French moniker *chèvre*, refers to any dairy cheese produced through the curdling and fermentation of caprine milk. Known for its distinct tangy acidity, earthy aroma, and chalky or creamy texture, it constitutes one of the oldest manufactured foodstuffs in agrarian history. The culinary staple holds protected regional status across southern Europe and is celebrated globally across both traditional pastoral cuisines and modern fine dining.

## you gotta know

- Derives its characteristic pungent and earthy flavor profile from elevated concentrations of medium-chain fatty acids, specifically *caproic*, *caprylic*, and *capric acid*, named after the Latin term for goat.
- Possesses naturally lower levels of lactose and smaller fat globule diameter compared to bovine milk, rendering it generally easier to digest in the human gastrointestinal tract.
- Features prominent historical roots in the *Loire Valley* of [[france]], where Moorish agricultural traditions introduced dairy goats following the eighth-century *Battle of Tours*.
- Produces celebrated protected appellation varieties including the cylindrical, ash-coated *Sainte-Maure de Touraine*, the truncated pyramid *Valençay*, and the button-shaped *Crottin de Chavignol*.
- Traditionally dusted with vegetable ash or powdered charcoal to protect delicate young curds from insect pests and moderate rind surface acidity during mold maturation.
- Enjoys global culinary variations such as Greek *feta* (traditionally blended with sheep milk), Spanish *Garrotxa*, and Norwegian caramelized *Brunost* or *Gjetost*.
- Forms a crumbly, porcelain-white paste due to caprine metabolism converting carotene directly into vitamin A, unlike bovine milk which retains yellow carotenoid pigments in butterfat.
