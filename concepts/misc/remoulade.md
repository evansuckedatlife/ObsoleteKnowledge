## summary

**Remoulade** (frequently accented as **rémoulade**) is a classic cold emulsion condiment originating in eighteenth-century French cuisine as a seasoned derivative of mayonnaise. Traditionally flavored with mustard, vinegar, capers, chopped gherkins, and aromatic fresh herbs, it was designed to cut through rich fried proteins and enliven cold roasted meats and vegetables. The condiment gained international prominence after crossing the Atlantic, where it was transformed into a fiery, reddish centerpiece of Louisiana Creole and Cajun gastronomy.

## you gotta know

- Originated in [[france]] during the mid-eighteenth century as a sharp cold emulsion intended to enhance cold poultry, boiled seafood, and sliced meats.
- Formulated in classical French cuisine by blending mayonnaise with pungent Dijon mustard, minced cornichons, capers, and herbs such as tarragon, chervil, and parsley.
- Serves as the indispensable dressing in *céleri rémoulade*, a ubiquitous French bistro salad consisting of finely julienned raw celeriac root steeped in the sauce.
- Transformed significantly in the American South, where Louisiana Creole cooks introduced Creole brown mustard, paprika, cayenne pepper, horseradish, and minced scallions.
- Acts as the definitive condiment for *shrimp rémoulade*, a celebrated cold appetizer in historic New Orleans dining institutions such as *Galatoire's* and *Arnaud's*.
- Adopted as a staple national fast-food accompaniment in Denmark and Scandinavian countries, where a mild, curry-tinted variant tops hot dogs and smørrebrød.
- Functions as an ideal balancing sauce for deeply seared, fatty beef cuts, frequently paired alongside chilled slices of grilled [[ribeye]] or roast beef sandwiches.

## connections

- [[france]] — developed the canonical culinary techniques of emulsified cold dressings that serve as the technical foundation for the sauce.
- [[five-senses]] — activates taste buds and olfactory pathways through the complex union of creamy fats, sharp vinegar acids, and spicy aromatics.
- [[ribeye]] — complements the rich, heavy fat profile of seared beef cuts through its sharp acidity and herbal brightness.
- [[colonialism]] — drove the maritime transfer and cultural adaptation of classical French saucing traditions into the Gulf Coast of North America.
- [[united-states]] — provided the regional melting pot wherein Louisiana Creole and Cajun traditions infused the condiment with native pepper spices.
- [[perception]] — influences the culinary sensation of balance by using pungent acidity and brine to counteract fatty sensory satiety.
