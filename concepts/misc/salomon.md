## summary

**Haym Salomon** was a Polish-born Jewish merchant, broker, and patriot who played a crucial role in financing the American side during the Revolutionary War. Operating out of Philadelphia, he converted foreign bills of exchange into liquidity, raised critical funds for the Continental Army, and personally underwrote military operations and government officials. His financial acumen and selfless support for the fledgling American republic have made him an enduring figure in early American history and patriotic lore.

## you gotta know

- Immigrated to New York City from Poland in 1775 after travelling through Europe, quickly establishing a prosperous business as a financial broker and commission merchant.
- Joined the pro-independence *Sons of Liberty* in New York and was arrested by the British as a spy in 1776 following the Great Fire of New York.
- Paroled by the British due to his linguistic fluency in German, French, and Polish to serve as an interpreter for Hessian mercenary forces, during which he covertly facilitated Hessian desertions and aided American prisoners of war.
- Sentenced to death for espionage in 1778 after British authorities uncovered his continued undercover activities, but successfully orchestrated a daring escape to Philadelphia.
- Appointed as the official broker to the Office of Finance under Superintendent of Finance *Robert Morris*, executing transactions of French, Dutch, and Spanish government debt with virtually zero personal profit.
- Famously raised over twenty thousand dollars on short notice after General *George Washington* dispatched an urgent request to Morris declaring "Send for Haym Salomon" to fund the pivotal 1781 march to Yorktown.
- Provided generous, interest-free personal loans to prominent revolutionaries experiencing severe financial distress, including *James Madison*, *James Monroe*, and *Thomas Jefferson*.
- Contributed heavily to civic and religious life by serving as a primary benefactor for the construction of Congregation Mikveh Israel, Philadelphia's first formal synagogue building.
