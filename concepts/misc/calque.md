## summary

In [[linguistics]], a **calque**, commonly known in English as a **loan translation**, is a word, phrase, or semantic structure borrowed from one language into another through literal, component-by-component or word-for-word translation. Rather than directly absorbing the foreign phonetic sounds as a loanword, the borrowing language adopts the conceptual blueprint and renders it using native lexical material. Calquing represents one of the most vital mechanisms of cross-cultural communication, allowing languages to expand their philosophical, technical, and colloquial vocabularies while preserving native morphology.

## you gotta know

- The technical term itself is a loanword borrowed directly into English from the French noun *calque*, meaning a tracing, copy, or carbon-paper transfer, derived from the verb *calquer* (to trace).
- Fundamentally contrasted with direct phonetic loanwords; for instance, English borrowed the German word *kindergarten* directly as a phonetic loanword, but translated the French idiom *marché aux puces* word-for-word as *flea market*.
- The English noun *skyscraper* serves as one of history's most famously prolific export calques, translated root-for-root into French as *gratte-ciel*, German as *Wolkenkratzer*, Spanish as *rascacielos*, and Russian as *neboskreb*.
- Modern English philosophical and psychological terminology relies heavily on loan translations from German, including *worldview* (from *Weltanschauung*) and Friedrich Nietzsche's *Übermensch*, which was translated by playwright George Bernard Shaw as *superman*.
- During classical antiquity, Roman scholars systematically enriched Latin by calquing abstract intellectual terminology from [[ancient-greece]]; for instance, Cicero coined the Latin word *qualitas* to calque the Greek term *poiotes*.
- A **semantic calque** or semantic loan occurs when an existing native word expands its definition to acquire a new, additional meaning modeled after a foreign counterpart, such as early computer terminology adapting *mouse*.
- Phraseological calques reproduce entire idiomatic expressions without altering their metaphorical syntax, exemplified by the English phrase *it goes without saying* rendered directly from the French expression *ça va sans dire*.
- Religious and scriptural history was profoundly shaped by calques; the Old English term *gōdspel* (modern *gospel*) was constructed as a compound translation of the Greek term *euangelion* meaning good tidings.

## connections

- [[linguistics]] — primary academic discipline that investigates language contact, lexical borrowing, etymology, and morphological adaptation across speech communities.
- [[france]] — source culture that provided the etymological name *calque* itself, as well as ubiquitous idioms like *point of view* from *point de vue*.
- [[germany]] — intellectual cradle whose complex compound philosophical vocabulary gave English foundational loan translations like *zeitgeist* and *worldview*.
- [[ancient-greece]] — foundational Mediterranean civilization whose philosophical, scientific, and grammatical terminology was systematically calqued into Latin by Roman thinkers.
- [[roman-empire]] — historic imperial society whose bilingual intellectual elite employed loan translations to establish Latin as the universal tongue of European scholarship.
- [[england]] — linguistic territory where centuries of intense Norman-French and Anglo-Saxon contact generated vast layers of bilingual loan translations.
