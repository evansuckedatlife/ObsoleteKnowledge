## summary

**Middle English** refers to the historical stage of the English language spoken from the aftermath of the Norman Conquest in 1066 until the late fifteenth century. Arising from the sustained contact between Anglo-Saxon dialects of *[[england]]* and the Norman French vernacular of *[[france]]*, it witnessed rapid structural transformation, shedding the elaborate grammatical gender and nominal inflections of Old English. It is the literary vehicle of *Geoffrey Chaucer*, *William Langland*, and the *Gawain Poet*, forming an indispensable evolutionary bridge toward Early Modern English.

## you gotta know

- Precipitated by the Norman Conquest of 1066, which relegated the vernacular tongue to peasant discourse while Anglo-Norman and Latin became the languages of courtly administration, law, and literature across *[[england]]*.
- Famously immortalized in *Geoffrey Chaucer's* fourteenth-century masterpiece *The Canterbury Tales*, whose General Prologue begins with the celebrated lines celebrating the sweet showers of April.
- Characterized structurally by significant morphological leveling, wherein unstressed vowels in grammatical inflectional endings merged into a schwa and eventually dropped away entirely.
- Transitioned the language from a synthetic, case-marked grammatical system into an analytic syntax where word order and the careful placement of each **[[modifier]]** became paramount for establishing semantic relations.
- Experienced a monumental lexical expansion through the borrowing of thousands of Romance loanwords relating to governance, fashion, religion, warfare, and gastronomy from the ruling aristocracy of *[[france]]*.
- Encompassed the flourishing of the fourteenth-century Alliterative Revival in regional dialects, yielding Arthurian poetic triumphs like *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight* and moral allegories like *Piers Plowman*.
- Began undergoing the radical long-vowel shift known as the *Great Vowel Shift* toward the close of the fifteenth century, paving the way for the language of *[[william-shakespeare]]*.
- Formally concluded around the arrival of William Caxton's printing press in 1476 and the adoption of the Chancery Standard in London during the early Northern *[[renaissance]]*.

## connections

- [[england]] — the island kingdom where the language developed and flourished through periods of foreign invasion and political consolidation.
- [[linguistics]] — the academic discipline analyzing its phonetic changes, grammatical reduction, and dialectal variation.
- [[france]] — the cultural source of the Anglo-Norman dialect that transformed English vocabulary and scribal conventions.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — the dramatist whose Early Modern English directly inherited the syntactic flexibility developed during this era.
- [[modifier]] — the syntactic grammatical element whose structural placement grew vital as case endings disappeared.
- [[renaissance]] — the intellectual movement whose printing technology and orthographic standardization ultimately ended this linguistic period.
- [[christianity]] — the dominant religion whose monasteries, Latin Vulgate translations by John Wycliffe, and cycle plays sustained vernacular prose.
