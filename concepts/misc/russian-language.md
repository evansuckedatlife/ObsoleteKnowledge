## summary

The **Russian language** is a major East Slavic tongue belonging to the Indo-European language family, functioning as the national language of Russia and the enduring historical lingua franca across the broader post-Soviet sphere. Written in the Cyrillic script and recognized globally as one of the six official working languages of the United Nations, it possesses a rich inflectional morphology characterized by six grammatical cases, mobile pitch-accent evolution, and widespread consonant palatalization. In academic quiz bowl tournaments and linguistic competitions, it frequently appears in tossups and bonuses regarding Slavic philology, 19th-century psychological literature, revolutionary orthographic reforms, and spaceflight communication protocols.

## you gotta know

- Written using a thirty-three-letter variant of the Cyrillic alphabet, which was adapted from medieval Slavic scripts rooted in the Greek uncial script devised by the disciples of saints *Cyril and Methodius*.
- Features a highly synthetic grammatical structure containing six primary cases—nominative, accusative, genitive, dative, instrumental, and prepositional—governing nouns, adjectives, numerals, and pronouns.
- Distinguishes phonemically between "hard" unpalatalized consonants and "soft" palatalized consonants, an essential phonetic contrast denoted by alternating vowel letters and the dedicated soft sign (*myagkiy znak*).
- Characterized by unpredictable, mobile lexical stress that dramatically alters vowel quality through phonetic reduction, notably causing unaccented *o* to be pronounced like *a*, a phenomenon known as *akan'ye*.
- Possesses a complex verbal system centered on grammatical aspect, pairing imperfective and perfective verbs while maintaining specialized prefixes for verbs of motion denoting unidirectional versus multidirectional transit.
- Standardized during the 18th and 19th centuries through the linguistic reforms of polymath *Mikhail Lomonosov* and the celebrated literary masterpieces of poet *Alexander Pushkin*, whom Russians venerate as the father of modern literary Russian.
- Underwent a major Bolshevik orthographic reform in 1918 that simplified spelling by abolishing archaic letters such as *yat*, *fita*, and *decimal i*, while eliminating the word-final silent hard sign (*yer*).
- Serves as the required co-operational language alongside English aboard the International Space Station, where all international astronauts and cosmonauts must achieve functional verbal and technical fluency.
