## summary

The **Oxford comma**, also known as the *serial comma* or *Harvard comma*, is the punctuation mark placed immediately before the coordinating conjunction in a series of three or more items. Named after its long-standing prescription by *Oxford University Press* in [[england]], its usage represents one of the most fiercely contested debates in English typography and [[linguistics]]. While championed by traditional book publishers and academic manuals to resolve grammatical ambiguity, journalistic style guides frequently discard it to conserve space.

## you gotta know

- Placed directly before coordinating conjunctions like "and" or "or" in enumerations, as in "apples, oranges, and bananas."
- Earned its primary name because *Horace Hart*, printer to the University of Oxford from 1883 to 1915, made it an absolute standard in his influential *Hart's Rules for Compositors and Readers*.
- Strongly mandated across major American publishing authorities in [[american-literature]], including *The Chicago Manual of Style*, the *MLA Handbook*, and the *APA Publication Manual*.
- Typically rejected by newspaper conventions across the [[united-kingdom]] and the [[united-states]], most visibly by *The Associated Press Stylebook* and *The Times*, unless strictly required to prevent misreading.
- Celebrated in legal history: in 2017, the lack of an Oxford comma in a Maine overtime statute concerning dairy drivers led to a 5-million-dollar court settlement (*O'Connor v. Oakhurst Dairy*).
- Famous humorous illustration of ambiguity without it: "We invited the strippers, JFK and Stalin," which falsely implies John F. Kennedy and Joseph Stalin were strippers rather than separate guests.
- Popularized in indie rock culture by the American band *Vampire Weekend* in their self-titled 2008 single "Oxford Comma."

## connections

- [[linguistics]] — grammatical and syntactic field that studies serial ambiguity, coordination, and punctuation mechanics.
- [[england]] — historic home of Oxford University Press and Horace Hart, who codified the punctuation convention.
- [[united-kingdom]] — linguistic ecosystem where national broadsheets and publishing presses developed contrasting comma rules.
- [[united-states]] — jurisdiction where academic publishers embraced the serial comma and legal battles tested its absence.
- [[american-literature]] — written tradition governed by style manuals such as Chicago and MLA that enforce the serial comma.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — early modern English playwright whose original folios and early printings utilized variable, rhetorical punctuation.
