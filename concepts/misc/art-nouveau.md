## summary

**Art Nouveau** was an international design movement (1890–1910) that revolutionized decorative arts, architecture, and graphic design by drawing inspiration from natural forms and rejecting industrial mass production in favor of organic, flowing lines and handcrafted elegance. Emerging during the Belle Époque, Art Nouveau celebrated sinuous plant forms, whiplash curves, and integrated ornament that reflected both aestheticist principles and anxieties about modernity. Though short-lived as a cohesive movement, Art Nouveau bridged the 19th-century Arts and Crafts tradition with 20th-century modernism, leaving an indelible mark on furniture, jewelry, posters, and public spaces across Europe and America.

## you gotta know

- Flourished primarily between 1890 and 1910, with its peak popularity around 1900; named retrospectively, it was called "Jugendstil" in German-speaking countries and "Modern Style" in some English contexts.
- Rejected industrial mass production and machine aesthetics, embracing instead the handcrafted object as a rebellion against Victorian excess and mechanization—a position that paradoxically made Art Nouveau expensive and inaccessible to working classes.
- Drew aesthetic language from nature—flowing plant stems, insect wings, organic curves—rather than geometric or historical ornament, creating a visually distinctive vocabulary of sinuous lines and naturalistic motifs across all media.
- Exemplified by poster artist *Alphonse Mucha*, who created iconic theatrical advertising featuring androgynous figures with flowing hair and costumes integrated with floral frameworks that became the movement's visual signature.
- Produced landmark architectural and design works: Hector Guimard's Paris Metro station entrances, *Louis Majorelle*'s furniture, Émile Gallé's glass vessels, and jeweler *René Lalique*'s pieces that demonstrated Art Nouveau's reach across disciplines.
- Particularly prevalent in Paris, Brussels, Vienna, and Barcelona, where local adaptations created regional variations in how organic forms were interpreted and applied.
- Declined after World War I, when modernism's geometric abstraction and industrial rationalism supplanted Art Nouveau's craft-based organicism; Post-Impressionist painting and Cubism further shifted aesthetic priorities away from natural representation.
