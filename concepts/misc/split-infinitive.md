## summary

**Split Infinitive** is an English grammatical construction in which an adverb or adverbial phrase is placed between the particle *to* and the base verbal stem of a full infinitive. Although widely utilized in natural spoken and written English since the Middle English period, the construction became the subject of intense prescriptive grammatical condemnation during the nineteenth century. Today, modern descriptive linguists and major style manuals overwhelmingly accept the form, recognizing it as a natural, expressive, and frequently necessary feature of English syntax.

## you gotta know

- Occurs when an intervening modifier separates the infinitive marker *to* from its verb root, famously exemplified by the opening narration of the television series *Star Trek*: "to boldly go where no man has gone before."
- The historical prohibition was largely fabricated by nineteenth-century prescriptive grammarians, notably *Henry Alford*, Dean of Canterbury, who attacked the construction in his 1864 treatise *A Plea for the Queen's English*.
- Prescriptivists often rationalized the ban through a false analogy to classical Latin grammar, arguing that because Latin infinitives are a single synthetic word that cannot physically be split, the English analytic infinitive should behave identically.
- English literary figures from *Geoffrey Chaucer* and *[[william-shakespeare]]* to *John Milton*, *[[percy-bysshe-shelley]]*, and *George Eliot* used the construction naturally across centuries of written work without controversy.
- Renowned lexicographer *Henry Watson Fowler* famously classified English users into five categories regarding this usage in his 1926 *Modern English Usage*, advocating for splitting when natural rhythm or clarity demanded it.
- Avoiding the construction mechanically often creates severe syntactic ambiguity or clumsy hypercorrections, particularly with adverbs like *really*, *clearly*, or *always*.
- Major contemporary publishing style guides—including *The Chicago Manual of Style*, *The Associated Press Stylebook*, and *The Oxford English Grammar*—explicitly permit the construction whenever it avoids awkward phrasing or confusion.
