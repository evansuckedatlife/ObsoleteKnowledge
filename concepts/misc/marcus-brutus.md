## summary

**Marcus Junius Brutus** (85–42 BCE) was a Roman senator and general whose participation in the assassination of *Julius Caesar* made him one of history's most infamous figures, symbolizing the ultimate betrayal of trust and friendship. Despite his illustrious family lineage and Caesar's personal trust and affection, Brutus joined the conspiracy believing the assassination would preserve the Republic from tyranny. His tragic fall—from honored senator to hunted fugitive—and his role in Shakespeare's dramatization cemented him as the archetypal traitor, though ancient sources reveal a more conflicted figure torn between personal loyalty and republican principle.

## you gotta know

- Born into the highest ranks of Roman nobility; his mother *Servilia* was Caesar's mistress, and his father *Marcus Junius Brutus the Elder* had been a prominent senator—positioning him as one of Rome's elite and a natural confidant of Caesar.
- Despite their blood connection and Caesar's patronage, Brutus initially opposed Caesar's rise to dominance; however, Caesar's clemency toward Brutus (when he fought for Pompey) earned the younger man's gratitude and respect.
- Caesar appointed Brutus to important offices, including the governorship of Cisalpine Gaul, and was preparing him for further advancement—making Brutus's conspiracy particularly shocking to contemporaries.
- Persuaded by republicans like *Gaius Cassius* to join the conspiracy, Brutus came to view the assassination as a republican duty—necessary to save the Republic from Caesar's apparent ambitions toward kingship (explicitly forbidden in Roman political culture).
- On the Ides of March (March 15), 44 BCE, Brutus participated in Caesar's murder; according to Plutarch, Caesar's recognition of Brutus among the assassins ("Et tu, Brute?"—"And you, Brutus?") expressed shock at the betrayal by his beloved associate.
- After the assassination, Brutus initially was received favorably; however, *Mark Antony*'s powerful oration at Caesar's funeral shifted public opinion against the conspirators, forcing Brutus and his allies into exile.
- Pursued by forces loyal to Caesar and later by Octavian and Antony, Brutus retreated to the East; defeated at the *Battle of Philippi* (42 BCE), he died by suicide rather than face capture.
- Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar* immortalized Brutus as the tragic traitor, despite ancient sources painting a more nuanced portrait of a man genuinely conflicted between personal bonds and abstract principles.
