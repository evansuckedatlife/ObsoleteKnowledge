## summary

**Cumin** is an aromatic culinary spice made from the dried seed of the herbaceous plant *Cuminum cyminum*, a flowering member of the parsley family Apiaceae. Native to the eastern [[middle-east]] and the Mediterranean Basin, it has formed a cornerstone of international cuisines from the Indian subcontinent to Latin America for thousands of years. It is valued across global gastronomy and quiz-bowl food history for its distinctive warm, earthy, and pungent aroma derived primarily from cuminaldehyde.

## you gotta know

- Obtained from the dried fruit of *Cuminum cyminum*, an annual plant belonging to the family Apiaceae alongside fennel, coriander, dill, and caraway.
- Characterized by a warm, bitter, and distinctly earthy profile whose essential oils are dominated by *cuminaldehyde*, *cymene*, and terpenoids.
- Essential component of staple culinary spice blends worldwide, including Indian *garam masala*, Mexican *taco seasoning*, North African *ras el hanout*, and Middle Eastern *baharat*.
- Extensively utilized in ancient medicine and preservation, appearing in archaeological excavations at *Tell ed-Der* in ancient Mesopotamia as well as throughout ancient Egyptian New Kingdom tombs.
- Mentioned explicitly in both the Old and New Testaments of the Bible, famously cited in the Gospel of Matthew regarding the meticulous tithing of garden herbs.
- Heavily traded throughout the [[roman-empire]] and [[ancient-greece]] as a ubiquitous pepper substitute, kept on dining tables in powdered form.
- Disseminated into the Americas by Spanish and Portuguese colonists during the [[age-of-exploration]], embedding itself permanently into Tex-Mex and Latin American meat preparation and [[curing]].

## connections

- [[middle-east]] — indigenous cradle where wild and cultivated varieties originated and spread across ancient trade arteries.
- [[ancient-greece]] — Mediterranean culture where it was kept in table shakers and used as an essential seasoning.
- [[roman-empire]] — classical civilization that consumed it in savory sauces and valued its medicinal digestive properties.
- [[age-of-exploration]] — historical period that introduced the spice to the Americas, transforming indigenous cookery.
- [[spain]] — European nation that integrated the spice from Moorish rule and carried it across the Atlantic.
- [[curing]] — preservation method where ground spices are applied alongside salt and nitrates to flavor and stabilize preserved meats.
