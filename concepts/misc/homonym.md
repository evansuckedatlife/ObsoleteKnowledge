## summary

A **homonym** is a word that shares the same spelling and the same pronunciation as another word, while maintaining an entirely distinct meaning and etymological heritage. Deriving from the Greek *homonymos*, meaning *having the same name*, the term is frequently employed in a strict technical sense for words identical in both sound and sight, such as *bark* of a tree versus *bark* of a dog. In popular vernacular, it is often utilized more broadly as an umbrella designation encompassing both acoustic homophones and visual homographs.

## you gotta know

- Derives from the Greek *homos* meaning *same* and *onyma* meaning *name*, signifying distinct entities denoted by an identical label.
- Defined strictly in structural linguistics as true homonyms only when words are simultaneously homophones and homographs with separate etymologies.
- Distinguished rigorously from *polysemy*, where a single word possesses multiple related semantic extensions derived from a common historical root.
- Exemplified in English by pairs such as *stalk* (plant stem) and *stalk* (to pursue stealthily), which arose along unconnected etymological lines.
- Provokes lexical ambiguity in formal semantic parsing, requiring contextual semantic analysis to disambiguate intended definitions.
- Explored by philosophical thinkers in *Ancient Greece*, including *Aristotle*, who opened the *Categories* by defining homonymous entities that share a name but differ in essence.
- Deployed across rhetorical satire and dramatic comedy to create misdirection, dramatic irony, and verbal dexterity in dramatic scripts.
- Classified systematically in taxonomy and biological nomenclature as *junior homonyms*, where two species accidentally receive identical scientific Latin binomials.
