## summary

A **zoom**, or **zoom shot**, is a cinematographic and photographic technique that dynamically changes the focal length of a lens to alter the magnification and field of view without physically shifting the camera. Developed mechanically in the mid-twentieth century through an **optical zoom** lens mechanism, the technique revolutionized film grammar by enabling rapid reframing, dramatic psychological emphasis, and surreal spatial transformations. It remains an essential concept in film aesthetics, visual perception, and quiz-bowl questions on experimental avant-garde cinema and classic cinematography.

## you gotta know

- Operates by mechanically adjusting internal optical elements within a variable-focal-length lens, altering the angle of view while keeping the camera apparatus stationary.
- Differs from a tracking shot or dolly movement, which physically transports the camera through space and changes spatial parallax rather than merely flattening perspective.
- Forms the core of the vertigo effect—also called a dolly zoom, zolly, or trombone shot—which simultaneously combines a forward dolly movement with a reverse lens zoom to dramatically distort background scale.
- Pioneered dramatically in narrative film by director *Alfred Hitchcock* and second-unit cameraman *Irmin Roberts*, who employed the dolly zoom in *Vertigo* to visually externalize intense acrophobia.
- Forms the entire conceptual framework of Canadian artist *Michael Snow*'s landmark 1967 structuralist film *Wavelength*, which consists of an apparent continuous forty-five-minute zoom across a Manhattan loft room toward a photograph of breaking waves.
- Commercialized for television broadcasting and sports by American optical engineer *Frank G. Back*, who patented the groundbreaking *Zoomar* lens in 1946.
- Used extensively for visceral punch by directors like *Sergio Leone* in spaghetti western standoffs and *Quentin Tarantino* in homage to vintage Hong Kong martial arts cinema via the rapid crash zoom.
- Contrasts with digital zooming methods, which merely crop and interpolate pixel grids electronically at the expense of optical resolution and true geometric fidelity.
