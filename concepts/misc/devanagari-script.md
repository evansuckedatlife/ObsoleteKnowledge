## summary

**Devanagari**, also historically termed **Nāgarī**, is an ancient abugida writing system native to the Indian subcontinent that evolved from the monumental *Brāhmī* script around the seventh century CE. Characterized visually by a distinctive horizontal continuous overbar connecting the tops of letterforms, it serves as the official script for modern standard *Hindi* and *Marathi*, and functions as the primary orthography for classical *Sanskrit*. As one of the most widely used writing systems on Earth, it embodies sophisticated phonological principles formulated by ancient grammarians that group consonants and vowels strictly by physiological place and manner of articulation.

## you gotta know

- Features a distinctive continuous horizontal hanging headstroke called the *shirorekha*, from which individual letter glyphs suspend horizontally from left to right.
- Operates as a true abugida, meaning each base consonant grapheme inherently carries the neutral inherent vowel sound *short a* unless modified by diacritical vowel marks or silenced by a stroke called a *halanta* or *virama*.
- Transcribes classical *Sanskrit*, modern *Hindi*, *Marathi*, *Nepali*, *Bhojpuri*, *Maithili*, and *Konkani*, while historically influencing neighboring scripts throughout Central and Southeast Asia.
- Reflects the scientific phonetic arrangement codified by the ancient linguist *Pāṇini*, ordering vowels first followed by consonants arranged systematically from velar to labial points of articulation.
- Employs complex conjunct consonants, termed *samyuktaksara*, which merge adjacent consonants into single graphic ligatures to depict consonant clusters without intervening vowels.
- Descended through intermediate *Gupta* and *Sharada* stages from the ancient *Brāhmī* script, reaching its mature recognizably modern typographical form by the eleventh century.
- Encodes grammatical features of inflected languages with extreme phonetic precision, recording case markers such as the accusative [[direct-object]] alongside subtle tonal accents preserved in Vedic recitation.
