## summary

The **Old Fashioned** is a foundational American cocktail prepared by muddling sugar with water and aromatic bitters, blending in whiskey, and garnishing the finished drink with a twist of citrus peel. Emerging in the nineteenth century as an explicit return to the earliest definitions of mixed drinks, it resisted the elaborate cordials, liqueurs, and vermouths then taking over urban saloons. It stands today as the quintessential benchmark of cocktail craft, celebrated for balancing high-proof grain spirits with subtle sweetness and herbal aromatics.

## you gotta know

- Corresponds directly to the first recorded newspaper definition of a cocktail, published in 1806 in *The Balance and Columbian Repository* as a stimulating liquor composed of spirits, sugar, water, and bitters.
- Originated as a specific barroom request during the late nineteenth century, when veteran patrons demanded libations prepared according to the *old-fashioned* method rather than modern, complex concoctions.
- Associated by enduring bartending lore with the *Pendennis Club* in Louisville, Kentucky, where bartender *Martin Cuneo* reportedly mixed it in tribute to prominent bourbon distiller *James E. Pepper*.
- Popularized nationally when *James E. Pepper* brought the house recipe to the celebrated bar of the *Waldorf-Astoria* hotel in [[new-york-city]].
- Lent its name to the standard *Old Fashioned glass*—a flat-bottomed, short tumbler also called a lowball or rocks glass, designed specifically for muddling ingredients and holding large cubes of ice.
- Developed a prominent regional variation in the state of Wisconsin, which traditionally substitutes brandy for whiskey, muddles sweet preserved cherries and orange slices, and finishes the blend with lemon-lime or club soda.
- Frequently customized in modern cocktail lounges with flaming citrus oils, wood smoke, or a light dusting of freshly grated baking spices such as [[nutmeg]].
