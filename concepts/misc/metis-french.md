## summary

**Métis French**, historically termed **Français métis**, is a distinctive dialect of Canadian French developed by the Métis people across the Red River basin and the Canadian Prairies. Originating from the linguistic contact between eighteenth-century French-Canadian fur trade voyageurs and Indigenous nations such as the Plains Cree and Ojibwe, it constitutes a fully French dialect rather than an intertwined mixed language like *Michif*. Celebrated during [[metis-history-month]], it preserves archaic seventeenth- and eighteenth-century French phonology and syntax while incorporating specialized Indigenous loanwords reflecting frontier ecology, river navigation, and bison hunting.

## you gotta know

- Fundamentally distinguished in [[linguistics]] from the mixed language *Michif*; while *Michif* uniquely binds Plains Cree verb structures to French noun phrases, this tongue is a legitimate dialect of French sharing structural and syntactic integrity with standard Romance varieties.
- Traces its historical roots directly to the *coureurs des bois* and French-Canadian voyageurs of the Montreal-based North West Company, who intermarried with First Nations women across Rupert's Land throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
- Preserves antique phonological features of pre-Revolutionary French dialects from Normandy, Poitou, and Île-de-France, including the retention of archaic vowel diphthongs and the audible pronunciation of final consonants commonly silenced in modern Parisian French (such as *icitte* for *ici* and *litte* for *lit*).
- Incorporates extensive botanical, zoological, and topographical borrowings from Plains Cree and Saulteaux (Ojibwe), yielding specialized prairie terms for indigenous flora, fauna, and survival goods like *pemmican*.
- Developed an extensive lexicon centered on early prairie commerce and material life, encapsulating terms for the two-wheeled wooden *charette* (Red River cart), *capote* blanket coats, and *bison* buffalo hunting brigades.
- Served as the primary diplomatic and legislative idiom of the provisional government led by *Louis Riel* at Upper Fort Garry during the Red River Resistance of 1869–1870.
- Faced severe historic marginalization through English-only educational mandates across Western Canada, residential school assimilation policies, and the institutional preference for Standard Laurentian French in Roman Catholic parish schools.
- Remains preserved today primarily by elders in legacy settlements such as St. Laurent and St. Boniface in Manitoba, Batoche in Saskatchewan, and St. Albert in Alberta, supported by grassroots archival recording initiatives.
