## summary

**Chimichurri** is an uncooked herb-based culinary sauce and marinade originating in Argentina and Uruguay, traditionally prepared as an essential accompaniment to grilled meats in the *asado* tradition. Formulated primarily from finely chopped flat-leaf parsley, minced garlic, olive oil, dried oregano, and red wine vinegar, it is prized throughout South American and international cuisine for its sharp acidity and aromatic brightness. The condiment represents a vital milestone in post-colonial culinary history, reflecting the convergence of European immigrant foodways and native livestock traditions in the fertile plains of the Río de la Plata basin.

## you gotta know

- Serves as the premier tabletop condiment of Argentine and Uruguayan *asado* barbecue culture, almost universally paired with cuts such as skirt steak, flank steak (*matambre*), or sausages in the popular street food *choripán*.
- Classical green formulation, known as **chimichurri verde**, relies on fresh flat-leaf parsley, minced garlic, dried oregano, crushed red pepper flakes, olive oil, and red wine vinegar or white vinegar.
- A distinctive red variation, **chimichurri rojo**, incorporates roasted red bell peppers, paprika (*pimentón*), hot chili flakes, and occasionally tomato paste or ground cumin for deeper color and warmer piquancy.
- Traditionally prepared without [[cilantro]], whose soapy or citrus notes conflict with the classic profile; modern adaptations in North America often substitute or blend cilantro, a practice widely regarded as unauthentic by South American purists.
- Often compared to other regional oil-and-herb emulsion sauces and vinaigrettes, such as Venezuelan *guasacaca*, Brazilian *molho a campanha*, Chilean *pebre*, and Italian *salsa verde*.
- Multiple competing etymological folk theories exist, such as a corruption of the name of an English meat importer named *Jimmy Curry* or an Irish adventurer named *Jimmy McCurry*, or British prisoners pleading for condiment (*give me curry*), though linguists frequently trace the name to the Basque term *tximitxurri*, loosely translating to "a mixture of things in no particular order".
- Frequently brushed onto grilling beef during the final moments of cooking or left to macerate for twenty-four to forty-eight hours prior to serving, allowing the volatile allicin from the garlic and the aromatic oils of the oregano to fully permeate the lipid base.

## connections

- [[cilantro]] — frequently substituted for or combined with flat-leaf parsley in non-traditional North American variations.
- [[chipotle]] — sometimes blended with chimichurri in modern pan-American fusion marinades to introduce wood-smoked heat to grilled steaks.
- [[cinco-de-mayo]] — contrasts the celebratory barbecue and salsa traditions of Mexico with the pampa beef culture of South America.
- [[spain]] — contributed wine vinegar, olive oil, and garlic traditions, as well as the initial herds of cattle that transformed the Argentine Pampas.
- [[colonialism]] — established the ranching economy and transatlantic trade routes that fostered distinctive gaucho culinary traditions.
- [[five-senses]] — stimulated simultaneously by the sauce's biting acetic acid, aromatic alliums, herbal bitterness, and rich oily mouthfeel.
