## summary

In grammar, **mood** refers to a morphological and syntactic category that expresses a speaker's attitude toward the reality, likelihood, obligation, or necessity of the proposition expressed by a verb. Distinct from grammatical tense and aspect, it allows speakers to distinguish factual assertions from hypothetical scenarios, commands, and wishes. Encoded across languages via verbal inflection, auxiliary verbs, or dedicated particles, it forms an essential axis of semantic and pragmatic communication across classical and modern tongues.

## you gotta know

- The indicative is the default, unmarked variety across most languages, used specifically to state objective facts, make assertions, and pose factual questions.
- The subjunctive expresses counterfactuals, hypothetical situations, desires, doubts, or conditional outcomes, highly prominent in Romance languages and surviving vestigially in English.
- The imperative issues direct commands, instructions, or prohibitions, often characterized by null subject pronouns or simplified verbal stems.
- Cross-linguistically accompanied by specialized forms such as the optative for wishes, the conditional for contingent events, and the jussive or cohortative for third- or first-person entreaties.
- Closely tied to the broader semantic domain of modality, which encompasses epistemic certainty, deontic permission and duty, and dynamic ability.
- Realized typologically through synthetic verbal inflections comprised of an inflectional [[morpheme]], or through periphrastic constructions using modal auxiliary verbs.
- Irrealis modes contrast systematically with realis modes by signaling that the described event has not occurred or belongs purely to potentiality.

## connections

- [[morpheme]] — basic structural unit of language that commonly affixes to verbal stems to encode modal distinctions.
- [[linguistics]] — parent field encompassing the syntactic, semantic, and morphological frameworks used to classify modal categories.
- [[ancient-greece]] — language whose classical grammar features an elaborate four-mood system consisting of the indicative, subjunctive, optative, and imperative.
- [[roman-empire]] — civilization whose Latin tongue cemented traditional Western grammatical paradigms, heavily merging earlier Indo-European modal nuances into the subjunctive.
- [[france]] — modern culture whose Romance language maintains a rigorous, highly visible inflectional subjunctive system in subordinate clauses.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — dramatist whose Early Modern English plays frequently utilized flexible modal verbs and surviving subjunctive constructions.
