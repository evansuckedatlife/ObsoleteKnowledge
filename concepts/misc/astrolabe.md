## summary

The **astrolabe**, most commonly encountered in the form of the **Planispheric Astrolabe**, is a sophisticated two-dimensional brass instrument that functioned as both an observational device and an analog mechanical computer across the classical, medieval, and early modern eras. Developed originally by Hellenistic mathematicians and elevated to unprecedented mathematical and mechanical refinement during the Islamic Golden Age, the instrument enabled users to determine local solar time, map the positions of the stars, and calculate celestial coordinates. It represents one of the crowning achievements of pre-modern technology, bridging the astronomical knowledge of antiquity with the scientific developments of medieval Islam and Renaissance Europe.

## you gotta know

- Composed structurally of a heavy outer casing called the *mater* (mother), inside of which sit interchangeable circular plates called *tympans* engraved for specific geographic latitudes, covered by an ornate, rotatable openwork stellar map called the *rete*.
- Functions mathematically through the principle of stereographic projection, mapping the three-dimensional celestial sphere onto a flat, two-dimensional coordinate plane while preserving circles and true angular measurements.
- Perfected by scientists and artisans in the medieval Islamic world, where instruments incorporated scales for calculating the canonical hours of Muslim prayer and determining the sacred direction of the *qibla* toward Mecca.
- Features an ecliptic ring upon its pierced brass *rete* that tracks the path of the Sun across the zodiac, marked with constellations such as [[aquarius]] and accented with sharp stellar pointers indicating prominent guide stars.
- Utilizes a rotating sighting arm on its reverse side termed the *alidade*, which an observer aimed toward the Sun or a known star while suspending the instrument vertically by a cord to measure celestial altitude.
- Prominent historical makers include tenth-century Syrian artisan *Mariam al-Ijliya* (known as *al-Asturlabiya*), who crafted exquisite precision brass devices for the court of *Sayf al-Dawla* in Aleppo.
- Transmitted into Western Europe via Muslim [[spain]] (al-Andalus) and Sicily, leading to extensive technical translations and inspiring the English author *Geoffrey Chaucer* to compose his *Treatise on the Astrolabe* for his son Lewis.
- Gradually superseded from the sixteenth century onward by specialized navigational and mathematical instruments, including the mariner's quadrant, the sextant, and precision mechanical clocks.

## connections

- [[astronomy]] — core natural science for which the instrument tracked celestial motions, solar elevations, and stellar positions.
- [[geometry]] — mathematical branch providing the stereographic projection principles necessary to project spherical coordinates onto flat planes.
- [[arabic]] — primary scientific language used to compose foundational medieval treatises and engrave star pointers on classical instruments.
- [[aquarius]] — zodiacal constellation whose ecliptic coordinates are inscribed upon the instrument's rotating rete.
- [[ancient-greece]] — classical civilization where pioneering astronomers including *Hipparchus* and *Theon of Alexandria* established the underlying mathematical theory.
- [[middle-east]] — region where medieval craft guilds and scholars in Baghdad, Damascus, and Isfahan refined metal casting and precision scale dividing.
- [[spain]] — cultural conduit where the multicultural translation centers of Moorish al-Andalus introduced the instrument to Christian Europe.
- [[renaissance]] — European intellectual era that witnessed renewed production of metal planispheres prior to the invention of the astronomical telescope.
