## summary

A **comma splice**, also called a **comma fault**, is a grammatical construction in which two independent clauses are joined by a comma without an accompanying coordinating conjunction. In prescriptive grammar, it is widely classified as an error in formal written English, though it has been deliberately used by notable authors for rhythmic pacing and rhetorical emphasis. Knowledge of this construction frequently arises in language arts, editing theory, and stylistic analysis of literary modernism.

## you gotta know

- Defined in standard syntax as the improper linkage of two complete grammatical thoughts using only a comma, creating a run-on sentence that violates prescriptive punctuation norms.
- Resolved conventionally in prescriptive style guides by adding a coordinating conjunction (*and*, *but*, *for*, *nor*, *or*, *so*, *yet*), inserting a semicolon, separating the clauses into two distinct sentences with a period, or converting one clause into a dependent subordinate clause.
- Distinguished from standard asyndeton, a classical rhetorical device where coordinating conjunctions are omitted intentionally between concise, parallel independent clauses to emphasize urgency, momentum, or stark contrast.
- Exemplified historically by famous Latin translations such as *Julius Caesar*'s military dispatch *"Veni, vidi, vici"*, conventionally translated into English as *"I came, I saw, I conquered"*, which uses commas alone between short, related assertions.
- Condemned systematically by 20th-century prescriptive usage arbiters, most famously by *William Strunk Jr.* and *E. B. White* in *The Elements of Style*, which warned that writers should not join independent clauses with a comma alone.
- Employed intentionally throughout [[american-literature]] and British literature by writers such as [[william-shakespeare]], *Jane Austen*, and *Virginia Woolf* to simulate fast-paced dialogue, colloquial speech, or emotional agitation.
- Featured heavily in stream-of-consciousness narratives characteristic of [[modernism]], where authors like *William Faulkner* and *James Joyce* disregarded conventional punctuation rules to evoke interior psychological reality.

## connections

- [[linguistics]] — discipline that analyzes clause coordination, syntactic boundaries, and the tension between prescriptive rules and descriptive language usage.
- [[american-literature]] — literary tradition wherein modernist and realist writers frequently broke punctuation conventions to replicate authentic regional dialects and spontaneous speech.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — early modern playwright whose First Folio texts and early quartos frequently utilized light, rhetorical punctuation that included spliced clauses.
- [[england]] — birthplace of the modern English grammatical tradition, where 18th-century grammarians first attempted to codify strict syntactic boundaries.
- [[united-states]] — nation where school curricula and collegiate composition handbooks popularized the rigorous prohibition against run-on punctuation errors.
- [[realism]] — 19th-century artistic movement whose commitment to depicting unfiltered everyday human speech led authors to utilize natural syntactic splices.
- [[modernism]] — early 20th-century cultural movement that revolutionized literary form by abandoning traditional punctuation to capture subjective psychological streams.
