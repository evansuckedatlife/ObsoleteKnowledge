## summary

**Perception** is the cognitive process by which the brain interprets sensory data into meaningful experience. It differs from mere [[sensation]]: two people can sense the same stimulus but perceive it differently based on attention, expectation, and prior knowledge. Perception is active, constructive, and shaped by both the external world and the observer's mind.

## you gotta know

- Sensation is the *detection* of stimuli (light hits the retina); perception is the *interpretation* (the brain recognizes it as your friend's face).
- The brain makes *predictive inferences* about incoming sensory data, constantly updating models based on past experience — perception is less like a passive camera and more like an active hypothesis-testing machine.
- *Selective attention* means we perceive only a fraction of available stimuli at any moment; the brain filters based on relevance, novelty, and emotional significance (e.g., hearing your own name in a crowded room).
- Perceptual *organization* follows gestalt principles: we group similar elements, perceive wholes rather than parts, and fill gaps to create coherent scenes from fragmented input.
- *Perceptual constancy* — recognizing a door as a rectangle even when viewed at an angle, or paper as white even under dim light — reveals how the brain corrects for viewing conditions and lighting.
- Cultural, emotional, and developmental factors shape perception: hungry people "see" food-related shapes more readily; native speakers of a language distinguish phonemes their native ear trained for; infants develop perceptual categories through experience.
- *Binocular depth cues* (disparity between eyes) and *monocular cues* (occlusion, perspective, shadow) allow the brain to construct 3D space from 2D retinal images.
- Optical illusions exploit perceptual mechanisms (the Müller-Lyer illusion, impossible figures, the spinning dancer), showing that what we "see" is a neural construction, not a direct window onto objective reality.
