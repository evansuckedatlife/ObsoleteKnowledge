## summary

The **exclamation point** is a punctuation mark consisting of a vertical stroke positioned above a baseline period, used to denote heightened emphasis, intense emotion, interjections, or loud volume. Originating among humanists during the fourteenth century as the Latin *punctus admirativus*, it spread across printing houses throughout early modern Europe. In typography, literary criticism, and computer programming, it represents both a vibrant emotional marker and an object of enduring stylistic debate.

## you gotta know

- Invented in fourteenth-century Italy by the scholar *Iacopo Alpolo da Fabriano*, who used a stroke with a dot beneath it to indicate grammatical exclamation or admiration in 1399.
- Italian humanist *Coluccio Salutati*, Chancellor of Florence, embraced and systematized the mark, naming it the *punctus admirativus* (point of wonder or admiration).
- Known traditionally in early English grammar as the *note of admiration* or *mark of admiration* through the eighteenth century before shifting toward its modern designation.
- Typographical lore often traces the shape to the Latin exclamatory word *io* (an exclamation of triumph), where scribes stacked the capital *I* above the lowercase *o*, though manuscript evidence shows it developed from the *punctus elevatus*.
- Early manual typewriters produced until the 1970s frequently omitted a dedicated key for the glyph, requiring typists to type a period, backspace, and overstrike an apostrophe.
- In modern computing jargon, the glyph is universally known as the *bang*, notably utilized in Unix *shebang* sequences (`#!`) and as a logical negation operator in languages such as C.
- Celebrated authors have famously cautioned against its overuse; *F. Scott Fitzgerald* remarked that using an exclamation mark is like laughing at your own joke, while *Elmore Leonard* advised never using more than two or three per 100,000 words.
- In comic strip conventions and early modern typesetting, a combined question mark and exclamation mark gave rise to experimental punctuation, culminating in *Martin K. Speckter's* 1962 invention of the *interrobang*.

- [[etiquette]] — the epistolary code that traditionally regulated emotional restraint and discouraged excessive punctuation in polite society.
- [[england]] — the realm where Elizabethan and Jacobean printers adapted continental Italian punctuation into standard English syntax.
