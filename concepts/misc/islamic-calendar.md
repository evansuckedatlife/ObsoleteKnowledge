## summary

The **Islamic calendar** (also called the *Hijri calendar*) is a purely lunar calendar used throughout the Islamic world to date events and determine religious observances. Unlike the solar *Gregorian calendar*, its months drift relative to the seasons, anchored instead to the *Hijra*—Prophet Muhammad's migration from Mecca to Medina in 622 CE.

## you gotta know

- Purely lunar: each month is either 29 or 30 days (matching actual lunar phases), making the year approximately 354 days—about 11 days shorter than the solar year.
- Year 1 AH (Anno Hegirae) corresponds to 622 CE, the date of the Hijra; dates are often written as "1445 AH" to distinguish them from the Common Era.
- Because the year is shorter than the solar year, Islamic months rotate through all seasons over a 33-year cycle—*Ramadan* is not always in the same season.
- Ramadan, the ninth month, is the Islamic holy month of fasting and spiritual renewal; the exact dates shift backward 10–11 days each solar year.
- The *Hijri New Year* falls on the first day of *Muharram* (the first lunar month), a date that changes relative to the Gregorian calendar annually.
- Coexists globally with the Gregorian calendar: Islamic-majority countries often use both systems for legal, administrative, and religious purposes.
- Month names carry religious and historical significance: *Rajab* is the month of pilgrimage; *Dhu al-Hijjah* is when the Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca occurs.
- The lunar year's shorter length means Muslims celebrate Islamic holidays at different times each solar year—ancient Ramadans in summer, later Ramadans in winter, a feature central to Islamic practice.
- Modern Islamic institutions use astronomical calculations to determine month boundaries precisely, though some communities still rely on actual moon sighting (ruyat al-hilal) for traditional observance.
