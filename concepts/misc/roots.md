## summary

In mathematics, **roots**—also termed *zeros*—are values within a function's domain that cause the function to evaluate to zero. Across algebra and analysis, finding the roots of polynomial equations has driven centuries of mathematical development, from classical geometric constructions to modern field theory. Understanding root multiplicity and the algebraic properties of solutions provides the essential bridge between geometric curve intersections and abstract numerical systems.

## you gotta know

- The *Fundamental Theorem of Algebra*, rigorously proven by mathematicians in *[[germany]]* including *Carl Friedrich Gauss*, states that every non-zero single-variable polynomial of degree *n* with complex coefficients has exactly *n* complex roots, counted with multiplicity.
- *Descartes' Rule of Signs* provides an upper bound on the number of positive and negative real roots of a polynomial by counting the sign alternations between consecutive non-zero coefficients.
- The quadratic formula gives the exact roots of second-degree polynomials, relying on the *discriminant* to determine whether solutions are distinct real numbers, repeated real numbers, or complex conjugates.
- The *Abel-Ruffini theorem* demonstrated that general polynomial equations of degree five or higher cannot be solved in radicals, an insight that led directly to *Évariste Galois* developing *Galois theory* in *[[france]]*.
- *Newton's method*, developed during the *[[enlightenment]]*, uses iterative tangent line approximations to compute successive estimates of real roots for differentiable functions.
- In complex analysis, *Rouché's theorem* allows mathematicians to determine the number of roots of holomorphic functions inside a closed contour by comparing their boundary magnitudes.
- Geometrically, the real roots of a real-valued function correspond precisely to the *x-intercepts* where its graph crosses or touches the horizontal axis.

## connections

- [[geometry]] — real roots mark the precise spatial coordinates where curves intersect coordinate axes.
- [[ancient-greece]] — classical mathematicians studied geometric methods for extracting square roots and cube roots.
- [[germany]] — home to pioneers of algebra such as *Carl Friedrich Gauss* who analyzed root distribution.
- [[france]] — origin of fundamental breakthroughs regarding root solubility by *Évariste Galois* and *René Descartes*.
- [[enlightenment]] — the era that formalized iterative calculus algorithms like *Newton's method* for approximating roots.
- [[renaissance]] — the period when algebraic formulas for cubic and quartic roots were first published in *[[italy]]*.
