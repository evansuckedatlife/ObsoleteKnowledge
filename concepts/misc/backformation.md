## summary

**backformation** (or **back-formation**) is a morphological process in *[[linguistics]]* where a new lexeme is coined by removing an actual or perceived affix from an existing word. It typically occurs when speakers misinterpret an established, structurally simplex word as a derived form—such as mistaking the historical noun *editor* for a suffixation of the nonexistent verb *edit*. As a vital engine of lexical change in English, it has produced numerous foundational verbs and nouns across centuries of linguistic evolution.

## you gotta know

- Coined as a linguistic term by Scottish lexicographer *James Murray*, the primary editor of the *Oxford English Dictionary*, in the late nineteenth century.
- Contrasts with regular affixation: rather than creating a longer word by adding prefixes or suffixes, it creates a shorter, simpler base by pruning an imagined affix.
- Famous examples include the creation of the verb *edit* from the prior noun *editor*, *peddle* from *peddler*, and *burgle* from *burglar*.
- The word *pea* represents a famous nominal back-formation, created because the singular Old English noun *pease* was mistaken for a plural form.
- The process often arises from folk etymology and reanalysis, where speakers intuitively apply common morphological templates to borrowed or fossilized words.
- Many back-formed verbs initially face severe criticism as barbarisms or slang from prescriptivist grammarians before gaining universal acceptance, such as *donate* (from *donation*) and *enthuse* (from *enthusiasm*).
- Modern technical and colloquial coinages continue to exploit this pattern, such as *liaise* (from *liaison*), *escalate* (from *escalator*), and *tether* (reanalyzed from compound nouns).
