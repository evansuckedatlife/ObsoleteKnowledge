## summary

In chess strategy, **Tempo (Chess)** (often referred to simply as **chess tempo**, with the plural *tempi*) designates the basic unit of dynamic operational time, quantified as the execution of a single legal turn. When a competitor develops pieces or launches threats while forcing their opponent to make passive, repetitive defensive maneuvers, they are said to be **gaining a tempo**, capturing the initiative and pressing their tactical advantage. Conversely, an unnecessary retreat or misplaced piece can cause a player to suffer by **losing a tempo**, handing the opponent dynamic momentum that can rapidly decide the contest.

## you gotta know

- Serves as the foundational currency of dynamic initiative in chess theory, where completing tactical objectives in fewer moves than conventional opening paths yields decisive spatial control.
- Gambit openings systematically sacrifice pawns or minor pieces specifically to gain multiple tempi, clearing diagonals and files to launch lethal early assaults before the defender can castle.
- Attacking an prematurely developed enemy queen with knights and bishops is the most common tactical method for **gaining a tempo**, as the queen must retreat while the attacker develops active pieces.
- In pawn endgames, players utilize triangulation, a geometric king maneuver where one king moves in a triangle to consume three turns while the defending king only has two squares, thereby effectively **losing a tempo** to force the opponent into *zugzwang*.
- The classical chess dictum formulated by Adolf Anderssen and Wilhelm Steinitz notes that time lost in open positions can never be recovered, whereas time lost in closed, locked positions is often harmless.
- During the [[renaissance]], European rule changes that permitted pawns to advance two squares on their initial move were specifically introduced to accelerate opening development and heighten tactical pace.
