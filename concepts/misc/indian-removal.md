## summary

**Indian Removal** was the U.S. government's policy of forcibly displacing Native American nations from their ancestral lands to the western frontier (primarily present-day Oklahoma), enacted through the Indian Removal Act of 1830. It stands as a catastrophic chapter in American history, justified by expansion ideology but implemented as ethnic cleansing that devastated Indigenous populations.

## you gotta know

- Authorized by President *Andrew Jackson*, the Indian Removal Act of 1830 gave the federal government the power to negotiate removal treaties—or impose them unilaterally.
- Justified by expansionist ideology: settler colonists demanded Native lands for agriculture and development; official rhetoric framed removal as "civilizing" and "protecting" Indigenous peoples.
- The *Five Civilized Tribes*—Cherokee, Creek, Chickasaw, Choctaw, and Seminole—were the primary targets, despite having adopted European-style governance and agriculture.
- The *Trail of Tears* (1838–1839) was the forced march of thousands of Cherokee west, killing thousands along the way through disease, starvation, and exposure.
- Removal was traumatic and disruptive: it shattered cultural continuity, separated communities, and concentrated Indigenous nations in a foreign landscape with limited resources.
- Set a precedent for subsequent forced relocations and reservations, becoming foundational to U.S. Indian policy for the century that followed.
- The Supreme Court ruled against Georgia in *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832), asserting tribal sovereignty, but Jackson famously refused to enforce the decision—a turning point in the Court's weakness.
- Not all tribes were removed by force; some negotiated and left voluntarily, but even these migrations were deeply disruptive and costly.
- The policy's legacy persists: chronic poverty, health crises, and political marginalization in Oklahoma and other reservation lands stem directly from Indian Removal and subsequent federal neglect.
- Other removals followed: the Seminole Wars, the forced march of the Navajo (the Long Walk of 1864), and later reservations—Indian Removal set the precedent for a century of dispossession.
