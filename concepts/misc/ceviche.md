## summary

**Ceviche** (frequently rendered as **cebiche** or **seviche**) is an iconic Latin American culinary preparation, celebrated as the national dish of Peru, consisting of fresh raw seafood cured in citrus juice. Rather than utilizing heat, the citric acid in the marinade denatures the proteins in the fish flesh, causing it to become firm and opaque in a manner that closely mimics thermal cooking. Recognized as part of the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, it reflects an enduring synthesis of pre-Columbian maritime customs and Old World culinary introductions.

## you gotta know

- Prepared fundamentally by marinating firm, fresh ocean whitefish—such as sea bass, flounder, or corvina—in freshly squeezed lime juice seasoned with sliced red onions (*cebolla roja*), salt, and spicy native chili peppers.
- Yields a celebrated, milky residual marinade known as *leche de tigre* ("tiger's milk"), which concentrates the juices of the fish, chilies, and citrus; it is frequently served in a shot glass as an invigorating beverage, popular hangover remedy, and reputed aphrodisiac.
- Accompanied traditionally in Peruvian dining by sweet and starchy sides that temper the aggressive acidity and heat, most notably boiled sweet potato (*camote*), giant-kernel Andean corn (*choclo*), and crisp pan-roasted corn kernels (*cancha*).
- Derived historically from the coastal traditions of the pre-Inca *Moche* culture, who preserved seafood with the fermented juice of passionfruit (*tumbo*) or corn beer (*chicha*) prior to the introduction of Eurasian citrus fruits.
- Transformed by the introduction of Mediterranean limes, bitter Seville oranges, and red onions transported by *[[spain]]* during the sixteenth century, creating the modern flavor profile.
- Underwent a significant modern evolution influenced by Japanese-Peruvian (*Nikkei*) gastronomy in the late twentieth century, shifting from multi-hour marinations to a rapid, flash-cured preparation that preserves raw fish tenderness.
- Inspired the related Nikkei delicacy *tiradito*, in which raw fish is sliced into thin strips reminiscent of sashimi, served without onions, and dressed with a spicy citrus emulsion immediately prior to consumption.
- Showcases prominent regional variations across Latin America, including Ecuadorian styles featuring poached shrimp served in a citrus-tomato broth with plantain chips (*chifles*), and Mexican versions served atop crunchy corn tostadas with avocado.

## connections

- [[spain]] — European power whose maritime conquests introduced Mediterranean limes, bitter oranges, and onions that established the modern recipe.
- [[five-senses]] — biological framework governing the sensory reception of intense citrus sourness, pungency, salinity, and raw fish texture.
- [[perception]] — cognitive and gustatory processing of chemical denaturation and capsaicin heat, creating the sensation of cooked protein without heat.
- [[colonialism]] — historical exchange of culinary traditions, crops, and domestic ingredients across the Atlantic that reshaped Andean coastal diets.
- [[cassoulet]] — contrasting classic preparation of *[[france]]* that relies on prolonged thermal stewing rather than cold acidic curing to break down protein.
