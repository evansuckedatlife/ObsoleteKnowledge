## summary

A **cloister** is an enclosed rectangular courtyard surrounded by covered, vaulted walkways or arcades, characteristic of monasteries, abbeys, and collegiate cathedrals. Emerging across medieval Western Europe under Christian monastic orders such as the Benedictines and Cistercians, the cloister served as a secluded transition zone connecting liturgical sanctuaries to domestic and communal facilities. Its architectural layout embodies the ascetic ideal of spiritual contemplation separated from worldly distractions, serving as the social, spiritual, and logistical center of monastic life.

## you gotta know

- The classic cloister plan features a central quadrangular open lawn or garden called a garth, framed on all four sides by covered ambulatories with arcaded stone openings facing inward.
- Originates from the Latin *claustrum*, signifying "enclosure" or "bolt," which also gives rise to the English verb to cloister and metonymically refers to the monastic life of ascetic retreat.
- In standard monastic layouts, the cloister is positioned immediately south of the cathedral or abbey nave to capture southern sunlight and shelter monks from cold winds, though local topography occasionally forced a northern placement.
- Beyond serving as sheltered passage between the church, chapter house, dormitory, and refectory, walkways held stone lavatorium troughs for ritual washing and desks where monks copied manuscripts.
- The Cistercian architectural reform forbade elaborate carved ornamentation in cloisters, reacting directly against the opulent Cluniac Romanesque style that featured narrative capitals carved with biblical beasts, demons, and foliage.
- *The Cloisters*, a renowned branch of the Metropolitan Museum of Art located in *Upper Manhattan*, *New York City*, was assembled from dismantled architectural portions of five medieval French monasteries, including *Saint-Michel-de-Cuxa*.
- Masterpieces of Gothic cloister design include the elaborate fan-vaulted walks of *Gloucester Cathedral* in *England* and the tiered double-arcaded walkways at *Mont-Saint-Michel* in Normandy.

## connections

- [[architecture]] — provides the structural typology of groin-vaulted and rib-vaulted arcades enclosing open quadrangles.
- [[christianity]] — the religious tradition whose monastic rules made the cloister the spatial core of communal spiritual practice.
- [[france]] — country rich in celebrated Romanesque and Gothic cloisters, including *Cluny*, *Fontenay*, *Moissac*, and *Mont-Saint-Michel*.
- [[england]] — site of magnificent medieval cathedral cloisters, notably at *Gloucester*, *Canterbury*, *Salisbury*, and *Wells*.
- [[italy]] — location of early monastic motherhouses such as *Monte Cassino* and intricate Cosmatesque marble cloisters in *Rome*.
- [[new-york-city]] — home to *The Cloisters* museum in Fort Tryon Park, showcasing reconstructed medieval monastic elements.
- [[renaissance]] — transformed the cloistered quadrangle into secular urban spaces, influencing palace courtyards and university quads.
