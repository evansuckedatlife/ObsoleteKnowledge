## summary

**Gorgonzola** is a celebrated veined blue cheese produced from unskimmed cow's milk in the northern regions of *Piedmont* and *Lombardy* in [[italy]]. Tracing its documented lineage back to the eleventh century, it has attained protected designation of origin status and stands as one of the culinary pillars of Italian gastronomy. In competitive trivia and academic quiz bowl, it frequently appears as an exemplar of mold-ripened dairy, famous for its distinct pungency, creamy texture, and the introduction of fungal cultures during the aging process.

## you gotta know

- Recognizable worldwide by its characteristic greenish-blue marbled veins, which are produced by piercing the curd with copper or stainless needles to introduce oxygen and promote the growth of the mold *Penicillium roqueforti* or *Penicillium glaucum*.
- Classified broadly into two commercial varieties: *dolce*, which is aged for roughly two to three months to yield a mild, sweet, and spreadable paste, and *piccante* (or *naturale*), aged for six months or more to create a firmer, crumbly, and sharp product.
- Granted European Protected Designation of Origin (*PDO*) protection in 1996, restricting authentic production strictly to designated provinces within *Lombardy* and *Piedmont*, including *Milan*, *Novara*, *Bergamo*, *Brescia*, and *Pavia*.
- Named after the eponymous town of *Gorgonzola* near *Milan*, where historical legend asserts it was first created in 879 CE when a distracted herdsman combined fresh curd with resting curd from the previous evening.
- Traditionally served alongside *polenta*, melted into creamy sauces for *gnocchi* or *risotto*, and paired with sweet fruits like figs and pears or fortified dessert wines like *vin santo*.
- Its distinctive sharp aroma is driven by volatile short-chain fatty acids and methyl ketones generated during lipolysis, engaging the retro-nasal olfactory pathways of the human sensory apparatus.
- Often contrasted in culinary trivia against other foundational European blue cheeses, particularly French *Roquefort* made from ewe's milk and English *Stilton* produced in designated midland counties.
