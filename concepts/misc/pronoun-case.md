## summary

**pronoun case** refers to the grammatical inflection of a [[pronoun]] that indicates its syntactic function and relationship to other words in a sentence. While modern English has discarded most noun inflections inherited from its Germanic ancestors, personal pronouns preserve an overt tripartite system: subjective (nominative), objective (accusative-dative), and possessive (genitive). Mastery of these distinct forms constitutes a classical touchstone of prescriptive grammar, syntactical parsing, and formal stylistic analysis in [[linguistics]].

## you gotta know

- Divides in standard English into three primary cases: the subjective or nominative case used for grammatical subjects and predicate nominatives; the objective or accusative case used for direct objects, indirect objects, and objects of prepositions; and the possessive or genitive case indicating ownership.
- Demonstrates morphological case alternation across personal pronouns, contrasting subjective forms (*I*, *we*, *he*, *she*, *they*) with objective forms (*me*, *us*, *him*, *her*, *them*) and possessive determiners or substantives (*my*/*mine*, *our*/*ours*, *his*, *her*/*hers*, *their*/*theirs*).
- Features the second-person pronoun *you*, which historically distinguished between subjective *ye* and objective *you* in Early Modern English before collapsing into a single, invariable shape.
- Generates frequent hypercorrection in coordinate noun phrases, where speakers mistakenly deploy subjective pronouns in objective syntactic positions, such as saying *between you and I* instead of *between you and me*.
- Governs the relative and interrogative pronoun system, maintaining a sharp prescriptive divide between the subjective *who* and the objective *whom*, the latter of which has undergone progressive decline in colloquial speech.
- Retains archaic reflexes of old Indo-European nominal inflection systems that flourished in classical tongues like Latin and Greek, surviving in modern Germanic and Romance languages as an irregular morphological residue.
- Interacts with elliptical comparative constructions involving conjunctions such as *than* or *as*, where traditional prescriptive syntax demands case assignment based on implied predicates (for example, *she runs faster than he* rather than *him*).

## connections

- [[pronoun]] — the core lexical category whose morphological forms are determined and organized by case systems.
- [[linguistics]] — the academic discipline that models syntactic structure, morphological paradigms, and historical language variation.
- [[ancient-greece]] — civilization whose grammarians, such as the Stoics and the scholars of Alexandria, first codified nominal case categories.
- [[roman-empire]] — realm whose Latin grammatical tradition established the standard terminology of nominative, accusative, genitive, and dative still applied to modern syntax.
- [[england]] — nation whose linguistic evolution witnessed the reduction of Old English complex nominal case into vestigial pronominal inflection.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — early modern dramatist whose plays document the transitional fluid usage of personal pronominal inflections like *who* and *whom*.
