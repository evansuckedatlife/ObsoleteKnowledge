## summary

A **verb** is the syntactic and semantic anchor of the predicate in human language, functioning as the primary part of speech that conveys an action, state, event, or occurrence. Deriving etymologically from the Latin *verbum* meaning "word," it governs clausal argument structure by assigning semantic roles such as agent, patient, and instrument to nominal phrases. Recognized across diverse grammatical traditions, **verbs** display rich inflectional morphology to mark grammatical categories including tense, aspect, mood, voice, and agreement.

## you gotta know

- Determines clause valency by selecting core arguments, distinguishing intransitive predicates requiring only a subject, transitive predicates demanding a direct object, and ditransitive predicates taking both direct and indirect objects.
- Expresses the temporal location and contour of states and actions through inflectional systems of tense (past, present, future), aspect (perfective, imperfective, progressive), and mood (indicative, subjunctive, imperative).
- The fundamental copula, exemplified in English by *to be*, operates as a specialized linking element that pairs a subject with a predicate adjective or noun phrase rather than denoting dynamic action.
- Distinguishes between regular and irregular morphology, seen in Germanic tongues through the split between weak forms using dental suffixes and strong forms employing Indo-European ablaut vowel alternation.
- Voice morphology modulates the structural mapping of semantic participants, shifting focus between active voice (agent as subject), passive voice (patient promoted to subject), and middle voice constructions.
- Appears in non-finite forms such as infinitives, participles, and gerunds, which suppress tense or agreement to operate syntactically as noun heads, adjectives, or adverbial adjuncts.
- While virtually all linguistic traditions identify this class alongside nouns as a universal structural foundation, languages vary drastically from isolating systems with invariant roots to polysynthetic systems where an entire sentence is incorporated into a single verbal complex.

## connections

- [[linguistics]] — primary academic discipline examining clausal syntax, predicate-argument structure, and verbal morphology.
- [[ancient-greece]] — early syntactic analysis pioneered by *Plato* and *Aristotle*, who divided declarative sentences into *onoma* (noun) and *rhema* (verb).
- [[roman-empire]] — classical Latin grammarians like *Aelius Donatus* and *Priscian* systematically categorized parts of speech under the label *verbum*.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — celebrated for poetic innovation in English syntax, frequently transforming established nouns into vigorous dynamic actions through functional shift.
- [[enlightenment]] — philosophical era that advanced universal grammar theories analyzing how mental judgments are enacted through predication.
- [[variation-chess]] — procedural game systems encode piece rules through precise operational actions such as leaping, capturing, and sliding.
