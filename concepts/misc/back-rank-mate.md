## summary

A **back-rank mate** (also known as a **back-rank checkmate** or **corridor mate**) is a quintessential checkmate pattern in chess delivered by a rook or queen along an opponent's first or eighth rank. The victimized king is trapped on its home rank because its escape squares on the rank directly ahead are blocked by its own friendly pieces, most typically a castled shield of three unmoved pawns. Recognizable and deadly across all skill levels, it is one of the most fundamental tactical themes in chess literature.

## you gotta know

- Occurs when a rook or queen infiltrates the opponent's back row (the first rank for White, or the eighth rank for Black) while the enemy king is trapped.
- The entrapment is conventionally caused by friendly pawns stationed on squares directly in front of the castled king (e.g., pawns on f7, g7, and h7 for Black), leaving no flight square.
- Standard defensive prophylaxis against this tactic involves creating a *luft* (German for "air"), typically achieved by nudging the h-pawn or g-pawn forward by one square.
- Often set up tactically via deflection, sacrifice, or clearing combinations, such as sacrificing a queen on the back rank to drag away an enemy defending rook.
- A classic illustrative game is *Adams versus Torre* (New Orleans, 1920), which featured repeated, brilliant queen offers to overload White's defense of the back rank.
- Known alternatively as the "corridor mate" because the king is trapped along a narrow corridor of squares behind its own defensive wall.
- One of the earliest tactical motifs taught to beginners, while remaining a recurring blind spot and tactical blunder even in master-level play.
