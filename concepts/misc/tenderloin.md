## summary

**Tenderloin** designates both the most prized, non-weight-bearing cut of beef or pork and a notorious nineteenth-century vice district in Manhattan. Derived from the elongated *psoas major* muscle along the lumbar spine, **beef tenderloin** yields iconic culinary luxuries including **filet mignon** and **Chateaubriand**, achieving world-renowned tenderness precisely because the muscle performs minimal physical exertion during locomotion. In municipal slang, nineteenth-century police officials adopted the culinary moniker for decadent entertainment precincts where lucrative bribes allowed officers to feast on tenderloin rather than humble chuck.

## you gotta know

- Corresponds anatomically to the sublumbar *psoas major* muscle, an internal flexor that undergoes virtually no structural strain during quadrupeds' ordinary locomotion, resulting in extraordinarily fine grain and minimal connective tissue.
- The thick center cut of the beef loin is roasted whole to create the classic **Chateaubriand**, while the tapered ends are carved into medallions called **filet mignon** and tournedos.
- Because it possesses negligible intramuscular marbling and almost no collagen, the cut dries out rapidly when overcooked and is frequently barded with pork fat, roasted in pastry as *beef Wellington*, or served with rich butter emulsions.
- Inspector Alexander S. Williams coined the urban term in 1876 upon being transferred to Manhattan's West Side precinct between 24th and 40th streets, quipping that he had eaten chuck steak on quiet beats but was now going to have tenderloin.
- The name subsequently spread across the [[united-states]], giving rise to San Francisco's historic Tenderloin neighborhood, which emerged as a famed center of jazz entertainment, boxing culture, and pioneering transgender civil activism.
- During the [[american-civil-war]] and Gilded Age urbanization, industrial slaughterhouses in Chicago mechanized primal beef fabrication, standardizing the commercial extraction of loins for nationwide luxury markets.
