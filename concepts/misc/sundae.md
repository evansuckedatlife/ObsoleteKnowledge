## summary

An **ice cream sundae**, known simply as a **sundae**, is an American dessert traditionally consisting of scoops of ice cream topped with sweet sauce, whipped cream, chopped nuts, and a maraschino cherry. Developed in the late nineteenth century during the [[industrial-revolution]] in the [[united-states]], the confection was popularized when local blue laws restricted the commercial sale of carbonated fountain soda water on the Christian Sabbath. By replacing effervescent soda with rich syrup, soda fountain proprietors created an enduring staple of American soda counter culture and culinary Americana.

## you gotta know

- Created specifically to circumvent municipal Sunday blue laws that banned the sale of ice cream sodas, soda fountain operators eliminated the sparkling carbonated water and retained only the ice cream and syrup.
- Multiple Midwestern cities claim to be the birthplace of the confection, with prominent rival historical claims asserted by Two Rivers, Wisconsin; Ithaca, New York; Plainfield, Illinois; and Evanston, Illinois.
- The municipal lore of Two Rivers claims that soda fountain owner Edward Berner first drizzled chocolate syrup over ice cream in 1881 after a customer requested soda syrup without the bubbly carbonated water.
- The spelling was deliberately altered from *Sunday* to *sundae* in soda fountain advertisements out of deference to religious leaders who objected to naming an indulgent sweet confection after the Lord's Day.
- Evanston, Illinois, known during the temperance movement as "Heavenston," outlawed soda sales on religious grounds, leading fountains to offer "Sunday sodas" without the carbonated liquid.
- The banana split, an elaborate triple-scoop variation served in a boat-shaped glass with sliced bananas, crushed pineapple, strawberry sauce, and chocolate syrup, was invented in Latrobe, Pennsylvania, in 1904.
- During the mid-twentieth century, commercial chains such as *Carvel* and *Dairy Queen* popularized soft-serve versions and hot fudge sundaes, while roadside diners transformed the dish into an emblem of mid-century youth culture.

## connections

- [[united-states]] — deeply embedded in twentieth-century commercial food history, drugstore fountains, and popular diner folklore across North America.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — mass production of commercial ice, mechanical refrigeration, and automated bottling allowed soda fountains to flourish nationwide.
- [[christianity]] — Sunday sabbatarian legislation and evangelical temperance campaigns directly spurred the culinary substitution that gave birth to the treat.
- [[new-york-city]] — prominent early documentation of the dessert appeared in upstate New York soda fountain ledgers and Manhattan restaurant menus.
- [[american-literature]] — frequently referenced in nostalgic twentieth-century prose, children's fiction, and period memoirs celebrating soda fountain traditions.
- [[realism]] — depicted in turn-of-the-century depictions of urban leisure, working-class soda counters, and changing social customs.
