## summary

The **potlatch** is a ceremonial feast and gift-giving institution practiced by Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast of North America, including the *Kwakiutl* (*Kwakwaka'wakw*), *Haida*, *Tlingit*, and *Salish* nations. Serving as a crucial mechanism for affirming social hierarchies, validating hereditary titles, redistributing material wealth, and recording legal contracts, the host of the gathering bestows lavish gifts upon guests to validate status. Targeted by colonial authorities who viewed its non-capitalist distribution of property as wasteful and an impediment to assimilation, the ceremony was outlawed by both Canadian and American governments in the late nineteenth century before being formally decriminalized decades later.

## you gotta know

- Core ceremonial practice among Pacific Northwest Indigenous nations such as the *Kwakwaka'wakw*, *Haida*, *Tlingit*, *Nuu-chah-nulth*, and *Tsimshian*.
- The term **potlatch** derives from Chinook Jargon, rooted in the Nuu-chah-nulth word *paɬaˑč*, meaning to make a ceremonial gift or bestow.
- Hosts systematically distributed vast quantities of goods, including cedar canoes, woven *Chilkat* blankets, dentalium shells, foodstuffs, and engraved sheet-copper shields known as coppers.
- High social prestige accrued not to individuals who accumulated and hoarded property, but to leaders who publicly gave away or even destroyed valuable assets to demonstrate unassailable wealth and hereditary authority.
- Accompanied by elaborate theatrical enactments, genealogical recitations, masked dances of secret societies like the *Hamatsa*, and the raising of heraldic totem poles.
- French sociologist *Marcel Mauss* famously examined the ceremony in his classic 1925 treatise *The Gift* (*Essai sur le don*), using it to illustrate reciprocal obligations and total social phenomena.
- Explicitly banned in Canada in 1884 under an amendment to the *Indian Act* and suppressed in the [[united-states]] by the federal government under the *Code of Indian Offenses*, leading to widespread confiscation of ancestral masks and regalia.
- Sustained underground by Indigenous elders until anti-potlatch laws were repealed in the mid-twentieth century, after which communities revived public feasts and repatriated stolen treasures.

## connections

- [[colonialism]] — the overarching imperial policy that criminalized the feast and sought the cultural assimilation of First Nations communities.
- [[united-states]] — the federal government whose Department of the Interior suppressed coastal indigenous ceremonials and banned the gathering.
- [[british-empire]] — the imperial power whose colonial dominion in Canada enacted the 1884 prohibition within the *Indian Act*.
- [[indian-removal]] — the broader ideological campaign of indigenous dispossession and cultural suppression carried out across North America.
- [[anthropology]] — the social science discipline deeply shaped by studies of the ceremony, notably through the fieldwork of *Franz Boas*.
- [[reconstruction-era]] — the contemporary North American historical epoch during which federal reservation policies and aggressive assimilation measures were codified.
