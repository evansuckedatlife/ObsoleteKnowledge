## summary

**Sequoyah** was a Cherokee polymath who single-handedly created a syllabary for the Cherokee language in the early 19th century, enabling his people to achieve widespread literacy overnight. His invention—one of the only writing systems devised by a single individual—profoundly transformed Cherokee society and stands as a triumph of intellectual ingenuity and cultural preservation.

## you gotta know

- Born around 1770 with the English name George Gist (possibly from a trader), though he became known by his Cherokee name Sikwayi, meaning "pig's tail," adopting his mother's family name officially.
- Developed a syllabary of 86 characters representing every sound in the Cherokee language, completing it around 1821 after years of isolated study and observation, without formal linguistic training or knowledge of any other written language.
- The syllabary was adopted across the Cherokee Nation with remarkable speed; within a few years, thousands of Cherokee could read and write their own language, transforming Cherokee society almost overnight.
- His writing system enabled the Cherokee to publish the *Cherokee Phoenix*, the first Native American newspaper in the United States, beginning in 1828 and providing a vehicle for Cherokee political and cultural expression.
- Worked actively to spread literacy among his people and was honored as *Ayokeh*, meaning "the one who caused the letters to stand" or "speaker," and traveled throughout Cherokee territory promoting education.
- The syllabary remains in use today among Cherokee speakers, making Sequoyah's creation one of the most enduring achievements of Native American intellectual history, comparable to the invention of writing systems by entire civilizations.
- His achievement is particularly remarkable because he invented it independently without studying any linguistic theory, relying purely on his keen ear for phonetic patterns in his native language.
