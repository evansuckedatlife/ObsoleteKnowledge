## summary

**Hollandaise**, also known as **hollandaise sauce** or **sauce hollandaise**, is a rich, warm culinary emulsion prepared from egg yolks, clarified butter, and an acid such as fresh lemon juice or white wine vinegar. Originating in [[france]] during the seventeenth century under the earlier name *sauce Isigny*, it was codified by *Auguste Escoffier* as one of the essential mother sauces of classical French cuisine. Renowned for its silky texture, bright citrus acidity, and demanding technical execution, it remains a standard benchmark of culinary skill.

## you gotta know

- Codified by culinary pioneer *Auguste Escoffier* in *Le Guide Culinaire* as one of the five foundational French mother sauces, alongside *béchamel*, *velouté*, *espagnole*, and *sauce tomate*.
- Prepared as a delicate warm emulsion by gently whisking egg yolks over indirect heat in a double boiler or *bain-marie* while slowly drizzling in melted clarified butter.
- Relies chemically on the phospholipid lecithin naturally present within egg yolks to act as an amphiphilic emulsifier, stabilizing microscopic butterfat globules suspended within water.
- Prone to breaking or curdling when subjected to excessive thermal heat, requiring cooks to restore split emulsions by vigorously whisking in small increments of warm water or a fresh beaten yolk.
- Known originally in historical French texts as *sauce Isigny*, named after the Normandy commune of Isigny-sur-Mer celebrated for its premium dairy cream and cultured butter.
- Forms the crowning element of eggs Benedict, where it is layered over warm poached eggs, Canadian bacon, and toasted English muffins.
- Serves as the base for renowned daughter sauces, including *béarnaise* flavored with tarragon, chervil, and shallots, *sauce mousseline* folded with whipped cream, and *sauce maltaise* perfumed with blood orange zest.
