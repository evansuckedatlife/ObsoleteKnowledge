## summary

**Church History** is the study of how [[christianity]]'s institutional structures, doctrines, and practices evolved across 2,000 years. From small persecuted communities to the medieval Catholic establishment, then splintering through the [[protestant-reformation]], the Church has shaped politics, art, science, and culture across the Western world. Understanding its development is essential to grasping European and American history.

## you gotta know

- The early Church faced Roman persecution (including martyrdoms) until [[constantine]] legalized Christianity in the 4th century; the Council of Nicaea (325 CE) then standardized doctrine.
- The rise of the papacy and the Eastern Orthodox schism (1054) divided Christian leadership geographically and theologically; the Pope claimed supremacy in the West while the Orthodox Patriarch was one voice among many.
- Medieval monasteries preserved learning, developed the [[gregorian-calendar]], and served as centers of manuscript copying, education, and theological innovation during Europe's "Dark Ages."
- [[thomas-aquinas]] and medieval scholasticism married Aristotelian philosophy with Christian theology, creating an intellectual synthesis that dominated European universities.
- The [[protestant-reformation]] (16th century) fractured Western Christianity into competing confessions: Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, and Catholic; [[martin-luther]]'s challenge to papal authority unleashed centuries of religious conflict.
- The Catholic Counter-Reformation responded with internal reforms (Council of Trent), renewed missionary zeal (Jesuits), and the [[spanish-inquisition]] as an enforcement mechanism.
- Church authority clashed with rising state power during the Enlightenment, reshaping the relationship between religion and governance; Enlightenment thinkers questioned clerical privilege and scriptural authority.
- Industrial-era secularization and the rise of biblical criticism (source analysis, historical-critical method) challenged traditional Church authority and sparked modernist versus fundamentalist debates that persist today.
