## summary

A **sentence fragment** is a grammatical construction punctuated as an independent sentence that fails to contain a complete main clause, typically because it lacks a grammatical subject, lacks a finite verb, or consists entirely of a dependent clause. In formal prescriptive grammar and rhetoric, fragments are traditionally treated as compositional errors that break syntactic coherence. Across creative writing and literary history, however, intentional fragments serve as powerful stylistic devices used to convey sensory immediacy, interior monologue, rhetorical urgency, or disjointed consciousness.

## you gotta know

- Punctuated with a capital letter and terminal punctuation, such as a period, question mark, or exclamation point, despite missing an essential clause component.
- Most commonly created when a subordinate clause introduced by a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun is detached from the independent clause it modifies.
- Frequently takes the form of an isolated participial phrase, prepositional phrase, infinitive phrase, or appositive noun phrase without a finite predicate.
- Corrected in standard formal editing either by attaching the isolated structure to an adjacent independent clause or by inserting the necessary subject and finite verb.
- Exploited extensively in literary [[modernism]] by authors seeking to simulate the natural pacing of human thought and psychological fragmentation.
- Functions in dramatic dialogue and journalism as a deliberate rhetorical device to heighten tension, emphasize an isolated observation, or mimic vernacular speech patterns.
- Distinguished in generative [[linguistics]] from ellipsis, where missing syntactic arguments are grammatically licensed and contextually recoverable.

## connections

- [[linguistics]] — structural analysis of clauses, syntax trees, and predication establishes why a clause lacks syntactic completeness.
- [[american-literature]] — twentieth-century authors frequently utilized unconventional sentence structures and fragments to break classical Victorian rhetorical patterns.
- [[modernism]] — the literary movement whose stream-of-consciousness techniques elevated fragmented prose to express alienation and mental rupture.
- [[realism]] — artistic philosophy whose adherents employed conversational fragments to capture authentic colloquial speech.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — dramatic poetry often incorporates brief elliptical utterances and broken clauses to express heightened emotional breakdown.
- [[tragedy]] — classical and modern theatrical works rely on fragmented dialogue to depict characters undergoing overwhelming grief or sudden shock.
