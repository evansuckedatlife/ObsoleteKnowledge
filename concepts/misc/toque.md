## summary

A **toque** is a close-fitting, brimless knit winter cap closely associated with Canadian cultural identity, as well as a historical term for specific professional and ceremonial headwear. Originating in Canadian French from traditional winter head coverings worn by early fur traders and *voyageurs*, the word has become one of the most prominent lexical markers distinguishing Canadian English from other dialects. Historically, the word also refers to the tall, pleated white pleat-crested hat worn by professional chefs around the world.

## you gotta know

- Serves in Canadian English as the ubiquitous term for a knitted wool or synthetic winter stocking cap, corresponding to what Americans typically call a *beanie* or *watch cap*.
- Derives etymologically from the Canadian French term *tuque*, which trace back to the Old French word *toque*, borrowed from Spanish or Arabic denoting a round, brimless head covering.
- Traditionally worn in red wool by early French Canadian *habitants*, fur traders, and *coureurs des bois* navigating freezing northern waterways during the colonial fur trade.
- Adopted as a symbol of rebellion during the 1837 *Patriotes' Rebellion* in Lower Canada, where blue and red wool caps represented resistance against British colonial authority.
- In professional culinary gastronomy, the *toque blanche* refers to the tall, vertically pleated white chef's hat popularized by legendary French chefs *Marie-Antoine Carême* and *Auguste Escoffier*.
- Culinary tradition holds that the hundred pleats on a traditional chef's hat symbolize the one hundred different techniques a master chef knows to cook an egg.
- Embraced as an iconic pop-culture marker of Canadian national identity, famously donned by comedy characters *Bob and Doug McKenzie* and celebrated in satirical outlets like [[the-beaverton]].
