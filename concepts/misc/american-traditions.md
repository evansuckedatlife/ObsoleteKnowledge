## summary

**American New Year traditions** encompass the celebratory customs observed across the [[united-states]] on New Year's Eve and New Year's Day, combining civic spectacles, lucky foods, broadcast countdowns, and communal gatherings. Evolving from European immigrant folklore and twentieth-century mass media, these rituals welcome the calendar year with public revelry, athletic exhibitions, and domestic feasts. They represent a distinct cultural framework defined by commercial entertainment, regional diversity, and shared hopes for annual renewal.

## you gotta know

- The annual [[times-square-ball-drop]] in [[new-york-city]] is the premier national countdown ritual, established on December 31, 1907, by *The New York Times* publisher *Adolph Ochs* to replace fireworks displays with an illuminated sphere lowered down a flagpole.
- Singing the eighteenth-century Scots ballad *"Auld Lang Syne"*, penned by *Robert Burns*, became an essential stroke-of-midnight tradition popularized nationwide by Canadian-American bandleader *Guy Lombardo* during his annual radio and television broadcasts from 1929 through 1976.
- National holiday television shifted toward contemporary pop music in 1972 with the premiere of *Dick Clark's New Year's Rockin' Eve*, which introduced live concert performances alongside coverage of the Times Square crowd to engage younger generations of viewers.
- Making personal New Year's resolutions and sharing a midnight kiss are ubiquitous interpersonal customs, reflecting historic traditions of moral renewal and superstitions that greeting loved ones at midnight ensures affection and good fortune throughout the coming year.
- Civic pageantry and college football dominate New Year's Day, anchored by the *Tournament of Roses Parade* in Pasadena, California, first staged in 1890, and the *Rose Bowl* game, which inaugurated the American tradition of holiday bowl games.
- Regional culinary superstitions guide New Year's Day meals, especially the Southern custom of eating *Hoppin' John* with collard greens and cornbread for financial prosperity, and the Pennsylvania Dutch practice of consuming pork and sauerkraut because pigs root forward into the future.
- Dozens of American towns stage playful novelty drops inspired by Manhattan's ceremony, lowering idiosyncratic regional emblems at midnight such as a peach in Atlanta, an acorn in Raleigh, a musical note in Nashville, and a marshmallow Peep in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania.
- Regional festivities also feature Philadelphia's historic *Mummers Parade*, an elaborate costumed folk procession established in 1901, as well as nationwide *Polar Bear Plunges*, where participants plunge into freezing winter waters to raise money for charitable causes.

## connections

- [[times-square-ball-drop]] — the centerpiece national countdown ritual staged in Manhattan since 1907.
- [[new-york-city]] — the primary urban center for mass holiday gatherings and nationwide broadcasts.
- [[united-states]] — the federal nation whose regional folkways and mass media shaped these holiday rituals.
- [[shogatsu]] — the traditional Japanese New Year celebration marked by shrine visits and symbolic meals.
- [[chinese-new-year]] — the major lunisolar holiday featuring dragon dances, family banquets, and red envelopes.
- [[tet]] — the Vietnamese lunar new year festival celebrating ancestral renewal and spring.
- [[nowruz]] — the Persian New Year festival celebrating the vernal equinox and rebirth.
