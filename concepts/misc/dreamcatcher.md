## summary

A **dreamcatcher** is a traditional handmade protective charm originating among the *Ojibwe* (*Chippewa*) people of North America, historically constructed with a willow hoop and woven nettle cord web. Traditionally hung above the cradles of sleeping infants to snare nightmares while allowing good dreams to pass through, it became a pan-Indian symbol during the *American Indian Movement* of the 1960s and 1970s. In contemporary ethnography and material culture, it serves as a central case study in Indigenous sacred craft, identity politics, and commercial cultural appropriation.

## you gotta know

- Known in the *Ojibwe* language as *asabikeshiinh*, the inanimate form of the word for *spider*, honoring the spiritual protector *Asibikaashi* (*Spider Woman*).
- Created according to legend because the *Spider Woman* could no longer watch over every child in person as the *Ojibwe* nation dispersed to the four corners of the continent.
- Traditionally fabricated from a small hoop of red willow (*cornus sericea*) bent into a circular or teardrop shape, strung with sinew or nettle-stalk thread to mimic a spider's web.
- Often decorated with sacred sacred items including a single central feather representing breath or air, and protective beads traditionally made of wood, shell, or bone.
- Designed to function as a spiritual filter: bad dreams lack the ability to navigate the complex woven mesh and remain trapped until the morning sun evaporates them.
- Good dreams pass through the small central hole of the web and gently slide down the pendant feathers onto the sleeping child below.
- Adopted widely by the *Pan-Indian movement* in the mid-twentieth century as a general badge of Native unity, which later precipitated mass commercial production and debate over cultural commodification.
