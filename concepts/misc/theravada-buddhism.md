## summary

**Theravada Buddhism** is the oldest surviving school of Buddhism, emphasizing adherence to the original teachings preserved in the Pali Canon scriptures. Predominant in Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, Cambodia, and Laos, Theravada stresses individual monastic discipline and the attainment of nirvana through one's own effort, contrasting sharply with Mahayana Buddhism's vision of universal enlightenment and bodhisattva ideals.

## you gotta know

- The name means "Way of the Elders," reflecting its claim to preserve the Buddha's earliest and most authentic teachings without later innovations, making it the most conservative of the major Buddhist schools.
- Relies on the *Pali Canon*, the oldest complete Buddhist scriptural collection, written in Pali (an ancient Indian language closely related to Sanskrit) rather than the Sanskrit used in Mahayana traditions.
- Emphasizes the monastic sangha (community) as the primary vehicle for spiritual practice; lay devotion centers on supporting monks and accumulating karmic merit through generosity (*dana*) and ethical conduct.
- The historical Buddha Siddhartha Gautama is revered as the supreme teacher and exemplar, but not worshipped as a deity; enlightenment is achievable by individuals following the Noble Eightfold Path and developing wisdom and moral discipline.
- Particularly strong in Southeast Asia after spreading from India to Sri Lanka in the 3rd century BCE; the missionary efforts of Emperor Ashoka's son Mahinda established Buddhism there and created Sri Lanka's role as the guardian of orthodox Buddhist practice.
- Often termed *Hinayana* ("Small Vehicle") by Mahayana Buddhists, though modern scholars recognize this as a polemical label applied retroactively; Theravada practitioners prefer their own self-designation as representing the authentic tradition.
- Emphasizes the attainment of *nirvana* through individual effort and monastic discipline, contrasting with Mahayana's focus on bodhisattva ideals and the accessibility of enlightenment to all beings.
