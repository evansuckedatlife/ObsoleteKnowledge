## summary

**Brisket** is a primal cut of beef derived from the lower chest or breast musculature of cattle, renowned across culinary traditions for its dense connective tissue and rich flavor. Because these pectoral muscles support significant body weight, the cut is exceptionally tough and requires extended low-temperature cooking, such as smoking, braising, or curing, to convert its collagen into rich gelatin. It occupies a central role in *Central Texas barbecue*, traditional Ashkenazi Jewish holiday cuisine, and New York delicatessen culture as the foundation for pastrami and corned beef.

## you gotta know

- Anatomically comprises the deep and superficial pectoral muscles, subdivided commercially into two distinct components: the lean *"flat"* (pectoralis profundus) and the fatty *"point"* or *"deckle"* (pectoralis superficialis).
- High concentration of collagen and elastic fibers necessitates slow, moist or smoke-assisted heat to breakdown tough fibrous sheaths without desiccating muscle cells.
- Serves as the quintessential centerpiece of *Texas barbecue*, where pitmasters smoke it for twelve to eighteen hours over hardwood logs to produce a dark, peppery crust known as the *"bark"*.
- Central to Ashkenazi Jewish culinary heritage as an inexpensive, kosher-certified cut braised with root vegetables, onions, and broth for holiday meals during *Rosh Hashanah* and *Passover*.
- Underpins classic cured deli meats popularized in [[new-york-city]], serving as the standard cut for corned beef via brine pickling and pastrami via smoking and steaming.
- Features prominently in East Asian braised and soup traditions, such as Cantonese braised beef flank, Vietnamese *phở*, and Korean boiled beef brisket (*yangji*).
- Reflects historical dietary patterns where working-class communities transformed inexpensive, neglected cuts into culinary staples.
