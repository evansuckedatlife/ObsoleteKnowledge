## summary

A **fedora** is a soft felt hat characterized by a medium-to-wide brim, an indented crown with a center crease, and pinched sides, which became an enduring icon of twentieth-century transatlantic fashion. Originating in *[[france]]* from an 1882 stage drama written by *Victorien Sardou* for actress *Sarah Bernhardt*, the hat was initially an avant-garde item of *[[feminine]]* attire and women's rights advocacy. Over subsequent decades, particularly across the *[[united-states]]*, it evolved into standard mid-century menswear, deeply embedded in detective fiction, film noir cinema, and mid-century urban style.

## you gotta know

- Named after Princess Fédora Romanoff, the heroine of the 1882 French melodrama *Fédora* by playwright *Victorien Sardou*, written expressly for superstar actress *Sarah Bernhardt*.
- *Sarah Bernhardt* wore the soft-creased felt hat on stage while playing the title character, transforming the headpiece into a sensation among European and American women.
- In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the hat was adopted by suffragists and modern women as a bold symbol of emancipation and defiance against restrictive *[[feminine]]* dress norms.
- By the 1920s and 1930s, the style transitioned primarily into mainstream menswear, popularized by aristocratic fashion leaders like *Edward VIII*, the Duke of Windsor.
- Became the quintessential headwear of Prohibition-era gangsters, urban journalists, and private investigators in American film noir, worn by actors like *Humphrey Bogart* in *The Maltese Falcon* and *Casablanca*.
- Frequently confused with the narrower-brimmed trilby hat or the rigid homburg, though a true version features a wider, snap-down brim and a softer pinched crease.
- Immortalized in popular cinema by the character of *Indiana Jones*, where costume designer *Deborah Nadoolman Landis* selected a distressed Australian-made model to define the adventurer's silhouette.
- In modern internet and social culture, improper styling and casual over-wearing of trilbies mislabeled with this name have made the accessory a subject of humorous subcultural commentary.

## connections

- [[france]] — country of origin for the theatrical play and dramatist whose production inaugurated the headpiece.
- [[feminine]] — gender category to which the hat originally belonged before shifting into the defining uniform of twentieth-century menswear.
- [[united-states]] — nation where the hat became an ubiquitous urban everyday accessory, industrial manufactured product, and cinematic icon.
- [[belle-epoque]] — historical period when the French theatrical premiere sparked international millinery trends across Western society.
- [[new-york-city]] — center of mid-century American garment manufacturing, advertising, and the urban commuter culture that standardized the hat.
- [[modernism]] — early twentieth-century movement marked by shifting gender aesthetics and the practical simplification of everyday wardrobe items.
- [[realism]] — artistic and dramatic style in late nineteenth-century theater that demanded realistic costuming and character-driven accessories.
