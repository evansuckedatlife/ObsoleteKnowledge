## summary

**Ghosting** is the interpersonal social practice of terminating all communication, contact, and presence with a romantic partner, acquaintance, or prospective employer without prior warning, verbal justification, or emotional closure. Entering popular cultural discourse during the early twenty-first century alongside mobile dating applications, digital messaging services, and ubiquitous smartphone adoption, the behavior represents an evasive relationship dissolution strategy mediated by modern telecommunications. Psychologists and sociologists analyze the phenomenon as an acute form of ostracism that imposes profound ambiguous loss upon recipients, reflecting broader shifts toward interpersonal disposability in consumer-oriented digital environments.

## you gotta know

- Operates through total communicative withdrawal, characterized by ignoring incoming telephone calls, text messages, direct messages, and mutual digital channels rather than articulating an explicit breakup.
- Derives morphologically from the noun *ghost*, drawing upon the metaphor of an individual abruptly vanishing into thin air or existing solely as a silent, non-interactive apparition.
- Proliferated rapidly through location-based mobile dating applications like *Tinder* and *Bumble*, where low social network overlap reduces social accountability for abrupt disengagement.
- Generates psychological distress known in clinical literature as *ambiguous loss*, leaving the discarded individual without confirmation of whether the silence signifies rejection, crisis, or technological malfunction.
- Extended beyond romantic intimacy into the corporate job market, where recruitment agencies cut off applicants and candidates abandon interviews without formal notification.
- Spawned a wider taxonomy of contemporary dating neologisms, including *orbiting* (lurking on social media stories without speaking), *breadcrumbing* (intermittent flirtatious contact), and *submarining* (resurfacing without addressing past silence).
- Examined sociologically as a symptom of commodified dating markets, where infinite choice paradoxically discourages emotional vulnerability and conflict resolution.
