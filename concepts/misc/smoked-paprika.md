## summary

**Smoked paprika**, widely celebrated under the Spanish designation **pimentón**, is a ground spice produced by slowly smoke-drying ripe red chili peppers over smoldering hardwood embers before milling them with traditional stone wheels. Originating in the fertile river valleys of central and western *Spain* following the Columbian exchange during the [[age-of-exploration]], the spice quickly established itself as an indispensable pillar of Iberian cuisine. Protected by European geographical indicators such as **pimentón de la Vera**, it provides an unmistakable earthy aroma, deep mahogany coloration, and complex savory depth to sausages, stews, and sauces across world gastronomy.

## you gotta know

- Denominación de Origen Protegida (*DOP*) status strictly safeguards *Pimentón de la Vera* in the *La Vera* comarca of *Cáceres* within *Extremadura*, where microclimatic humidity requires oak-smoke dehydration rather than open sun-drying.
- Produced almost exclusively using fruit from the species *Capsicum annuum*, predominantly varieties like *Jaranda*, *Jariza*, *Jeromín*, and *Bola*, which were introduced to Europe through the [[age-of-exploration]] and [[colonialism]].
- Dried inside two-story smokehouses called *secaderos* for ten to fifteen days, where farmers burn branches and logs of *encina* (*holm oak*) on lower hearths while peppers rest on upper slatted floors, closely related to artisanal food preservation methods in [[smoking]].
- Categorized into three foundational heat and flavor grades: *dulce* (sweet and mild), *agridulce* (medium pungent or bittersweet), and *picante* (spicy), each calibrated by blending sweeter cultivars with sharper chili selections.
- Traditionally milled using slow-turning granite millstones, which friction-grinds dried pods into fine powder while maintaining low heat to prevent thermal degradation of essential aromatic capsanthin pigments and volatile smoke compounds.
- Serves as the quintessential preservative and coloring agent in authentic Spanish *chorizo*, *sobrasada*, and classic dishes like *pulpo a la gallega* and *patatas bravas*.
- Distinct from Hungarian paprika (*paprika*), which typically relies on thermal hot-air drying without direct wood combustion, resulting in a cleaner, non-smoky sweetness.
