## summary

**Auld Lang Syne** is an 18th-century Scots-language poem and folk song transcribed and adapted by [[scotland]]'s national poet, [[robert-burns]], in 1788. Universally sung at the stroke of midnight to welcome the New Year—most famously during the Scottish celebration of *Hogmanay* and at the [[times-square-ball-drop]] in [[new-york-city]]—it evokes poignant themes of nostalgic reunion, lifelong friendship, and the passage of time. Its worldwide dissemination across the [[united-kingdom]], the [[british-empire]], and modern broadcasting networks has made it one of the most recognized melodies in human history.

## you gotta know

- Globally celebrated as the definitive New Year's Eve anthem, sung at midnight as revellers join hands in an interlocking circle; its American ubiquity was forged by *Guy Lombardo* and his *Royal Canadians* during annual broadcasts from the [[times-square-ball-drop]] in [[new-york-city]].
- The title translates literally from Scots as "old long since," meaning idiomatically "days gone by" or "for old time's sake," establishing a bittersweet mood of remembrance for absent companions and shared hardships.
- Originates culturally as the emotional climax of *Hogmanay*, the Scottish New Year festival, where customs prescribe that singers cross their right arms over their left during the final stanza to clasp the hands of their neighbors.
- Preserved by [[scotland]]'s bard, [[robert-burns]], who sent the verses in 1788 to *James Johnson's* anthology *The Scots Musical Museum*, noting he had transcribed the core text from an elderly balladeer.
- The familiar pentatonic melody sung today was not *Burns's* original choice, but a traditional Scottish dance air appended in 1799 by editor *George Thomson* in *Select Collection of Original Scottish Airs*.
- Lyrical imagery contrasts memories of youth—such as gathering wild daisies (*gowans*) and wading in brooks (*burns*)—with adulthood's separation across wide oceans, resolved by sharing a "cup o' kindness."
- Adapted internationally with localized lyrics, notably serving as the tune for South Korea's early national anthem (*Aegukga*) and in [[japan]] as *Hotaru no Hikari*, a classic graduation and store-closing hymn.
- Sung during the 1914 Christmas Truce of [[world-war-i]], when British and German soldiers shared the melody across No Man's Land, cementing its international association with reconciliation.

## connections

- [[times-square-ball-drop]] — annual New Year's Eve broadcast in [[new-york-city]] that popularized the song as the definitive American midnight anthem through bandleader *Guy Lombardo*.
- [[robert-burns]] — celebrated Scottish poet who collected the traditional oral verses in 1788 and authored the canonical literary text.
- [[scotland]] — cultural homeland where the Scots-language lyrics originated and where the song serves as the emotional peak of *Hogmanay* festivities.
- [[united-kingdom]] — sovereign realm whose civic customs, military regiments, and emigrants carried the Scots ballad to global prominence.
- [[british-empire]] — global colonial network that exported Scottish cultural institutions, diaspora communities, and seasonal rituals across the Commonwealth.
- [[world-war-i]] — conflict whose spontaneous 1914 Christmas Truce saw opposing Scottish, British, and German troops sing the melody together in No Man's Land.
- [[japan]] — East Asian nation that naturalized the melody as *Hotaru no Hikari*, a ubiquitous ceremonial anthem for school graduations and store closings.
