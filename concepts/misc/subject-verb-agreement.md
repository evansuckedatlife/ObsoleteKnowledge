## summary

**subject-verb-agreement**, also termed grammatical concord, is the foundational syntactic rule dictating that a finite verb must match its grammatical [[subject]] in number and person. Across inflected languages, this morphological alignment ensures structural coherence between the central argument of a clause and its predicate. Mastered as a cornerstone of formal grammar and analyzed in theoretical [[linguistics]], it governs both regular syntactic distribution and complex edge cases involving collective, compound, or quantified nominal phrases.

## you gotta know

- Dictates in standard modern English that a third-person singular present-tense [[subject]] takes a verb marked with an *-s* or *-es* suffix, while plural subjects take uninflected verbs.
- Compels compound subjects joined by coordinating *and* to take plural verbs, whereas subjects joined by *or* or *nor* typically trigger the rule of proximity.
- Complicated by intervening prepositional phrases or relative clauses that separate the true syntactic head from the verb, often inducing erroneous attraction to the nearest noun.
- Produces dialectal divergence with collective nouns, where American English prefers singular verbs (*the team is*) while British English frequently allows plural concord (*the team are*).
- Applies semantic rather than formal concord in *synesis*, matching the perceived conceptual number of an entity rather than its strict morphological inflection.
- Demands singular agreement with indefinite pronouns such as *everyone*, *somebody*, and *each*, representing one of the most frequently tested conventions on standardized academic exams.
- Analyzed in generative syntax through probe-goal relationships and Agree operations within functional projections like Tense or Inflection.

## connections

- [[subject]] — the governing nominal argument whose person and number features determine the inflectional morphology of the finite verb.
- [[linguistics]] — the scientific field that examines morphosyntax, feature checking, and agreement paradigms across global language families.
- [[ancient-greece]] — the philosophical milieu in which early grammarians systematized concord between nouns and verbs in highly inflected classical tongues.
- [[enlightenment]] — the intellectual era that codified formal prescriptive grammar handbooks, rigidifying rules of concord for modern vernaculars.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — the dramatist whose early modern works often exhibit historical concord patterns, such as plural nouns appearing with *-s* inflected verbs.
