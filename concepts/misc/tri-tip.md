## summary

The **tri-tip**, also known as **Santa Maria steak** or the **triangle roast**, is a triangular cut of beef harvested from the bottom sirloin subprimal of cattle. Historically relegated to the meat grinder for hamburger or trimmed away for braising stew, it was rescued from obscurity during the 1950s in the Central Coast region of California. Today it serves as the centerpiece of regional grilling culture, celebrated for its rich marbling, lean texture, and versatility when roasted whole or carved across the grain.

## you gotta know

- Anatomically corresponds to the *tensor fasciae latae* muscle located at the bottom of the sirloin subprimal, tapering into a distinct three-cornered wedge that weighs roughly one and a half to two and a half pounds.
- Popularized in the late 1950s by butcher *Bob Schutz* at the Safeway market in Santa Maria, California, who decided to roast the cut on a rotisserie rather than grind it into ground sirloin.
- Anchors the iconic [[santa-maria-style-barbecue]] tradition, where the meat is traditionally coated in a simple dry rub of salt, black pepper, and garlic powder without sweet barbecue sauces.
- Cooked over open iron grates fueled by native California coastal red oak wood (*Quercus agrifolia*), imparting a distinct smoky char without scorching the lean muscle fibers.
- Accompanied traditionally by indigenous local pinquito beans, fresh tomato salsa, toasted sweet French bread dipped in melted butter, and a green salad.
- Must be carved perpendicular to the meat fibers, a challenging culinary task because the muscle grain changes direction midway across the triangular lobe.
- Outside the American West, butcher counters frequently label the unseparated muscle within sirloin tip roasts or market it under terms like Newport steak or triangle steak.
