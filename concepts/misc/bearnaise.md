## summary

**Béarnaise** is a classic warm French emulsion sauce prepared from clarified butter suspended in egg yolks and a fragrant reduction of white wine, vinegar, shallots, chervil, and tarragon. Considered the premier child sauce derived from the mother sauce *Hollandaise*, it is prized in classic culinary arts for its unctuous texture, bright acidity, and anise-like herbal aroma. The sauce was created in the 1830s in Saint-Germain-en-Laye and became a definitive hallmark of nineteenth-century Parisian restaurant dining.

## you gotta know

- Invented in 1836 by chef *Jean-Louis-François Collinet* during the opening celebrations of the restaurant *Le Pavillon Henri IV* in Saint-Germain-en-Laye near Paris.
- Named in honor of King *Henry IV of France*, who was born in the historic province of *Béarn* and was known affectionatey to French history as *le Grand Béarnais*.
- Classified systematically in classic French cuisine by *Auguste Escoffier* as a daughter sauce of *Hollandaise*, substituting lemon juice with an acid reduction flavored by fresh herbs.
- Employs an acid reduction made by gently simmering white wine, white wine vinegar, minced shallots, crushed black peppercorns, and fresh tarragon stems until nearly dry.
- Requires delicate emulsification over a gentle double boiler or water bath (*bain-marie*) to prevent the egg yolks from curdling or the butter from separating.
- Traditionally finished with freshly chopped tarragon leaves and chervil, imparting its characteristic licorice scent and pale green flecks.
- Serves as the quintessential accompaniment for grilled red meats, notably *chateaubriand*, filet mignon, and *tournedos Rossini*, as well as poached salmon.
- Generates famous tertiary derivative sauces, including *sauce Choron* (enriched with concentrated tomato purée) and *sauce Foyot* (thickened with rich meat glaze or *glace de viande*).
