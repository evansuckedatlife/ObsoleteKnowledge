## summary

The **mole** is the base unit of amount of substance in the International System of Units, denoted by the unit symbol *mol*. It bridges the microscopic scale of atoms, ions, and molecules with macroscopic quantities measured in laboratory practice, defining an exact counting number of elementary entities. Formally linked to the fundamental Avogadro constant, the mole remains an indispensable bedrock of stoichiometry, thermodynamics, and physical chemistry.

## you gotta know

- Fixed by the 2019 redefinition of SI base units to contain exactly 6.02214076 times 10 to the 23rd elementary entities.
- Named after the foundational constant associated with Italian scientist *Amedeo Avogadro*, who hypothesized that equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of particles.
- Coined as a chemical term by German chemist *Wilhelm Ostwald* in 1893, derived from the German word *Molekül* (itself stemming from the Latin *moles*, meaning a massive heap or pile).
- Historically defined as the number of atoms contained within precisely twelve grams of pure carbon-12 before the universal transition to fixed fundamental constants.
- Serves as the central concept in chemical stoichiometry, allowing scientists to balance equations and calculate reacting masses, yields, and concentrations.
- Commemorated globally by chemists, educators, and science enthusiasts on *Mole Day*, celebrated annually on October 23 from 6:02 a.m. to 6:02 p.m.
- Solves the practical challenge that individual atoms have masses too minuscule to weigh directly on standard laboratory balances.
