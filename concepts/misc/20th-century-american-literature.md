## summary

**20th-century American literature** encompasses the poetry, drama, and prose produced in the [[united-states]] across an era of intense industrialization, world conflict, and social upheaval. Spanning from the disillusionment of the Lost Generation following [[world-war-i]] through the formal ruptures of [[modernism]] and the urgent testimonies of the [[civil-rights-movement]], it dismantled nineteenth-century conventions to forge distinctively modern idioms. Its works fundamentally redefined world literature by interrogating the American Dream, racial injustice, existential displacement, and postwar mass culture.

## you gotta know

- The *Lost Generation* crystallized after [[world-war-i]], led by expatriate authors in Paris such as *F. Scott Fitzgerald* (*The Great Gatsby*) and *Ernest Hemingway* (*The Sun Also Rises*), who captured pervasive cynicism, moral drift, and the hedonism of the Jazz Age.
- High [[modernism]] fractured narrative structure, syntax, and chronology through the stream-of-consciousness Southern epics of *William Faulkner* (*The Sound and the Fury*) and the cosmopolitan poetic collages of *T. S. Eliot* (*The Waste Land*).
- The *Harlem Renaissance* of the 1920s established [[new-york-city]] as the vibrant capital of Black artistic self-determination, celebrated in the vernacular verse of *Langston Hughes* and the anthropological fiction of *Zora Neale Hurston*.
- The Great Depression fostered uncompromising social realism and documentary protest, epitomized by *John Steinbeck*'s *The Grapes of Wrath* and the leftist stylistic collages of *John Dos Passos*.
- American drama achieved international stature through psychological intensity and tragedy in ordinary life, driven by *Eugene O'Neill*, *Arthur Miller* (*Death of a Salesman*), and *Tennessee Williams* (*A Streetcar Named Desire*).
- Post-[[world-war-ii]] writing fractured into the spontaneous road narratives of the *Beat Generation* (*Jack Kerouac*), Southern Gothic introspection (*Flannery O'Connor*), and the darkly comic metafiction of *Kurt Vonnegut* and *Thomas Pynchon*.
- Writers of the [[civil-rights-movement]] era exposed the psychological and structural reality of American racism through towering masterworks by *Ralph Ellison* (*Invisible Man*), *James Baldwin*, and *Toni Morrison* (*Beloved*).
- Postwar Jewish American fiction flourished through *Saul Bellow*, *Philip Roth*, and *Bernard Malamud*, examining assimilation, urban neurosis, and moral responsibility in a fragmented society.

## connections

- [[modernism]] — the international movement whose radical aesthetic fragmentation and linguistic experiments anchored the century's early masterpieces.
- [[united-states]] — the nation whose geographic expanses, demographic diversity, and evolving identity provided the setting and subject matter.
- [[world-war-i]] — the catastrophic conflict that obliterated Victorian optimism and inspired the disillusioned themes of the Lost Generation.
- [[world-war-ii]] — the global devastation that catalyzed existential skepticism, absurdity, and the rise of postmodern American fiction.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — the social justice struggle that catalyzed profound literary reckonings with racial segregation, identity, and liberation.
- [[cold-war]] — the geopolitical standoff whose atmosphere of paranoia and ideological conformity prompted mid-century satire and dystopian allegory.
- [[existentialism]] — the philosophical framework adapted by postwar American novelists and dramatists exploring alienation and individual responsibility.
- [[new-york-city]] — the primary literary metropolis that hosted the Harlem Renaissance, Greenwich Village bohemia, and leading literary periodicals.
