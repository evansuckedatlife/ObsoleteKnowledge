## summary

A **postscript**, universally abbreviated as **P.S.**, is an explanatory sentence, supplementary paragraph, or afterthought appended to a letter or text after the signature and closing salutation. Originating from the Latin *postscriptum*, literally meaning written after, the convention arose out of mechanical necessity in handwritten epistolary culture to avoid rewriting entire sheets of paper when new thoughts occurred. In literary traditions, marketing rhetoric, and everyday correspondence, the device functions both as a spontaneous conversational footnote and as a deliberate stylistic ploy designed to command the highest reader attention.

## you gotta know

- Derived etymologically from the Latin neuter past participle phrase *post scriptum*, translating directly to that which has been written after.
- Arisen fundamentally from manuscript culture, where letters penned in indelible ink could not be edited or rearranged without laboriously scraping parchment or re-penning the entire document.
- Multiple additions are conventionally denoted with subsequent abbreviations such as *P.P.S.* (*post-postscriptum*) or *P.P.P.S.* to signal cascading supplementary layers.
- In direct-mail advertising and copywriting psychology, tests indicate that the **postscript** is often the second most widely read element of a correspondence after the initial headline.
- Extensively employed as an epistolary framing device in eighteenth-century and nineteenth-century novels, such as works by *Samuel Richardson* and *Jane Austen*, to convey secret intimacy or abrupt revelations.
- In computer science, *PostScript* (capitalized with an internal capital) refers to the influential page description language developed by *John Warnock* and *Charles Geschke* at Adobe Systems in 1982.
- A natural companion to space-limited correspondence formats like pictorial [[postcards]], where authors frequently squeezed a hurried final line into margins and borders.
- Scribes and scholars throughout the Renaissance and early modern Europe used formal Latin postscripts to update urgent diplomatic dispatches before sealing with wax.
