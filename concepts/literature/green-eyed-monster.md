## summary

The **green-eyed monster** is an iconic literary idiom and figurative personification of jealousy, coined by *William Shakespeare* in the English Renaissance masterpiece *Othello*. Uttered by the villain *Iago* as psychological manipulation, the metaphor captures the self-consuming, irrational nature of sexual suspicion that feeds upon the very psyche hosting it. Beyond its immediate theatrical context, the phrase has entered the English lexicon as the standard cultural embodiment of toxic envy, romantic insecurity, and destructive obsession.

## you gotta know

- Coined in Act III, Scene 3 of *William Shakespeare's* tragedy *Othello*, when the ensign *Iago* issues a duplicitous warning to his general: "O, beware, my lord, of jealousy; / It is the green-eyed monster which doth mock / The meat it feeds on."
- Predated by Shakespeare's earlier allusion in *The Merchant of Venice*, where *Portia* refers to "green-eyed jealousy" while anxiously watching *Bassanio* choose the leaden casket.
- Relies on the Renaissance humoral theory of medicine, which associated the color green and excess yellow or green bile (*choler*) with sickness, nausea, malice, and bilious passions.
- Functions as the central thematic engine of *Othello*, in which *Iago* systematically infects the Moorish general's mind with fabricated suspicions of his wife *Desdemona's* infidelity with *Michael Cassio*.
- Illustrated in the text through a stolen prop, *Desdemona's* strawberry-spotted handkerchief, which *Iago* transforms into "ocular proof" to drive Othello into murderous madness.
- Personified as a predatory feline creature that toys with, torments, and ultimately destroys its prey—representing the tragic victim who tortures himself with imagined injuries.
- Frequently linked to related Shakespearean depictions of catastrophic domestic jealousy, such as *King Leontes* in *The Winter's Tale* and *Master Ford* in *The Merry Wives of Windsor*.
