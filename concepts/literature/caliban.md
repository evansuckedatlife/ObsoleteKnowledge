## summary

**Caliban** is the deformed, enslaved indigenous inhabitant of an unnamed Mediterranean island in *William Shakespeare*'s late romance *The Tempest*, composed around 1610–1611 during the *English Renaissance*. The son of the banished Algerian witch *Sycorax*, he is subjugated by the exiled sorcerer *Prospero*, who forces him into manual servitude after an alleged attempt to rape *Miranda*. In modern scholarship and performance, he is renowned as a foundational archetype of colonial subjugation, resistance, and linguistic dispossession, embodying the complex tensions between native sovereignty and imperial dominance.

## you gotta know

- Famous for his bitter retort regarding language acquisition, declaring to *Prospero* and *Miranda* that "You taught me language, and my profit on't / Is, I know how to curse," encapsulating his resentment of colonial acculturation.
- Plotted a drunken rebellion to murder *Prospero* by recruiting the shipwrecked jester *Trinculo* and the drunken butler *Stephano*, whom he reveres as a god dropped from the moon after tasting his celestial liquor.
- Delivers one of the most lyrically tender speeches in the play, "Be not afeard; the isle is full of noises," revealing a deep aesthetic sensitivity to the island's natural music that contrasts sharply with his brutish exterior.
- Born on the island to the banished Algerian sorceress *Sycorax*, who had imprisoned the airy spirit *Ariel* in a cloven pine before dying, leaving *Caliban* claiming original hereditary sovereignty over the entire territory and revering his mother's god *Setebos*.
- Initially welcomed *Prospero* and *Miranda* by showing them the island's natural resources, fresh springs, and fertile patches, but was reduced to harsh penal bondage following an attempt to violate *Miranda* in order to people the isle with his progeny.
- Instructs his co-conspirators that *Prospero* is completely powerless without his library of magical texts, urging them to seize and burn his books before driving a stake through his brain.
- Named with an anagram of "cannibal" derived from the Spanish *caníbal*, his character reflects the influence of *Michel de Montaigne*'s essay "Of Cannibals" and served as a foundational symbol of anti-colonial resistance in twentieth-century literature, notably in *Aimé Césaire*'s drama *Une Tempête*.
- Chased and tormented alongside his fellow conspirators by spirit hounds summoned by *Prospero* and *Ariel*, he ultimately repents his foolish worship of the drunkards, promising to "seek for grace" while *Prospero* cryptically acknowledges his own complicity with the line, "this thing of darkness I acknowledge mine."
