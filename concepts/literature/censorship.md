## summary

**Censorship** is the systematic suppression, prohibition, or expurgation of speech, public communication, literature, and artistic expression deemed politically subversive, morally harmful, blasphemous, or dangerous by state authorities, religious bodies, or editorial gatekeepers. From ancient legal decrees and ecclesiastical bans to modern authoritarian state apparatuses, it has fundamentally shaped the composition, publication, and circulation of the global literary canon. Comprehending its mechanisms is essential for analyzing how writers across centuries developed subversive devices, including allegory, satire, pseudonymity, and clandestine distribution networks, to survive institutional repression.

## you gotta know

- *John Milton*'s 1644 prose tract *Areopagitica* established the foundational philosophical polemic against state pre-licensing of the press, arguing that truth will naturally triumph over falsehood in open discourse.
- The Catholic Church enforced the *Index Librorum Prohibitorum* from 1559 until 1966, systematically proscribing texts judged heretical, including works by *Descartes*, *Rousseau*, and *Voltaire*.
- French *Ancien Régime* licensing required the *approbation du censeur*, forcing Enlightenment *philosophes* to print masterworks like [[candide]] anonymously in Geneva or Holland and smuggle them back across French borders.
- Mid-nineteenth-century French prosecutors famously placed *Gustave Flaubert*'s *Madame Bovary* and *Charles Baudelaire*'s *Les Fleurs du mal* on trial in 1857 under charges of outraging public and religious morality.
- In England, theatrical performances were strictly regulated by the *Lord Chamberlain*'s office under the *Theatres Act 1737* and *1843*, requiring every new stage script to receive official moral clearance until abolition in 1968.
- The United States codified federal postal prohibitions through the 1873 *Comstock Act*, which outlawed mailing obscene material and suppressed literature addressing sexuality, birth control, and anatomy.
- Twentieth-century landmark legal trials over *James Joyce*'s *Ulysses* and *D. H. Lawrence*'s *Lady Chatterley's Lover* dismantled archaic obscenity doctrines and established constitutional protections for works of genuine artistic merit.
- Totalitarian regimes institutionalized comprehensive pre-publication state control, such as *Glavlit* in the [[soviet-union]], prompting widespread dissident *samizdat* and *tamizdat* self-publishing undergrounds.

## connections

- [[candide]] — famously banned and burned in Geneva and Paris within weeks of its clandestine publication in 1759.
- [[captain-nemo]] — altered through editorial intervention when publisher *Pierre-Jules Hetzel* forbade depicting the protagonist as a Polish rebel against Russia.
- [[united-states-constitution]] — guarantees freedom of speech and the press through the First Amendment, establishing key legal battles over obscenity.
- [[enlightenment]] — stimulated groundbreaking philosophical defenses of free inquiry, liberty of conscience, and tolerance by thinkers across Europe.
- [[soviet-union]] — maintained the *Glavlit* bureaucracy, leading to the repression of writers such as *Mandelstam*, *Pasternak*, and *Solzhenitsyn*.
- [[modernism]] — challenged conventional Victorian taboos, provoking bitter legal censorship battles in Britain and America over modernist prose and poetry.
- [[carson-mccullers]] — navigated mid-twentieth-century American moral and social scrutiny regarding the frank depiction of queer desire and racial inequality.
