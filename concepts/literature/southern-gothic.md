## summary

**Southern Gothic** is a mode of American fiction that transplants the machinery of European Gothic — decay, madness, buried secrets, the grotesque — into the American South, using it to expose the region's inheritance of slavery, poverty, and religious intensity. Emerging in the 1920s and dominant through mid-century, it replaced the Gothic's haunted castles with collapsing plantations and small towns, and its supernatural terrors with social and psychological ones. Its central subject is a society living amid the ruins of its own myth, unable to disown a past it cannot admit to. Practised by *William Faulkner*, *Flannery O'Connor*, *Tennessee Williams*, *Carson McCullers* and *Truman Capote*, it remains the most influential regional idiom in American letters.

## you gotta know

- Takes the conventions of *Gothic literature* — ruin, isolation, entrapment, the return of the repressed — and relocates them from the European past to the contemporary American South.
- Its defining device is the *grotesque*: physically or mentally deformed characters whose distortion exposes a moral or spiritual condition rather than merely shocking the reader.
- *William Faulkner* built the mode's deepest architecture in *Absalom, Absalom!*, *The Sound and the Fury*, and *A Rose for Emily*, set in the invented Yoknapatawpha County.
- *Flannery O'Connor* is its most concentrated practitioner, using sudden violence as the occasion of grace in *A Good Man Is Hard to Find* and *Wise Blood*; her Catholicism makes her grotesques theological rather than merely lurid.
- *Tennessee Williams* carried it onto the stage, most famously with Blanche DuBois in *A Streetcar Named Desire*, a figure destroyed by the gap between genteel pretence and economic reality.
- Recurring materials: the decaying family estate, the imprisoned or hidden relative, the itinerant preacher, the outsider who exposes the community, and an oppressive climate that functions as psychology.
- Its true subject is *history as haunting* — chattel slavery, Reconstruction, and Jim Crow persisting as guilt, silence, and violence rather than as settled past.
- Later writers reworked the mode rather than abandoning it: *Toni Morrison* turned its hauntings toward the interior life of Black Southerners, and *Cormac McCarthy* stripped it toward pure violence.
- Distinguished from *plantation fiction*, which romanticises the antebellum South; Southern Gothic exists largely to refuse that romance.

## connections

- [[gothic-literature]] — the parent tradition whose conventions it inherits and relocates.
- [[william-faulkner]] — its most ambitious practitioner, who gave it a whole invented county.
- [[flannery-o-connor]] — the preeminent short-story writer of the mode, and its most theological.
- [[a-streetcar-named-desire]] — the mode's canonical stage work, and Blanche DuBois its emblematic ruined aristocrat.
- [[american-south]] — the region whose history supplies both the settings and the guilt.
- [[toni-morrison]] — reworked the mode's hauntings from a Black perspective, most fully in *Beloved*.
- [[modernism]] — Faulkner's fractured chronology and interior narration place the mode inside modernist technique.
- [[realism]] — Southern Gothic departs from realism's restraint while keeping its interest in social conditions.
