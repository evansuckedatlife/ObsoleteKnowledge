## summary

The **supernatural** in literature denotes forces, beings, or events that defy natural law: ghosts, witches, prophecies, magic, and the intervention of gods or demons in mortal affairs. It operates as both literal plot element (a ghost appears, a curse takes effect) and psychological condition (uncertainty whether what is perceived is real or imagined). Shakespeare's *witches* in *Macbeth* and the *ghost* in *Hamlet* exemplify how the supernatural destabilizes the boundary between the knowable world and the hidden forces that move within it.

## you gotta know

- The supernatural in literature predates realism; it belongs to the oldest storytelling modes (myth, legend, fairy tale) and persists even in realist and modernist fiction as a breach in the rational.
- The literary supernatural differs from religious belief: it is *ambiguous*, allowing for both literal and psychological interpretation, thus generating suspense and psychological depth.
- Ghosts are the supernatural's most versatile figure: they represent unfinished business, guilt, the past erupting into the present, the return of the repressed.
- Prophecy—knowing the future with certainty—is a form of supernatural knowledge that complicates human agency; *Macbeth*'s witches offer prophecy that seems to guarantee outcome but actually courts disaster.
- The supernatural often marks boundaries or thresholds: the supernatural occurs at midnight, on crossroads, at liminal times and places where normal rules break down.
- Gothic literature and horror depend on the supernatural for their effects, but the supernatural also appears in comedy, tragedy, and psychological fiction, always as a destabilizing force.
