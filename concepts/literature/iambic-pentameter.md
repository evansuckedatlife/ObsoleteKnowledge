## summary

**Iambic pentameter** is a metrical pattern in English poetry consisting of five iambic feet (an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed syllable) per line, creating a ten-syllable rhythm. It became the standard metre of English dramatic verse and remained dominant in lyric poetry from the *Renaissance* through the 19th century. Because of its natural alignment with English speech patterns, it became the closest thing English has to a "default" poetic line.

## you gotta know

- Each *iamb* is two syllables: unstressed-STRESSED (da-DUM), and pentameter means five feet per line, yielding ten syllables.
- The pattern is most recognisable in *Shakespeare*'s plays and sonnets: *"Shall I comPARE thee TO a SUMmer's DAY?"*
- The regular rhythm mirrors natural English speech more closely than most metres, making iambic pentameter feel both formal and conversational.
- Variations and substitutions—trochaic feet, spondees—within the pattern allow poets to avoid monotony and emphasise particular words.
- *Heroic couplets* (pairs of rhymed iambic pentameter lines) were the dominant form for narrative poetry, especially in 17th and 18th-century satire and epic; *Pope's* *Essay on Criticism* exemplifies the form's satiric power.
- *Sonnets* are defined partly by their use of iambic pentameter, which creates the tonal elevation and formal beauty the form demands.
- *Milton's* *Paradise Lost* uses iambic pentameter in unrhymed form (blank verse), establishing it as the metre for philosophical and narrative weight.
- The *pentameter* line's ten syllables map imperfectly onto English stress patterns, creating productive tension between metrical regularity and natural speech rhythm; this flexibility is part of the form's power.
- In performance, iambic pentameter creates a hypnotic regularity that makes departures from the norm resonate; a *spondee* or *trochee* in place of an expected iamb draws the ear's attention to a key word.
