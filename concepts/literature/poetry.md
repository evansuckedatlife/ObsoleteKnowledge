# Poetry

## summary

**Poetry** is the literary art form that uses language's sonic, formal, and semantic properties—*rhythm*, *rhyme*, *meter*, *imagery*, *metaphor*—to achieve concentrated expression of emotion, thought, or vision. Older than prose, poetry stands at the origin of literature itself, linking sacred ritual, oral tradition, and aesthetic refinement. Across cultures and centuries, it remains the form most intimate to the human voice and most distant from ordinary speech.

## you gotta know

- Predates written language; oral poets—*Homer*, bards in Celtic and Germanic traditions, griots in African cultures—preserved history, myth, and value through metrical verse that aided memory and performance.
- Uses *form* as intrinsic to meaning: meter, rhyme, stanza, line break, and typography shape how language sounds and is understood; *free verse*, which abandons regular meter, emerged in the 20th century but retains line-based form.
- *Metaphor* and *image* compress meaning: poetry achieves through suggestion and compression what prose achieves through explanation; "the heart is a drum" contains more than "the heart beats like a drum."
- Encompasses genres: *lyric* (first-person emotion or meditation), *epic* (grand narrative), *dramatic* (theater in verse), *satire*, *elegy*; modern poetry often blurs these boundaries.
- Modern poetry (20th–21st centuries) saw radical formal innovation: *T.S. Eliot* and the *Modernists* fragmented narrative; *Langston Hughes* integrated African-American vernacular and jazz rhythms; *Beat poets* rejected formality for spontaneous breath.
- Poetry is the form most shaped by individual *voice*—the distinctive music and perspective of each poet; reading poetry is partly decoding technique, partly inhabiting the poet's singular consciousness.
- Often marginalized in the modern world as elite or irrelevant; yet poetry persists as a form of *resistance*, *identity*, and *pleasure*, from protest movements to hip-hop to contemporary slam and performance poetry.

## connections

- [[t-s-eliot]] — modernist poet whose *Waste Land* redefined poetic form and consciousness; a central figure in 20th-century poetry.
- [[langston-hughes]] — African-American poet who grounded poetry in vernacular speech and rhythm, drawing on jazz and blues; central to Harlem Renaissance.
- [[gwendolyn-brooks]] — American poet of extraordinary technical mastery and social conscience; winner of the Pulitzer Prize.
- [[verse]] — the formal substrate of poetry; the arrangement of words according to metrical or accentual patterns.
- [[lyric]] — the dominant form of modern poetry; personal, emotional, imagistic, often brief.
- [[metaphor]] — the central device of poetic language; the comparison that reveals unexpected likeness.
- [[homer]] — the originary poet of Western tradition; his epics established poetry as a vehicle for human meaning.
- [[romanticism]] — movement that elevated poetry and the poet as supreme artistic forms; Romantic poets defined modern lyric.
