## summary

**Henry James** (1843–1916) was an American-born writer who spent most of his adult life in Europe and became one of modernism's greatest novelists and theorists of narrative form. His obsessive exploration of consciousness, moral ambiguity, and the psychology of social encounter redefined what the novel could achieve. *The Turn of the Screw*, his novella of psychological horror, remains unsettling precisely because its ambiguities resist resolution.

## you gotta know

- Born in New York to a prosperous family; brother *William* was a pioneering psychologist whose ideas deeply influenced *Henry's* literary practice; the brothers' dialogue shaped modern thought about consciousness and perception.
- Expatriated to England and continental Europe in his twenties, eventually settling in London and becoming a British citizen late in life—always positioned as cultural outsider and observer; this liminality became his artistic vantage point.
- Pioneered the technique of *free indirect discourse*, rendering thought and perception from a character's perspective while maintaining narrative distance and irony; this allowed unprecedented intimacy with consciousness while preserving authorial subtlety.
- *The Portrait of a Lady* (1881) is his masterpiece of psychological realism and moral complexity—a novel about freedom, desire, and the constraints of social expectation; *Isabel Archer* embodies the tragedy of female agency.
- *The Turn of the Screw* (1898) is an ambiguous ghost story whose narrative unreliability has provoked a century of interpretive debate: are the ghosts real or is the governess delusional?; the novella's genius lies in its irresolvable ambiguity.
- Later in his career, *James* experimented with extreme narrative compression and complexity; his final novels (*The Golden Bowl*, 1904) are dense, labyrinthine explorations of consciousness and moral relationships; the prose becomes increasingly difficult and rewarding.
- His critical essays and prefaces to his collected works were foundational to literary theory—he theorised narrative technique with unprecedented sophistication; his prefaces remain essential to understanding modernist formal innovation.
- *James's* influence on the novel was profound: he demonstrated that psychology, subtlety, and formal complexity could sustain a work's emotional and moral power; he redefined what literature could be.
