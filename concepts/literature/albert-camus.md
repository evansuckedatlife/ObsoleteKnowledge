# Albert Camus

## summary

**Albert Camus** (1913–1960) was an Algerian-French philosopher, novelist, and essayist whose work grapples with the absurdity of human existence—the collision between humanity's hunger for meaning and a universe indifferent to our search. His novel *The Stranger* and his essay *The Myth of Sisyphus* are foundational texts of absurdist philosophy. Though often grouped with existentialists, Camus rejected that label, insisting that existentialism's emphasis on freedom and choice contradicted the fundamental meaninglessness he identified. He remains one of the 20th century's most influential voices on alienation, rebellion, and how to live meaningfully in a senseless world.

## you gotta know

- *The Stranger* (1942) depicts *Meursault*, a man emotionally detached from life who commits a meaningless murder and faces execution with indifference; the novel's sparse prose and Meursault's emotional flatness embody absurdist alienation.
- *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942) is Camus's philosophical essay on the absurd: he uses the myth of Sisyphus (condemned to eternally roll a boulder uphill) as a metaphor for human existence, arguing that we must imagine Sisyphus happy despite the futility.
- The *absurd* for Camus is the collision between human rationality and the world's irrationality; it cannot be overcome by suicide (which Camus calls the central philosophical question) or by false hope (the existentialist leap of faith).
- *The Plague* (1947) is an allegorical novel about a plague-stricken North African city; it explores solidarity, heroism, and the absurdity of suffering in a godless universe.
- Camus was awarded the *Nobel Prize in Literature* in 1957, at age 43, making him one of the youngest laureates; the prize recognized both his literary artistry and his moral philosophy.
- His philosophical and literary work refused clear political alignment: he broke with *Jean-Paul Sartre* over the Soviet Union and was skeptical of all ideologies that claimed to resolve the absurd.

## connections

- [[existentialism]] — often conflated with Camus, though he rejected the label, arguing that existentialism's faith in freedom and choice contradicted his vision of cosmic meaninglessness.
- [[the-stranger]] — his most famous novel, the paradigmatic work of absurdist literature.
- [[franz-kafka]] — a precursor whose work (especially *The Trial*) established the archetype of absurd bureaucracy and meaningless suffering.
- [[jean-paul-sartre]] — a philosophical rival and former ally whose existentialism Camus critiqued; their debate defined mid-20th-century European philosophy.
- [[absurdism]] — Camus is the founding figure of this philosophical movement.
- [[algeria]] — Camus's birthplace and the setting for much of his fiction; the Algerian War's outbreak near the end of his life forced him to confront his political principles.
