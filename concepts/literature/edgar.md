## summary

**Edgar** is the legitimate son of the Earl of Gloucester in Shakespeare's *King Lear*, whose inheritance and sanity are stolen by his illegitimate half-brother *Edmund*. Falsely accused of plotting against his father, Edgar flees and disguises himself as a madman, becoming the instrument of his father's punishment and, eventually, his salvation. His tragic arc — from innocent heir to exile to redemption — mirrors and complicates the play's central themes of family betrayal and the nature of legitimacy.

## you gotta know

- Edgar is the rightful heir to Gloucester's estate until Edmund's ruthless schemes displace him and cunningly turn their father against him through calculated deception.
- Edmund forges a letter in Edgar's hand to convince Gloucester that Edgar is plotting regicide and patricide against his father, leading to Edgar's wrongful banishment and exile from home.
- Disguised as "Poor Tom," a beggar driven mad by desperation and despair, Edgar becomes the instrument through which Gloucester's blindness (both literal and metaphorical moral blindness) is ultimately exposed.
- Gloucester's blinding at the hands of his political tormentors occurs after he belatedly discovers Edmund's terrible treachery, and it is Edgar, still disguised and unrecognized, who saves him from suicidal despair.
- The revelation of Edgar's innocence comes painfully late, after extensive suffering and psychological torment, aligning with the play's persistently bleak vision of justice, redemption, and familial trust.
- Edgar's survival and return to authority at the play's end suggests possible redemption and restoration, though the devastating cost of his suffering fundamentally undercuts any simple or optimistic reading of recovery.
- Edgar's disguise as "Poor Tom" allows him to experience society from the bottom, gaining both humility and moral wisdom that eventually qualify him to rule with justice and understanding.
