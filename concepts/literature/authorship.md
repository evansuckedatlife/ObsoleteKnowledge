## summary

**Authorship** designates the cultural, legal, and intellectual condition of being the recognized creator and origin of a written work. While premodern manuscript cultures and oral traditions often favored anonymity, collective compilation, or divine transmission, modern authorship crystallized alongside the print marketplace and *[[enlightenment]]* legal frameworks in *[[england]]* and *[[france]]*. During the nineteenth century, *[[romanticism]]* elevated the writer to the status of an original, solitary genius, an ideal later interrogated by twentieth-century theorists who deconstructed the biographical creator in favor of textuality and reader reception. Today, authorship encompasses not only legal copyright and creative accountability but also authorial prestige, institutional prizes, and the commercial mechanics of literary celebrity.

## you gotta know

- French literary theorist *Roland Barthes* published the influential 1967 essay *The Death of the Author*, asserting that writing begins where the biographical creator enters their own death, leaving textual meaning to be produced entirely by the reader.
- The *Shakespeare authorship question* has generated extensive fringe controversy since the nineteenth century, with Anti-Stratfordians disputing that *[[william-shakespeare]]* wrote the plays and proposing alternate candidates such as *Edward de Vere* or *Francis Bacon*.
- Philosopher *Michel Foucault* answered Barthes in his 1969 lecture *What Is an Author?*, formulating the "author function" to explain how societies legally bind texts to individual names to control and categorize discourse.
- The *intentional fallacy*, articulated in 1946 by New Critics *W.K. Wimsatt* and *Monroe Beardsley*, argues that an author's private intentions or psychological motivations are neither accessible nor desirable standards for judging literary works.
- Victorian novelists such as *[[charles-dickens]]* established professional authorship as a viable mass-market career, leveraging serialized fiction, international speaking tours, and copyright advocacy to achieve unprecedented public celebrity.
- Women writers routinely adopted male pseudonyms—including *Mary Ann Evans* writing as *George Eliot* and the *Brontë* sisters publishing as the *Bells*—to evade gender prejudice and secure serious critical reception.
- Major literary awards and institutional honors confer vital authorial prestige, dramatically boosting book sales, sustaining career longevity, and securing an author's lasting inclusion in the literary canon.
- The legal codification of individual literary property began with the 1710 *Statute of Anne* in *[[england]]*, which broke the monopoly of the *Stationers' Company* by granting writers statutory copyright for a limited term.

## connections

- [[william-shakespeare]] — dramatist whose life and works sparked enduring attribution debates and questions of authorial identity.
- [[romanticism]] — literary movement that elevated the author to an inspired, solitary genius endowed with original creative vision.
- [[charles-dickens]] — Victorian novelist whose immense fame and commercial success defined modern authorial celebrity and copyright advocacy.
- [[modernism]] — aesthetic movement that challenged Victorian biographical criticism by championing authorial impersonality and formal experimentation.
- [[virginia-woolf]] — modernist essayist who analyzed the material and societal conditions required for women to achieve independent authorship.
- [[enlightenment]] — philosophical era whose emphasis on individual reason and property rights fostered the legal codification of copyright.
- [[american-literature]] — national tradition shaped by nineteenth-century campaigns for international copyright and the institutional prestige of major literary prizes.
