## summary

**Psychology** in literature refers to the deep representation of characters' interior mental and emotional lives—their conflicts, desires, fears, rationalization, and the often-unconscious forces governing their behavior. Modern literature (especially the psychological novel pioneered by *Dostoevsky*) treats the mind as the primary site of action, making thought, feeling, and perception more consequential than plot. This shift transformed fiction from external narrative into an exploration of consciousness itself.

## you gotta know

- **Psychological realism** emerged in 19th-century fiction, especially in Russian novels; *Dostoevsky's* *Crime and Punishment* (1866) was a watershed—the novel became an instrument for mapping the criminal mind.
- Stream of consciousness (pioneered by *James Joyce* in *Ulysses*) renders thought directly without conventional narrative mediation; the reader enters the character's mind unfiltered.
- Unreliable narrators and limited perspective: if the protagonist's perception is warped by trauma, illness, or delusion, the reader must reconstruct truth from fragments and distortions.
- Psychology explains motivation beyond conventional morality: characters act from unconscious drives, repressed desires, childhood wounds, or irrational fears that shape behavior beneath conscious choice.
- The psychological novel treats anxiety, depression, paranoia, obsession, and hysteria as profound human experiences worthy of literary attention; mental life *is* the drama.
- Modern detective fiction (*Agatha Christie*, *Conan Doyle*) uses psychological insight to solve crimes—understanding motive through mind-reading and psychological reconstruction.
- Literary psychology informed and was informed by *Sigmund Freud's* theories of the unconscious; 20th-century writers explicitly engaged psychoanalytic concepts (dreams, repression, the id).
