## summary

In Western literature and critical thought, **Nature** evolved from a mechanistic, orderly clockwork during the [[enlightenment]] into a transcendent spiritual sanctuary, aesthetic ideal, and moral teacher during the rise of [[romanticism]]. Spanning British Romantic [[poetry]], European [[gothic-literature]], and the 19th-century American Renaissance, the natural world was reimagined both as an external sublime landscape and as an interior wellspring of human instinct, emotion, and creative genius. In literary history and competitive quiz bowl, the concept is essential for understanding how writers reacted against the urbanization of the [[industrial-revolution]], bridging philosophical shifts from pantheistic reverence to the psychological terrors of the untamed wilderness.

## you gotta know

- The Romantic reconceptualization of the natural world directly countered Enlightenment rationalism and the rapid urbanization of the [[industrial-revolution]], treating pristine landscapes as living, organic unities imbued with the divine rather than mere resources to be cataloged, dissected, or exploited.
- Influenced by philosopher *Edmund Burke* and thinker *Immanuel Kant*, writers celebrated the aesthetic category of the sublime, discovering intense spiritual transcendence and moral awe in terrifying, vast vistas such as precipitous alpine peaks, violent tempests, and fathomless oceans.
- In foundational works like *Lyrical Ballads* and *Lines Composed a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey*, poet *William Wordsworth* established the countryside of [[england]] as a moral guide, nurse, and guardian of the human heart, contrasting pastoral simplicity directly against metropolitan corruption.
- In [[gothic-literature]], author *Mary Shelley* framed forbidding alpine crags and frozen Arctic wastes in *Frankenstein* as external mirrors of psychological torment, while Victorian theorist *John Ruskin* later categorized the poetic projection of human emotion onto inanimate environments as the *pathetic fallacy*.
- The philosophical movement reached its peak in the [[united-states]] through *Ralph Waldo Emerson*, whose influential 1836 manifesto *Nature* urged the reader to become a *transparent eyeball* absorbed into God, directly inspiring *Henry David Thoreau* to conduct his experiment in deliberate living in *Walden*.
- Beyond external scenery, writers explored the innate passions of human psychology as an internal wilderness, championing uncorrupted primal instinct—frequently drawing upon *Jean-Jacques Rousseau*'s idealization of natural innocence—against the rigid, suffocating artifices of polite aristocratic society.
- By the late 19th century, the advent of evolutionary theory and the emergence of literary [[realism]] stripped the environment of its benevolent divinity, reinterpreting the outdoors as an indifferent, pitiless battleground in the Naturalist fiction of *Stephen Crane* and *Jack London*.

## connections

- [[romanticism]] — the dominant aesthetic movement that elevated the natural world as a primary source of spiritual renewal and artistic genius.
- [[enlightenment]] — the rationalist intellectual era whose mechanistic, orderly worldview provoked the Romantic turn toward wilderness and emotional mystery.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — the rapid urban and technological transformation that drove poets and essayists to seek solace and purity in untouched rural landscapes.
- [[gothic-literature]] — the darker genre that inverted pastoral tranquility into hostile wilderness, threatening storms, and brooding, uncanny environments.
- [[american-literature]] — the national tradition profoundly shaped by the frontier, wilderness exploration, and the Transcendentalist philosophy of self-reliance.
- [[poetry]] — the central literary medium through which Romantics captured subjective encounters with landscapes, weather, and sublime beauty.
- [[realism]] — the subsequent movement whose adherence to objective social observation and Naturalist determinism stripped the landscape of its Romantic divinity.
