## summary

**Acting** is the performative art and craft in which an individual embodies a character, conveying dramatic narrative, emotional truth, and psychological depth through speech, gesture, movement, and physical presence. Originating in religious ritual and choral performance in [[ancient-greece]], the practice evolved from stylized declamation into complex psychological investigations across world dramatic traditions. In literary study and theatrical history, acting serves as the vital interpretive medium that translates dramatic text from static verse and dialogue into living cultural experience, profoundly shaping the development of playwrighting from classical [[tragedy]] to theatrical [[realism]].

## you gotta know

- *Thespis* of Icaria is traditionally celebrated as the first individual actor in [[ancient-greece]], stepping out of the dithyrambic chorus in the sixth century BCE to engage in dialogue as a distinct character, thereby giving rise to the term *thespian*.
- *Konstantin Stanislavski* revolutionized modern performance at the Moscow Art Theatre by formulating the "System," an influential approach prioritizing psychological truth, emotional memory, and an actor's "magic if," which laid the foundation for modern dramatic [[realism]].
- *Lee Strasberg* adapted Russian psychological techniques into "Method acting" at the Actors Studio in [[new-york-city]], training generations of American stage and screen performers to draw directly upon affective personal memories.
- During the [[english-renaissance]], acting troupes such as the Lord Chamberlain's Men operated as commercial repertory companies in [[england]], where celebrated performers like *Richard Burbage* originated lead tragic roles written by [[william-shakespeare]].
- *Bertolt Brecht* developed epic theatre and the *Verfremdungseffekt* (defamiliarization or alienation effect) in [[germany]], urging performers to historicize characters and demonstrate actions critically rather than encouraging emotional immersion or passive audience catharsis.
- In classical Athenian drama, actors were exclusively male citizens who performed in open-air amphitheatres wearing expressive linen or cork masks (*prosopa*), allowing a single performer to embody multiple roles across a single [[tragedy]].
- *Denis Diderot* composed the landmark eighteenth-century treatise *Paradoxe sur le comédien* in [[france]], arguing that the greatest performers do not genuinely experience the passions they depict onstage, but instead maintain cool, detached intellectual control over their craft.
- The Roman actor *Quintus Roscius Gallus* achieved unprecedented celebrity and immense wealth in the late republic era of the [[roman-empire]], successfully elevating the cultural prestige of a profession often legally classified among slaves and disenfranchised social classes.

## connections

- [[ancient-greece]] — the historical culture where solo dramatic performance originated through the choral innovations of *Thespis*.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — the dramatist whose works frequently dramatized acting theory, most famously in *[[hamlet]]*'s advice to the players.
- [[tragedy]] — the classical dramatic genre demanding heightened vocal projection, emotional catharsis, and sustained psychological endurance.
- [[realism]] — the nineteenth-century movement that rejected rhetorical declamation in favor of naturalistic psychological embodiment.
- [[silent-film]] — the early visual medium that translated theatrical acting into exaggerated gesture, pantomime, and facial expression.
- [[english-renaissance]] — the theatrical golden age that institutionalized professional repertory companies, public playhouses, and boy players.
- [[new-york-city]] — the urban theatrical center where the Group Theatre and Actors Studio popularized American Method acting.
