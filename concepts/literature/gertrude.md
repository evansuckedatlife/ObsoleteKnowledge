## summary

**Gertrude** is the Queen of Denmark in *William Shakespeare's* *Hamlet*, whose hasty marriage to her murdered husband's brother *Claudius* sets the tragedy in motion. Her ambiguous moral status—whether complicit, knowing, or tragically blind—has made her one of literature's most debated figures. She represents the corruption of the royal court and the collision between maternal bond and feminine agency.

## you gotta know

- A widow who marries her brother-in-law *Claudius* within weeks of her first husband's death, scandalizing the court and offending *Hamlet*.
- Her marriage suggests either her knowledge of the murder, her innocence and manipulation, or her inability to resist political pressure—Shakespeare leaves this deliberately ambiguous.
- Represents the feminine figure trapped between male authority: she obeys her father, then her husband, then her son, never controlling her own agency.
- Drinks poison meant for *Hamlet* in the final duel scene, dying with her son—an accidental death that underscores her tragic entrapment.
- *Hamlet* conflates his disgust at his mother's sexuality with his rage at her betrayal, complicating readings of the play's gender politics.
- Her role as the bridge between *Hamlet* and *Claudius* makes her central to the play's action despite relatively few lines; much of her character is created through what others say about her.

## connections

- [[hamlet]] — her son, whose obsession with her sexuality and betrayal drives the tragedy.
- [[claudius-hamlet]] — her new husband and Hamlet's uncle; their marriage is the act that most offends Hamlet.
- [[ophelia]] — another woman destroyed by the male violence and corruption surrounding the Danish court.
- [[ghost-hamlet]] — the ghost of her first husband, whose murder Hamlet avenges partly out of rage at Gertrude's marriage.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — her creator, who uses her to explore complicity, agency, and the female body as contested ground.
- [[incest]] — the marriage to Claudius invokes this taboo, central to the play's moral decay.
