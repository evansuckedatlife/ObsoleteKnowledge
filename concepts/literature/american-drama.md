## summary

**American Drama** emerged as a major literary force in the 20th century, becoming a vehicle for exploring national identity, social crisis, and moral reckoning. From the economic devastation of the *Great Depression* through the anxieties of the *Cold War*, American playwrights transformed the stage into a space where ordinary citizens confront extraordinary moral and social pressures. The form prioritizes psychological depth and social realism, anchoring universal human conflicts in distinctly American contexts.

## you gotta know

- *Arthur Miller* and *Tennessee Williams* are the canonical figures, establishing American drama's commitment to tragic protagonists drawn from working-class and Southern life.
- *Arthur Miller's* *Death of a Salesman* (1949) redefined tragedy for American audiences: the protagonist is not a king but a traveling salesman, and the tragic flaw is not hubris but misplaced faith in capitalist promise.
- *Tennessee Williams* brought lyrical language and Southern Gothic atmosphere to the stage; *A Streetcar Named Desire* and *The Glass Menagerie* explore desire, memory, and the collision between illusion and brutal reality.
- *Lorraine Hansberry's* *A Raisin in the Sun* (1959) was the first play by an African American woman to open on Broadway, centering Black family life and dignity in the face of systemic racism.
- American drama inherits from *European realism* but filters it through American material: the *frontier*, *class anxiety*, *race*, the *American Dream* as both promise and illusion.
- The form privileges *psychological interiority*: characters speak their emotional truth even when it contradicts plot; *memory plays* and *unreliable perspectives* allow playwrights to render consciousness on stage.
- Unlike *British drama's* emphasis on wit and social manners, American drama confronts moral crises directly, often portraying ordinary people discovering their capacity for dignity or destruction.
