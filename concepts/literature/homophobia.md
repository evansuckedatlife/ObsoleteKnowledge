## summary

**Homophobia** denotes systemic animus, institutional discrimination, and cultural prejudice directed against lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender individuals. First coined in clinical contexts during the late 1960s, the term fundamentally shifted psychological discourse by framing anti-queer hostility as an irrational pathology rather than treating non-heterosexual desire as an illness. In modern theater, fiction, and cultural history, particularly within the [[united-states]], the representation of this prejudice provides a vital framework for analyzing state-sponsored marginalization, theological condemnation, and the resilient emergence of civil liberation movements. By examining how literature dramatizes systemic intolerance, critics illuminate the complex intersections of sexual identity, state power, and human rights.

## you gotta know

- Coined by clinical psychologist *George Weinberg* in 1969 and popularized in his 1972 book *Society and the Healthy Homosexual*, redirecting diagnostic attention toward heterosexual bigotry.
- Intensified dramatically during the early years of the HIV/AIDS epidemic in the 1980s, when political neglect, media scapegoating, and moral hysteria in [[new-york-city]] devastated vulnerable communities.
- Serves as the central political and emotional antagonist in *Tony Kushner's* Pulitzer Prize-winning drama *Angels in America*, in which historical figure *Roy Cohn* embodies closeted self-loathing.
- Portrayed with raw urgency in *Jonathan Larson's* rock musical *Rent*, depicting the systemic marginalization, housing insecurity, and healthcare barriers confronting queer artists in the East Village.
- Explored across mid-century [[american-literature]] through painful depictions of secrecy, domestic entrapment, and internal shame in the works of writers such as *James Baldwin* and *Tennessee Williams*.
- Catalyzed sustained political mobilization and grassroots activism, inspiring the militant resistance of the 1969 *Stonewall Riots* and accelerating the broader [[civil-rights-movement]].
- Theorized by literary critic *Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick* in *Epistemology of the Closet*, demonstrating how anxieties over same-sex desire structure modern Western thought, literature, and culture.
- Legally entrenched through state sodomy statutes and federal bans, which civil rights litigators gradually overturned through federal court challenges grounded in the [[14th-amendment]].

- [[modernism]] — artistic movement that allowed pioneering writers to introduce subtle, coded explorations of same-sex desire amid strict public censorship.
- [[tony-kushner]] — prominent American playwright whose landmark dramatic works directly interrogated institutional prejudice and the politics of the AIDS crisis.
