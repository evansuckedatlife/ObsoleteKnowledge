# King Lear

## summary

**King Lear** is a tragedy by *William Shakespeare*, first performed around 1606, that dramatizes the fall of a British king who divides his kingdom among his daughters based on flattery, only to be betrayed and driven mad. The play explores themes of filial ingratitude, the precariousness of paternal authority, the nature of madness, and the dissolution of social order. It stands alongside *Hamlet* and *Macbeth* as Shakespeare's supreme achievement in tragic form.

## you gotta know

- The plot turns on King *Lear*'s foolish decision to partition his realm among his three daughters in proportion to their professions of love; his elder daughters *Goneril* and *Regan* flatter him extravagantly, while his youngest *Cordelia* refuses to outbid them in flattery and is disinherited.
- After giving away his kingdom, Lear finds himself reduced to a powerless dependent, mistreated by Goneril and Regan, who strip his retinue and deny him shelter, driving him into the heath during a storm where he descends into madness.
- The storm scene becomes both literal and psychological: Lear's outer madness mirrors the chaos of nature and the collapse of his inner world; his nakedness on the heath literalizes his stripping of all rank and dignity.
- The subplot of the *Earl of Gloucester* and his sons *Edmund* and *Edgar* parallels the main plot: Edmund tricks Gloucester into believing Edgar is disloyal, leading Gloucester to have Edgar blinded; blindness (literal and metaphorical) becomes the play's central image.
- *Cordelia*, the wronged youngest daughter, aligns with the French king against her father's usurping sisters, setting up a battle for restoration that fails; the play's ending is catastrophically tragic—Cordelia dies, Lear dies holding her body, and Gloucester dies of grief.
- The play introduces *the Fool*, Lear's jester, who speaks truth in riddles and rhymes, offering penetrating commentary on Lear's folly even as he is powerless to prevent it.
- Shakespeare uses the theme of nature and the natural order (*the great chain of being*) to frame Lear's violation of hierarchy as a cosmic disturbance that unleashes chaos on the moral and physical universe.

## connections

- [[william-shakespeare]] — the playwright; *King Lear* is his most structurally complex and thematically ambitious tragedy.
- [[hamlet]] — another Shakespearean masterpiece; both feature noble protagonists undone by failures of judgment and perception.
- [[macbeth]] — the third of Shakespeare's great tragedies; all three explore the dissolution of authority, the breakdown of natural order, and the ascent of evil.
- [[tragedy]] — *King Lear* redefines tragic form by refusing conventional catharsis; its ending is not merely sad but philosophically bleak.
- [[revenge-tragedy]] — though not a revenge play itself, it inherits tropes of familial betrayal and violent retribution from the revenge-tragedy tradition.
- [[english-renaissance]] — the play exemplifies Renaissance dramaturgy and themes: the dissolution of order, the conflict between nature and civilization, the precariousness of power.
- [[madness]] — central to the play's action; Lear's madness becomes a vehicle for philosophical truth-telling, as he articulates social critique while mad.
