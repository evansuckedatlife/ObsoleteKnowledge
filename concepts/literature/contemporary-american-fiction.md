## summary

**Contemporary American fiction** denotes prose narrative produced in the *United States* from the late twentieth century through the twenty-first century. Marked by the convergence of highbrow literary technique and popular genre traditions, this body of work interrogates national myths, media saturation, consumer capitalism, and multicultural identity. It encompasses both sprawling maximalist encyclopedic epics and tightly controlled minimalist realism, charting American life across the post–*World War II* era into the digital age.

## you gotta know

- Evolved out of mid-century postmodernism, transitioning from ironic metafiction toward what critics termed new sincerity and hysteria realism.
- Prominently features the assimilation and subversion of genre modes such as *detective-fiction*, *science-fiction*, and graphic narrative into mainstream literary prestige.
- Major mid-to-late twentieth-century cornerstones include the suburban anxieties of *John Updike*, the satirical historiography of *Philip Roth*, and the media-drenched paranoia of *Don DeLillo*.
- Spurred monumental maximalist works such as *David Foster Wallace*'s *Infinite Jest* and *Thomas Pynchon*'s *Mason & Dixon*, which diagnose modern information overload and cultural addiction.
- Advanced profound explorations of racial trauma, diaspora, and historic violence through monumental works like *Toni Morrison*'s *Beloved* and *Cormac McCarthy*'s apocalyptic Southern and Western novels.
- Increasingly defined in the twenty-first century by autofiction, post-9/11 sociopolitical trauma, digital culture, and structural experimentation by writers such as *Colson Whitehead*, *Jennifer Egan*, and *Jesmyn Ward*.
