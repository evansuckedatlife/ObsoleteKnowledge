## summary

**19th-century American poetry** encompasses the vibrant verse traditions that emerged across the [[united-states]] as writers broke away from European models to forge a distinct national literature. Rooted in transatlantic [[romanticism]] and philosophical transcendentalism, the era was defined by the revolutionary free verse of *Walt Whitman*, the reclusive, dash-laden metaphysical lyrics of *Emily Dickinson*, and the macabre musicality of [[edgar-allan-poe]]. Ranging from fireside ballads to *Emma Lazarus*'s sonnet celebrating [[american-immigration]], the period transformed poetic form while engaging deeply with democracy, spiritual crisis, and the [[american-civil-war]].

## you gotta know

- *Walt Whitman* revolutionized modern [[poetry]] with his landmark 1855 collection *Leaves of Grass*, abandoning traditional meter and rhyme in favor of expansive, democratic free verse that celebrated the common working person and the American continent in poems like *Song of Myself*.
- *Emily Dickinson* composed nearly eighteen hundred intensely compressed, enigmatic lyric poems in Amherst, Massachusetts, using slant rhyme, ballad meter, and idiosyncratic dashes to probe mortality, faith, nature, and interior consciousness.
- [[edgar-allan-poe]] championed aesthetic unity, hypnotic rhythm, and melancholic musicality in masterworks like *The Raven* and *Annabel Lee*, arguing in *The Philosophy of Composition* that the death of a beautiful woman is the most poetical topic.
- *Emma Lazarus* wrote the famous 1883 Petrarchan sonnet *The New Colossus* for the pedestal of the [[statue-of-liberty]], welcoming the "huddled masses yearning to breathe free" and linking civic identity to [[american-immigration]] and [[jewish-identity]].
- The Fireside Poets, including *Henry Wadsworth Longfellow*, *William Cullen Bryant*, and *John Greenleaf Whittier*, composed accessible, metrically regular verse intended for domestic recitation, establishing enduring national folklore in works like *The Song of Hiawatha*.
- Transcendentalist writers led by *Ralph Waldo Emerson* viewed nature as an emblem of the divine spirit in poems like *Brahma*, while Emerson's influential 1844 essay *The Poet* urged American writers to celebrate their continent's distinct voice.
- The trauma of the [[american-civil-war]] shattered romantic optimism, leading *Herman Melville* to write the disillusioned collection *Battle-Pieces* and Whitman to chronicle wartime hospitals in *Drum-Taps*, marking an aesthetic pivot toward [[realism]].
- African American and abolitionist writers such as *Frances Ellen Watkins Harper* deployed ballad forms and biblical rhetoric to demand emancipation in *Poems on Miscellaneous Subjects*, demonstrating verse's vital role in social agitation.

## connections

- [[american-literature]] — the national canon that 19th-century poets helped establish through original forms and themes.
- [[romanticism]] — the transatlantic movement emphasizing emotion, individualism, and nature that shaped early American verse.
- [[poetry]] — the literary medium revolutionized during this era through free verse and compressed lyricism.
- [[edgar-allan-poe]] — major poet and critic whose theories on aesthetic unity and musicality defined the era.
- [[american-civil-war]] — the cataclysmic conflict that shattered romantic optimism and redirected poetic themes toward grief and realism.
- [[united-states]] — the expanding nation whose democratic ideals, diverse landscapes, and civic identity formed the central subject.
- [[realism]] — the literary movement that supplanted Romantic styles as writers confronted wartime trauma and industrialization.
