## summary

**Emotion** is the felt response—fear, pity, joy, sorrow—that literature cultivates in its audience. From ancient tragedy's design to evoke cathartic release to Romantic poetry's celebration of feeling as truth, emotion has been central to how literature matters morally and aesthetically. It is both the subject writers explore and the mechanism by which readers internalise meaning.

## you gotta know

- Ancient tragic drama was deliberately structured to arouse emotion—particularly fear and pity—which Aristotle theorised as essential to the form's moral purpose; the goal was not mere sensation but a profound reordering of the soul.
- Catharsis, the purification of emotion through witnessing tragedy, was considered the ultimate effect of dramatic art in classical aesthetics; by feeling intensely alongside the protagonist, the audience was believed to purge destructive passions.
- Romantic literature elevated emotion and emotional authenticity as the highest source of truth, replacing Enlightenment rationalism with feeling as the measure of reality; poets like Wordsworth made personal emotional experience the very ground of truth-telling.
- Sentimentalism emerged as both aesthetic movement and social practice in the 18th century, celebrating tearfulness and emotional excess as marks of moral sensitivity; the ability to *feel* became equated with virtue and depth of soul.
- Stream-of-consciousness narrative technique uses emotional texture and associative psychology to render interiority—emotion becomes the very form of the prose; writers like *Virginia Woolf* rendered consciousness as continuous emotional resonance rather than logical thought.
- Modernist writers often distrust emotion as false or kitsch, yet their fragmented styles paradoxically intensify emotional ambiguity and psychological complexity; the surface disenchantment masks profound emotional investment beneath.
- The tension between emotional authenticity (what the character truly feels) and emotional performance (what they show) is central to Shakespearean drama and psychological realism; exploring this gap became the obsession of 20th-century fiction and theatre.
- Literature itself can be understood as an emotion-machine: the reader's engagement is fundamentally affective, structured by rhythm, image, voice, and narrative design to produce specific emotional states and transformations.

## connections

- [[catharsis]] — the transformative emotional release that classical tragedy aims to produce.
- [[aristotle]] — theorised emotion's role in tragic form and its moral function.
- [[romanticism]] — movement that made feeling and emotional intensity primary aesthetic and philosophical values.
- [[tragedy]] — the form most theoretically concerned with emotion's moral and redemptive power.
- [[sentimentalism]] — aesthetic and social movement built on the cultivation and display of tender feeling.
- [[stream-of-consciousness]] — narrative technique that represents emotion as the texture of thought itself.
- [[shakespeare]] — dramatist who explored the performance and authenticity of emotion with unmatched subtlety.
