## summary

**African American Literature** is the body of writing—poetry, fiction, essays, drama—produced by Black American authors that articulates the African American experience, from slavery through oppression to resistance and self-assertion. Far from a unified movement, it encompasses multiple traditions, voices, and aesthetics united by the centrality of race, identity, and the struggle for dignity in a racist society. From the *Harlem Renaissance* through the *Civil Rights Era* and beyond, African American literature has been both a mirror held to American injustice and a declaration of Black humanity and genius.

## you gotta know

- The *Harlem Renaissance* (1920s–early 1930s) was the first major flowering: *Langston Hughes*, *Claude McKay*, *Zora Neale Hurston*, and others asserted Black cultural pride, vernacular voice, and aesthetic innovation in the face of systemic racism.
- *James Baldwin* in essays and novels (*Go Tell It on the Mountain*, *Another Country*) wrote about identity, sexuality, and race with moral urgency and lyrical precision, establishing the essay as a weapon of Black political consciousness.
- *Ralph Ellison's* *Invisible Man* (1952) is a masterwork: the nameless protagonist's journey through American society dramatizes the psychological violence of racism and the African American struggle for recognition and selfhood.
- The *Civil Rights Era* (1950s–1960s) saw African American writers engaged directly in political struggle: literature as testimony, resistance, and vision for a different America.
- Distinct from *African literature* (literature from the continent); African American literature is rooted in the American experience, addressing slavery, Jim Crow racism, systemic oppression, and the fight for civil rights and human dignity.
- Encompasses diverse formal innovations: *stream of consciousness*, *vernacular voice*, *magical realism*, *experimental form*—often as a deliberate break from dominant literary traditions and aesthetic norms.
- Historically excluded from American literary canons; its recovery and centring has transformed our understanding of American literature itself.

## connections

- [[james-baldwin]] — essayist and novelist who made race, sexuality, and American identity inseparable in his work.
- [[ralph-ellison]] — author of *Invisible Man*, the definitive novel of racial alienation in America.
- [[langston-hughes]] — poet of vernacular voice and the Harlem Renaissance; a founding figure of modern African American literature.
- [[civil-rights-era]] — the historical period that African American literature addressed and shaped through artistic witness.
- [[harlem-renaissance]] — the cultural movement that launched African American literary modernity.
- [[civil-rights-movement]] — the political struggle that African American literature both reflected and advanced.
