## summary

The **Peloponnesian War** (431–404 BCE) was the catastrophic conflict between Athens and Sparta for hegemony over ancient Greece, lasting twenty-seven years and ending in Athens' defeat, the collapse of its empire, and the fracturing of Greek city-state independence. Beyond its historical and military significance, the war became an obsession of Greek dramatists—particularly [[aristophanes]]—who used theatrical comedy to critique the conflict's futility, question martial propaganda, and explore the psychological and moral costs of prolonged warfare on women, soldiers, and democratic society. The war's documentation and literary response essentially invented Western war literature, establishing templates for how artists represent military catastrophe that writers would follow for millennia.

## you gotta know

- Fought between the Athenian empire and its allies against Sparta and the Peloponnesian League, stemming from tensions over Athenian imperial expansion and Spartan fears of Athens' power.
- Athens' catastrophic Sicilian Expedition (415 BCE)—a failed attempt to expand empire into Sicily—marked the turning point, draining Athens' resources and destroying its military credibility.
- [[aristophanes]] wrote plays directly satirizing the war while it was happening, particularly *Lysistrata* (where women withhold sex to end war) and *The Frogs* (where the god Dionysus descends to recover a dead tragic poet).
- The conflict devastated the Greek population, introducing plague, famine, refugee crises, and moral compromises (like the massacre at Melos) that undermined Athenian democracy's ethical foundations.
- Thucydides' historical account became the definitive narrative, establishing conventions for war writing and moral analysis that shaped all subsequent historical and literary treatments.
- The plays written during and after the war established comedy as a vehicle for antiwar sentiment and political dissent, using absurdity to expose the disconnect between patriotic rhetoric and catastrophic reality.
- Sparta's eventual victory proved hollow: it brought no lasting peace but rather decades of Spartan tyranny, followed by the rise of Macedonia and the end of city-state independence.

## connections

- [[aristophanes]] — the dramatist who made the war his central artistic subject, using comedy to question military propaganda and human sacrifice.
- [[athens]] — the democratic power whose imperial ambitions and eventual defeat defined the war's tragic arc and its treatment in literature.
- [[sparta]] — Athens' rival and ultimate victor, representing authoritarian militarism in contrast to Athenian democratic values (however corrupted).
- [[ancient-greece]] — the civilization whose internal warfare effectively ended Greek independence and autonomy.
- [[lysistrata]] — Aristophanes' antiwar comedy using gender and sexual politics to imagine women stopping the war through collective action.
- [[thucydides]] — the historian whose account established conventions for war narrative and moral analysis that literature would build on.
- [[political-satire]] — the literary mode Aristophanes pioneered, using theatrical absurdity to critique power and military madness.
