## summary

**Bella Wilfer** is the central heroine of *Our Mutual Friend* (1864–1865), the final completed novel by [[charles-dickens]], set in Victorian [[england]]. Initially depicted as an avowedly mercenary, petulant young woman whose inheritance depends on marrying a stranger, she undergoes one of the author's most nuanced moral awakenings by renouncing fortune for integrity and love. Her psychological depth, human flaws, and spirited independence distinguish her from the passive heroines typical of Victorian [[realism]], cementing her as one of Dickens's finest character studies.

## you gotta know

- The central premise of her story hinges on the will of the eccentric contractor *Old John Harmon*, who leaves his vast fortune to his estranged son on the strict condition that he marry Bella, whom the testator only ever observed throwing a childhood tantrum in Greenwich.
- When introduced in the novel, she openly professes a mercenary philosophy, describing herself as shallow, petulant, and resentful of being treated like a piece of property bequeathed to a man she never met.
- Following the reported murder and drowning of her prospective husband, she is taken in by the kindly *Mr. Nicodemus Boffin* and his wife, who inherit the estate and treat Bella as their pampered protégée.
- She is courted by the mysterious secretary *John Rokesmith*, whom she initially disdains for his poverty, but she undergoes a crisis of conscience while watching Mr. Boffin seemingly degenerate into a cruel, paranoid miser who abuses Rokesmith.
- In a pivotal confrontation, Bella denounces Boffin's tyranny, champions Rokesmith, and walks away from the Boffin mansion, deciding that personal honor and affection outweigh corrupting wealth.
- She marries Rokesmith in modest circumstances, managing their cottage with *The Complete British Housewife* and doting on her henpecked father, *Reginald Wilfer* ("the Cherub"), in contrast to her shrewish mother.
- Her moral growth is rewarded when Rokesmith is revealed as the living *John Harmon*, and Boffin's cruelty is unmasked as a deliberate performance designed to preserve Bella from greed.
- Critics highlight her as a rare departure from Dickens's idealized, angelic female archetypes, embodying a mature exploration of money, identity, and ethics during the [[industrial-revolution]].

## connections

- [[charles-dickens]] — Victorian author who broke from his usual pattern of sentimental heroines to grant Bella genuine psychological growth.
- [[our-mutual-friend]] — the 1864–1865 serial novel in which her moral education and the Harmon dust-heap inheritance unfold.
- [[john-harmon]] — her designated husband who woos her under the disguise of the impoverished secretary *John Rokesmith*.
- [[realism]] — the literary movement reflected in her believable flaws, internal struggles, and resistance to moral perfection.
- [[satire]] — Dickens's weapon against Victorian greed and commercialism, exemplified by the absurd conditions of Old Harmon's will.
- [[england]] — the Victorian society whose social ladders, legal oddities, and class pressures define Bella's world.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — the era of urban dust heaps, rampant speculation, and capital accumulation that shapes the novel's backdrop.
