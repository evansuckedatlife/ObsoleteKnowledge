## summary

**War and Peace** (1869) is *Leo Tolstoy*'s monumental novel depicting Russian society during the Napoleonic Wars, following multiple characters across class boundaries as their personal dramas intersect with historical events. Often called the greatest novel ever written, *War and Peace* combines sweeping historical narrative with intimate psychological portraiture; it explores the relationship between individual will and historical inevitability, asking whether great historical events are shaped by individuals' decisions or by impersonal forces beyond anyone's control. The novel's scope is encyclopedic—hundreds of characters, intricate plotting, philosophical digressions—yet Tolstoy achieves an aesthetic unity that has shaped how subsequent novelists understand the novel's possibilities.

## you gotta know

- The *Rostov* and *Bolkonsky* families form the narrative center; their children (*Natasha*, *Andrew*, *Pierre Bezukhov*) navigate love, war, and spiritual transformation against the backdrop of Napoleon's invasion of Russia.
- *Pierre Bezukhov* is one of literature's most complex protagonists: an awkward, idealistic nobleman searching for meaning; his spiritual journey mirrors the novel's meditation on purpose and faith.
- *Natasha Rostova* embodies youthful innocence transformed by experience; her romance with *Prince Andrew* and subsequent trials and growth constitute one of literature's great love stories.
- The *Battle of Borodino* (1812) is the novel's military climax: Tolstoy depicts the battle from multiple perspectives, emphasizing confusion, accident, and the inadequacy of military strategy in the face of chaotic reality.
- Tolstoy digresses philosophically throughout: he debates the nature of historical causation, arguing that great events are not caused by individuals but by vast impersonal forces; great leaders are merely products of their times.
- The novel spans approximately 1,200 pages; it encompasses aristocratic salon society, military campaigns, intimate domestic scenes, and philosophical argument—a totality of human experience.
