# Postmodernism

## summary

**Postmodernism** emerged in the late 20th century (roughly 1945–2000s) as a reaction against and deconstruction of *modernism*'s faith in progress, universal truth, and coherent meaning. It emphasizes *irony*, *pastiche*, *self-reflexivity*, and *fragmentation*, treating grand narratives and stable identity as illusions. In literature, postmodern works foreground their own artificiality, layer multiple voices and narratives, and playfully violate genre boundaries.

## you gotta know

- Defines itself against modernism's seriousness and teleology; where modernism sought to innovate toward new forms of meaning, postmodernism questions whether meaning itself is stable or achievable.
- Employs *metafiction*: literature that calls attention to its own constructed nature, breaking the fourth wall, acknowledging the author, treating narrative as a game or puzzle rather than an illusion of reality.
- Celebrates *pastiche* and *intertextuality*: the mixing of high and low culture, the quotation and sampling of prior texts, the collapse of hierarchies between serious and commercial art.
- Features *unreliable narrators*, *multiple endings*, *nested narratives*, and *narrative fragmentation* that resist coherent interpretation; meaning becomes contingent, playful, provisional.
- Often expresses skepticism toward authority, grand claims, and coherent identity; the postmodern subject is fragmented, constructed, and historically contingent rather than unified or essential.
- Major figures include *Thomas Pynchon*, *David Foster Wallace*, *Don DeLillo*, *Italo Calvino*; in drama, *Samuel Beckett*; in visual art and architecture, widespread.
- Emerged partly in response to postwar disillusionment, the Vietnam War, consumer capitalism, television, and mass media; it reflects a culture saturated by images and narratives that no longer cohere.

## connections

- [[modernism]] — postmodernism's predecessor and foil; where modernism innovates toward new meaning, postmodernism deconstructs meaning itself.
- [[samuel-beckett]] — his stripped, repetitive plays are proto-postmodern; they expose theater's artificiality and bracket narrative meaning.
- [[thomas-pynchon]] — quintessential postmodern novelist; his works layer paranoia, conspiracy, entropy, and historical pastiche into baroque systems that resist interpretation.
- [[david-foster-wallace]] — postmodern heir who combines experimental structure with emotional intensity and meta-commentary on literature's possibility.
- [[irony]] — postmodernism's dominant mode; irony allows simultaneous engagement and detachment, sincerity and skepticism.
- [[self-reflexivity]] — central technique; postmodern works display their own construction, rules, and limitations.
- [[fragmentation]] — the postmodern aesthetic; meaning, narrative, and identity are fractured, non-linear, resistant to synthesis.
