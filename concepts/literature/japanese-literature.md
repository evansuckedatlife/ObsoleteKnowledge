# Japanese Literature

## summary

**Japanese literature** encompasses a rich, ancient tradition spanning from the 8th-century court writings of *The Tale of Genji* to contemporary fiction. It developed largely independently from European models until the Meiji Restoration (1868), after which Japanese writers synthesized Western literary forms with indigenous poetic and narrative sensibilities. Key figures like *Akutagawa Rynosuke*, *Kawabata Yasunari*, and *Mishima Yukio* pioneered modern Japanese fiction, exploring psychological depth, aesthetic refinement, and the collision between tradition and modernity. Japanese literature is known for its attention to nuance, transience, and the unspoken—virtues inherited from classical poetry and Buddhist philosophy.

## you gotta know

- *The Tale of Genji* (early 11th century) by *Murasaki Shikibu* is often considered the world's first novel; it depicts the life and loves of *Hikaru Genji*, a prince of the Heian court, with psychological depth and aesthetic sophistication.
- *Haiku* is Japan's signature poetic form: three lines of 5-7-5 syllables capturing a single moment of sensory perception and emotional resonance; *Matsuo Basho* (1644–1694) perfected the form.
- *Akutagawa Rynosuke* (1892–1927) pioneered the modern short story in Japan, synthesizing European modernist techniques with Japanese sensibility; *Rashomon* is his most famous work, told from multiple perspectives.
- *Kawabata Yasunari* (1899–1972) won the Nobel Prize and is known for his delicate, introspective novels like *Snow Country* and *The Sound of the Mountain*, which emphasize aesthetic beauty and emotional restraint.
- *Mishima Yukio* (1925–1970) brought transgressive energy and classical Japanese aesthetics to modern fiction; his tetralogy *The Sea of Fertility* is an ambitious exploration of beauty, death, and reincarnation.
- Japanese literature emphasizes *mono no aware* (the pathos of things)—a Buddhist-influenced aesthetic of melancholy acceptance of impermanence and the beauty found in transience.
