## summary

**Biblical translation** encompasses the rendering of the religious scriptures of [[judaism]] and [[christianity]] from their original *Biblical Hebrew*, *Aramaic*, and *Koine Greek* into vernacular languages across global history. Beginning in antiquity with Jewish communities in Hellenistic Egypt and continuing through the Protestant Reformation to modern missionary and scholarly efforts, it has served as a central engine of cultural diffusion, theological transformation, and linguistic standardization. The practice fundamentally shaped world literature, canonized national languages, and gave rise to landmark texts that transformed Western literary style.

## you gotta know

- The *King James Bible* (or *Authorized Version*), commissioned in 1604 by King James I of [[england]] and published in 1611, established foundational idioms of the English language and exerted profound stylistic influence on the prose and [[poetry]] of the [[english-renaissance]] and later literature.
- *William Tyndale* produced the first English translation drawn directly from original Hebrew and Greek texts in 1526, introducing enduring phrases such as "passover" and "scapegoat" before being executed for heresy near Brussels.
- *Martin Luther* translated the New Testament and Old Testament into vernacular German in [[germany]] during the 1520s and 1530s, an achievement that standardized the Early New High German literary language and accelerated the Protestant Reformation.
- The *Septuagint*, produced in Alexandria during the 3rd and 2nd centuries BCE for Greek-speaking Jewish communities of [[ancient-greece]], constitutes the oldest surviving Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures and derives its name from the legendary seventy-two translators who produced it.
- Saint *Jerome* produced the *Vulgate* in the late 4th century under the direction of Pope Damasus I, creating a comprehensive Latin version based primarily on the Hebrew original rather than earlier Old Latin copies, which served as the official scripture of the Western Church throughout the [[roman-empire]] and the Middle Ages.
- The *Geneva Bible* of 1560, produced by English Marian exiles in Switzerland, introduced numbered verses, maps, and extensive Calvinist marginal annotations, becoming the preferred study Bible of the Puritans and early settlers in the [[united-states]].
- *John Wycliffe* and his followers, known as the *Lollards*, completed the first full Middle English translation of the scriptures from the Latin Vulgate during the late 14th century, provoking the Oxford Constitutions of 1408 that strictly banned unauthorized vernacular versions.
- Bishop *Ulfilas* translated the Bible into the *Gothic language* during the 4th century, creating a dedicated Gothic alphabet for the purpose and leaving behind the single most important surviving linguistic document of the extinct East Germanic branch.

## connections

- [[christianity]] — the religion whose expansion and theological controversies drove the global translation of canonical scriptures into vernacular tongues.
- [[judaism]] — the ancient religious tradition that produced the Hebrew source scriptures and earliest translations including the Septuagint and Targums.
- [[english-renaissance]] — the literary era in which vernacular translations culminated in the King James Version, reshaping poetic syntax and national prose style.
- [[germany]] — the homeland of the Protestant Reformation where Martin Luther's translation helped unify the national language and foster vernacular literacy.
- [[ancient-greece]] — the cultural and linguistic sphere whose *Koine* dialect formed the original language of Christian scripture and early Jewish translations.
- [[roman-empire]] — the Mediterranean power during whose late imperial period Jerome compiled the Latin Vulgate as the standard text of the West.
- [[linguistics]] — the academic discipline that utilizes early biblical translations to analyze historical phonology, morphology, and dialect variation across dead languages.
