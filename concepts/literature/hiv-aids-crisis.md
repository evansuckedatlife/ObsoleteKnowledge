## summary

The **HIV/AIDS crisis** was the devastating pandemic that emerged in the 1980s and reshaped American culture, politics, sexuality, and literature. Striking primarily gay men, communities of colour, and the poor, it triggered simultaneous catastrophe and resistance—and provoked the artistic response that became foundational to contemporary drama. Tony Kushner's *Angels in America* and Jonathan Larson's *Rent* transformed the crisis into urgent theatrical art that spoke public truth to private suffering.

## you gotta know

- AIDS was first identified in 1981; by the mid-1980s it had become an epidemic; the virus was identified as HIV in 1984; by 1987 it had killed tens of thousands, disproportionately gay men in major American cities and rural areas worldwide.
- The Reagan administration's negligence and the religious right's condemnation created a political vacuum: the affected communities themselves became the agents of care, resistance, and truth-telling; governments abandoned the dying while communities organised survival.
- *ACT UP* (AIDS Coalition to Unleash Power), founded 1987, pioneered direct action activism—civil disobedience, public shaming of government and pharmaceutical companies, and a fusion of radical politics and artistic performance; they made activism an aesthetic practice.
- The crisis produced profound homophobia and scapegoating; politicians and media blamed gay men for the epidemic; it also generated unexpected solidarity and accelerated demands for queer visibility, rights, and representation; suffering became politicised.
- *Angels in America* (1991–1992) premiered as the crisis was still raging; it transformed the epidemic into a national allegory addressing Republicanism, freedom, transcendence, and American futurity itself; the play made the crisis a matter of state and spirit.
- *Rent* (1996) brought the crisis into musical theatre, centering queer and trans and poor communities' lives, loves, and deaths as worthy of Broadway attention and spectacle; the musical reached mainstream audiences with marginalised stories.
- By the 2000s, antiretroviral therapies transformed AIDS from immediate death sentence into chronic manageable condition—but the crisis's cultural and artistic consequences remain; the epidemic shaped contemporary LGBT+ identity and literature.
- The crisis produced an extraordinary archive of artistic response: plays, novels, poetry, photography, and performance art that witnessed suffering and resistance; literature became a vehicle for testimony and survival.
