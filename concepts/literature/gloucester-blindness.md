## summary

**Gloucester's blindness** refers to the horrific physical mutilation and subsequent spiritual epiphany of the Earl of Gloucester in Act III, Scene 7 of *[[william-shakespeare]]'s* tragedy *[[king-lear]]*. Carried out by the Duke of Cornwall and Regan following the treasonous betrayal of Gloucester's illegitimate son Edmund, the blinding stands as one of the most shocking and visceral spectacles in Renaissance drama. Structurally, the act mirrors King Lear's descent into madness, demonstrating how sensory destruction can paradoxically yield profound moral and emotional clarity.

## you gotta know

- Takes place on stage when the sadistic Duke of Cornwall gouges out Gloucester's eyes with the famous exclamation "Out, vile jelly! / Where is thy lustre now?" urged on by Regan.
- Provokes a rare moment of plebeian heroism in early modern drama when a nameless servant draws his sword against Cornwall to halt the cruelty, mortally wounding the duke.
- Mirrors the psychological ruin of King Lear, establishing parallel subplots of parental credulity, filial betrayal, and acute [[generational-conflict]].
- Preceded by Gloucester's moral gullibility, having readily believed Edmund's forged letter and unjustly banished his devoted legitimate son Edgar.
- Evokes the paradox of vision articulated in *[[sophocles]]'s* *Oedipus Rex*, summarized when the mutilated earl admits: "I stumbled when I saw."
- Drives Gloucester toward attempted suicide at the imaginary cliffs of Dover, where his disguised son Edgar orchestrates a compassionate psychological deception to save his life.
- Concludes when Gloucester dies offstage from an overwhelming emotional surge when his heart bursts smilingly between grief and joy upon learning Edgar has survived.

## connections
