## summary

**Aestheticism** emerged in the late 19th century as a philosophical and artistic movement asserting that aesthetic experience and formal beauty matter independently of moral, political, or social purpose. Championed by figures like *Oscar Wilde*, aesthetes rejected didacticism and utilitarian thinking, advocating *art for art's sake*. The movement scandalized Victorian society while reshaping how artists and critics understood creativity itself.

## you gotta know

- Rooted in a rebellion against Victorian moralism: the claim that art serves no function beyond its own beauty.
- Art should be judged on craft, style, and sensory power—not on its message, moral uplift, or social utility.
- The movement elevated style and form to equal or superior importance compared to content or plot.
- *Oscar Wilde* was the movement's most visible and provocative spokesperson; his wit and deliberate flamboyance made him a public symbol of aesthetic excess.
- Associated with the *decadent* movement in visual art (the Japanese prints, peacock motifs, and carefully curated interiors of the era).
- The slogan *art for art's sake* became a rallying cry, directly challenging the Romantic notion that art must express feeling or social commitment.
- Connected to *dandy* culture: the cultivation of exquisite taste, artifice, and personal style as a life-work.
- The movement's influence persists in modernist and contemporary art; the notion that formal innovation justifies itself remains central to avant-garde practice.
- Aestheticism provoked fierce criticism from moralists and social realists, who accused aesthetes of frivolity and irresponsibility toward society; this debate has never fully resolved.
