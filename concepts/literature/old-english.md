## summary

**Old English**, also historically known as **Anglo-Saxon**, is the earliest recorded form of the English language, spoken in early medieval [[england]] and parts of southern Scotland from the mid-fifth century until shortly after the Norman Conquest of 1066. Rooted in the West Germanic dialects brought to Britain by migrating Angles, Saxons, and Jutes, it developed a rich synthesis of Germanic heroic ethos and Latin [[christianity]]. It produced a monumental corpus of alliterative [[poetry]] and historical prose—most notably the epic masterpiece *Beowulf*—that forms the bedrock of English linguistic and literary history.

## you gotta know

- Preserved primarily as the language of *Beowulf*, the heroic epic detailing the Geatish prince's battles with Grendel, Grendel's mother, and a dragon, recorded in the unique Nowell Codex.
- Characterized as a synthetic and highly inflected language, featuring grammatical gender, four nominal cases, dual personal pronouns, and a complex system of strong and weak verbs.
- Structured poetic verse through accentual alliterative meter rather than end-rhyme, using a distinct medial caesura to divide each poetic line into two balanced half-lines.
- Employs vivid metaphorical compound words known as kennings, substituting figurative imagery such as *hronrad* (whale-road) for the sea and *banhus* (bone-house) for the human body.
- Recorded in four principal manuscript codices that preserve virtually all surviving verse: the Nowell Codex, the Exeter Book, the Vercelli Book, and the Junius Manuscript.
- Contains melancholic elegiac poems in the Exeter Book, such as *The Wanderer*, *The Seafarer*, and *The Wife's Lament*, which dwell on exile, transience, and the classical *ubi sunt* lament.
- Features *Caedmon's Hymn*, recorded in *Bede*'s Latin ecclesiastical history, recognized as the earliest documented poem in the language, composed by an unlettered cowherd given divine song.
- Underwent systematic administrative and literary promotion under *King Alfred the Great*, who translated major Latin treatises into the West Saxon dialect and initiated the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*.

## connections

- [[poetry]] — the preeminent artistic medium of the corpus, governed by alliterative hemistichs and formulaic diction.
- [[england]] — the territorial realm formed by unified Anglo-Saxon kingdoms where the vernacular flourished prior to 1066.
- [[linguistics]] — the academic discipline tracing its West Germanic syntax, phonological sound shifts, and grammatical evolution into Middle English.
- [[christianity]] — the institutional force introduced by monastic missions that established scriptoria, literacy, and biblical themes across Britain.
- [[roman-empire]] — whose departure from Britain in the fifth century created the power vacuum that enabled Germanic settlement and language shift.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — the Early Modern dramatist whose modern language represents the long-term syntactic outcome of Old English inflectional loss.
- [[jesus]] — depicted in heroic verse such as *The Dream of the Rood* as a victorious, resolute warrior-king mounting the cross.
