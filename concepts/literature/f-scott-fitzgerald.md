## summary

**F. Scott Fitzgerald** (1896–1940) was the literary voice of the Jazz Age, the American writer who best captured the glamour, excess, and moral emptiness of 1920s wealth and privilege. His masterpiece *The Great Gatsby* remains the definitive portrait of the American Dream's corruption. Fitzgerald's elegantly fractured prose style and his obsession with wealth, beauty, and inevitable decline made him a modernist master.

## you gotta know

- Born in St. Paul, Minnesota; educated at Princeton; lived much of his adult life in New York, the South, and Europe—rootlessness became both his subject and his condition, shaping his obsession with belonging and exile.
- *The Great Gatsby* (1925) depicts the failed pursuit of love and reinvention across the class boundaries of Long Island's gilded estates, set against the speculative mania of the Roaring Twenties; the novel asks whether the American Dream is a beautiful lie or an achievable truth.
- His debut *This Side of Paradise* (1920) made him famous overnight and codified the image of the Jazz Age youth—beautiful, educated, morally adrift; the novel's protagonists embodied the spiritual exhaustion beneath the era's glamour.
- Accompanied his wife *Zelda Sayre* into the European expatriate scene of the 1920s, where he completed *Tender Is the Night* (1934), a modernist epic about American decay abroad; the novel tracks the slow dissolution of a marriage and a man.
- Struggled with alcoholism throughout his career; his unfinished final novel *The Last Tycoon* was published posthumously and depicts Hollywood's golden age as a system of enchantment, power, and inevitable decline.
- His prose style combines lyrical precision with a sense of loss—beauty and wealth are rendered with sensory intensity even as the narrator observes their spiritual emptiness; this contradiction between form and content is the essence of Fitzgerald.
- Influenced by *Henry James* and *T.S. Eliot*; deeply shaped modernism's aesthetic of elegance within cultural dissolution; his work synthesised American themes with European formal sophistication.
- Though dismissed as a mere chronicler of Jazz Age excess, Fitzgerald is now recognised as a major modernist whose formal innovations and moral seriousness rank him alongside his greatest contemporaries.
