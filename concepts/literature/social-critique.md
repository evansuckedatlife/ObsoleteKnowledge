## summary

**Social critique** is the literary project of examining and condemning the structures, institutions, values, and power relations of a given society, often with the implicit or explicit goal of reform or revolution. Unlike satire, which employs irony and mockery, social critique may take the form of realistic documentation, philosophical argument, or speculative projection into dystopian futures. It is one of literature's most consequential functions: to hold a mirror to society and ask whether its foundations are just.

## you gotta know

- Social critique became a major literary project during the industrial revolution when realist novelists like *Dickens* and *Tolstoy* exposed the human costs of capitalist society.
- The critique may be implicit (showing suffering without commentary) or explicit (the narrator or protagonist articulates the injustice); both are effective.
- Dystopian fiction is critique by extrapolation: writers like *Huxley* and *Orwell* project present social tendencies into horrifying futures as warning.
- Social critique often targets the gap between official ideology and lived reality: the cruelty hidden behind claims of progress, the greed behind philanthropy.
- The form may be radical (calling for systemic overthrow) or reformist (calling for correction within existing systems); the literature itself rarely settles the question.
- Realist narrative techniques—showing concrete particulars rather than abstract principles—prove most effective for social critique; readers see and feel injustice directly.
- Social critique requires naming the system under fire: class, race, gender, colonialism, war, or environmental destruction—the specificity makes the analysis powerful.
- The danger of social critique is didacticism: when the message overwhelms the narrative, the work becomes polemic rather than literature, diminishing its emotional and imaginative power.

## connections

- [[dystopia]] — the speculative form social critique most often takes in science fiction.
- [[aldous-huxley]] — chronicler of how pleasure and comfort can mask totalitarian control.
- [[george-orwell]] — master of political critique through fiction and essay.
- [[satire]] — a related but distinct mode; satire ridicules while critique exposes.
- [[realism]] — the aesthetic tradition most suited to social critique.
- [[totalitarianism]] — the political evil most frequently subjected to literary critique in the 20th century.
