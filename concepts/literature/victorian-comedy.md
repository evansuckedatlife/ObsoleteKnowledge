## summary

**Victorian comedy** is the comic tradition of 19th-century British literature, characterized by wit, social observation, and verbal playfulness deployed against contemporary social pretensions. Unlike the melodrama and sentiment that dominated much Victorian culture, Victorian comedies—especially the drawing-room comedies of Oscar Wilde—used humor, paradox, and clever dialogue to interrogate hypocrisy, morality, and respectability while entertaining audiences. The tradition peaked in the 1890s with a flowering of brilliant comic drama.

## you gotta know

- Oscar Wilde's *The Importance of Being Earnest* (1895) stands as the apotheosis of Victorian comedy—a masterpiece of wit, paradox, and comic construction where plot itself becomes secondary to brilliant, dazzling language and character.
- Victorian comedies typically deploy *epigram*—a witty, paradoxical one-liner—as their dominant rhetorical weapon, as when Wilde writes "The truth is rarely pure and never simple," turning conventional wisdom upside down through verbal cleverness.
- The genre frequently satirizes Victorian society's obsession with propriety, respectability, and class convention, using absurd situations and impossible complications to expose their arbitrariness and reveal social hypocrisy.
- Gilbert and Sullivan operettas represent another major stream of Victorian comedy, combining music, elaborate plots, and comic characters to gently mock contemporary institutions (law, the military, Parliament, academia).
- *Dialogue* became the primary vehicle of Victorian comedy, privileging clever wordplay and repartee over physical comedy or intricate plotting, making Victorian comedies literature-driven rather than action-driven.
- The tradition emerges partly as a sharp reaction against earlier Victorian earnestness and melodrama, offering intellectual humor and sophisticated wit for educated audiences who appreciated language as entertainment.
- Major practitioners include Wilde, George Bernard Shaw, W.S. Gilbert, and Richard Brinsley Sheridan (whose 18th-century works remained influential models for Victorian comic playwrights).
- Victorian comedy reached its zenith in the 1890s, then declined after Wilde's imprisonment in 1895, which cast a shadow over the witty culture he had embodied.
