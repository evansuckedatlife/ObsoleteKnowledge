## summary

**Cordelia** is the virtuous youngest daughter in William Shakespeare's *King Lear*, distinguished by her refusal to flatter her aging father and her unwavering loyalty in the face of abandonment and cruelty. When *Lear* demands that his three daughters declare their love to determine their inheritance, Cordelia speaks plainly and honestly, earning his rage and disinheritance. Her tragic dignity—refusing false flattery even when it costs her everything—makes her the moral centre of the play and a symbol of integrity crushed by folly and malice.

## you gotta know

- In the love-auction that opens *King Lear*, Goneril and Regan offer extravagant professions of devotion while Cordelia simply says she loves her father "according to [her] bond" and cannot "heave [her] heart into [her] mouth."
- *Lear*, enraged by what he perceives as coldness and ingratitude, disowns her and divides his kingdom between her sisters, who promptly betray him.
- Despite her father's cruelty, Cordelia leads the French forces against her sisters, motivated by filial duty and moral conviction.
- Her tragic death—after the battle turns against her—is one of Shakespeare's most devastating moments; she is murdered at *Edmund's* orders, and dies in *Lear's* arms.
- Represents the collision between truth-telling and pragmatism, honesty and survival; her refusal to play the flattery game costs her a kingdom and ultimately her life.
- Often read as the play's conscience—the one character whose moral clarity exposes the corruption and blindness of those around her.
