## summary

*The Tell-Tale Heart* is a short story by *Edgar Allan Poe*, published in 1843, narrated by an unnamed man who insists on his sanity while recounting the murder of an elderly man and the psychological torment that follows. The story exemplifies Poe's mastery of unreliable narration and psychological horror, using the narrator's obsessive voice to collapse the boundary between guilt and auditory hallucination. It remains a canonical exploration of madness and the human conscience rendered as narrative.

## you gotta know

- The narrator murders his elderly roommate specifically because of the old man's "vulture eye"—an obsession rooted in perceptual distortion rather than rational motive.
- The narrator meticulously plans the murder over eight nights, demonstrating a twisted rationality that coexists with obvious psychological instability.
- He dismembers the corpse and hides it beneath the floorboards, believing the deed is perfect until police arrive—whether summoned or coincidentally.
- The narrative climaxes with the narrator hearing the victim's heartbeat beneath the floor, leading him to confess; whether the sound is real or hallucinatory remains deliberately ambiguous.
- The story pioneered the technique of psychological realism in American literature—the interior monologue of a disturbed mind becomes the story's substance.
- The obsessive first-person voice creates complicity: readers are positioned inside the killer's consciousness, forced to inhabit his distorted perception.
- The story influenced the development of the detective story and psychological thriller, establishing archetypes of guilt, paranoia, and confession.
