## summary

**Amy Lowell** was an influential American poet, critic, and editor who championed [[modernism]] and free verse during the early twentieth century. A leading figure of the Imagist movement, she assumed leadership of the transatlantic faction from *Ezra Pound*, who subsequently ridiculed her democratic, accessible reinvention of the aesthetic as "Amygism." Awarded a posthumous Pulitzer Prize in 1926 for *What's O'Clock*, she expanded the technical horizons of English [[poetry]] through her pioneering work with polyphonic prose and remains renowned for vivid sensory lyrics such as *Patterns* and *Lilacs*.

## you gotta know

- Assumed leadership of the Imagist poetry circle following the departure of *Ezra Pound*, prompting him to disparagingly label her populist aesthetic and anthologies as "Amygism."
- Composed the celebrated anti-war lyric *Patterns*, in which a woman in a formal gown walking down garden paths laments the combat death of her betrothed, ending with the famous outcry, "Christ! What are patterns for?"
- Financed, compiled, and edited the influential anthology series *Some Imagist Poets* across three successive volumes from 1915 to 1917, popularizing free verse in both [[england]] and the [[united-states]].
- Won the 1926 Pulitzer Prize for Poetry posthumously for the collection *What's O'Clock*, which included her acclaimed descriptive poem *Lilacs*.
- Championed the technique of "polyphonic prose", an experimental form combining the rhythms, assonance, and meter of [[poetry]] within the continuous paragraphs of prose.
- Belonged to Massachusetts' prominent Boston Brahmin Lowell family, counting astronomer *Percival Lowell* and Harvard President *A. Lawrence Lowell* as brothers.
- Maintained a decades-long romantic companionship and domestic "Boston marriage" with actress *Ada Dwyer Russell*, who inspired passionate love lyrics including *The Taxi* and the sequence *Two Speak Together*.
- Cultivated an eccentric public persona marked by smoking black cigars in public, keeping late-night writing hours, and dying of a stroke shortly after completing a mammoth two-volume biography of *John Keats*.

## connections

- [[modernism]] — she served as a combative champion, organizer, and stylistic innovator during the formative years of the Anglo-American modernist movement.
- [[poetry]] — she broadened poetic technique by advocating for musical cadence, precise sensory detail, and the hybrid medium of polyphonic prose.
- [[american-literature]] — her prominent New England heritage and widely circulated collections helped introduce avant-garde poetics to mainstream American readers.
- [[t-s-eliot]] — fellow American modernist poet whose expatriate circle in London interacted with and reacted against her Imagist campaigns.
- [[world-war-i]] — the devastating global conflict that provides the tragic subject of her masterpiece *Patterns* and halted transatlantic literary exchanges.
- [[england]] — the setting of London salons where she met leading poets, negotiated editorial control, and launched her anthology projects.
- [[united-states]] — her home country where she staged national lecture tours, read her works to packed halls, and ignited the free verse controversy.
