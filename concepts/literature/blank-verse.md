## summary

**Blank verse** is unrhymed poetry written in iambic pentameter—a rhythmic pattern of unstressed and stressed syllables (da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM da-DUM) repeated five times per line. Pioneered in English by poets of the *English Renaissance*, particularly *Christopher Marlowe* and *William Shakespeare*, blank verse became the dominant form for serious dramatic and narrative poetry in English. Its flexibility and dignity made it the natural vessel for ambitious literary projects.

## you gotta know

- Iambic pentameter consists of five *iambs* (metrical feet of two syllables: unstressed-stressed) per line.
- The *blank* refers to the absence of rhyme; the regularity comes from meter, not end-rhyme.
- Introduced to English by *Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey* in the 16th century, adapting the Italian *verso sciolto*.
- *Christopher Marlowe* popularized blank verse for drama in plays like *Tamburlaine* and *Doctor Faustus*, proving it could match the energy and passion of spoken dialogue.
- *William Shakespeare* mastered blank verse, using it as the foundation for his dramatic verse while varying the meter for dramatic effect—speeding lines for excitement, slowing them for melancholy.
- *John Milton* elevated blank verse to epic grandeur in *Paradise Lost*, demonstrating that unrhymed verse could sustain philosophical and narrative complexity at the highest level.
- Blank verse offers a middle path between prose and rhymed poetry: more naturalistic than rhyme but more elevated and patterned than free verse.
- Modern poets rarely write in strict blank verse, though the form remains important in understanding English literary tradition, prosody, and dramatic verse technique.
