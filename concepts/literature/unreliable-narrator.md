## summary

An **Unreliable Narrator** is a voice whose account of events cannot be trusted because of deliberate deception, mental illness, limited perspective, or moral blindness. The reader must actively interpret what is being told, separating truth from distortion, in order to understand the real story beneath the narration. This technique creates dramatic irony and forces readers into the position of detectives, constructing their own truth from an unreliable account.

## you gotta know

- *Edgar Allan Poe's* *The Tell-Tale Heart* is canonical: the narrator insists he is "not mad" while describing the methodical murder of an old man and his obsession with a disembodied heartbeat—his very defense proves his unreliability.
- *Agatha Christie's* *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* features a narrator who tells the truth about events but withholds a crucial fact; the reader is technically not lied to, but deliberately misled through omission.
- Unreliability can stem from *madness* (mental illness distorting perception), *deception* (the narrator lying), *limited knowledge* (the narrator doesn't have access to all information), or *bias* (the narrator's values distort judgment).
- Creates *dramatic irony*: the reader knows or suspects the narrator is wrong; the pleasure comes from solving the puzzle beneath the false account.
- Differs from an *ignorant* narrator (who simply doesn't know what's happening) or an *innocent* narrator (who misunderstands correctly); unreliable narrators actively mislead.
- In *modernist* and *postmodern* literature, unreliability becomes a formal technique reflecting epistemological doubt—the idea that knowledge itself is unstable and contested.
- Often paired with *first-person narrative*, which naturally restricts perspective, making the narrator's reliability a central question.

## connections

- [[the-tell-tale-heart]] — the foundational text exemplifying madness-driven unreliability.
- [[the-murder-of-roger-ackroyd]] — the detective fiction that redefined unreliability as strategic omission.
- [[psychological-thriller]] — the genre that weaponizes unreliable narration to generate suspense.
- [[first-person-narrative]] — the narrative mode most susceptible to unreliability.
- [[metafiction]] — unreliability as formal self-awareness; the text calls attention to narration's constructed nature.
- [[fight-club-twist]] — a contemporary example where the narrator's unreliability is the entire structure's secret.
