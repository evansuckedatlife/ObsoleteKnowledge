## summary

**Desire** functions in literature as the primal erotic and psychological force that marriage both channels and condemns. Across dramatic and narrative traditions, this instinct drives characters to transgression when societal institutions fail to contain it, clashing directly with moral law and domestic order. When threatened or thwarted, desire turns inward and transforms into the obsessive fear of loss, giving rise to the destructive [[jealousy]] that precipitates canonical [[tragedy]].

## you gotta know

- Precipitates the catastrophic unraveling of *[[william-shakespeare]]*'s *[[othello]]*, where the Moor's intense passion for *Desdemona* is corrupted by *Iago* into the conviction that carnal betrayal has occurred, demonstrating how desire turns inward into all-consuming [[jealousy]].
- Provides the philosophical battlefield for *[[leo-tolstoy]]*'s novella *[[the-kreutzer-sonata]]*, in which *Pozdnyshev* indicts sensual lust as a destructive madness masked by the legal veneer of matrimony, culminating in the murder of his wife after obsessing over her musical intimacy with a violinist.
- Animates nineteenth-century [[realism]] through the classic adultery novel, notably *Gustave Flaubert*'s *Madame Bovary* and *Tolstoy*'s *Anna Karenina*, where female romantic longing rebels against the sterile decorum and economic practicalities of provincial marriage.
- Serves in classical [[tragedy]] as an uncontrollable, destructive madness inflicted by the gods, dramatized in *Euripides*' *Hippolytus* and *Jean Racine*'s *Phèdre* through *Phaedra*'s illicit obsession with her stepson, which shatters civic law and prompts suicidal false accusations.
- Manifests across the works of *[[fyodor-dostoevsky]]*, particularly in *The Brothers Karamazov*, where uncontrolled sensualism—termed *Karamazovian baseness*—drives father *Fyodor* and son *Dmitri* into violent rivalry over *Grushenka*.
- Framed in medieval romance and Renaissance [[poetry]] as an inherently transgressive ideal, establishing the courtly tradition in which true spiritual and erotic devotion could only exist outside the contractual confines of arranged marriage, as seen with *Tristan and Iseult* or *Lancelot and Guinevere*.
- Ignites the central conflict of twentieth-century [[american-literature]] in *Tennessee Williams*'s *A Streetcar Named Desire*, where the brutal animal vitality of *Stanley Kowalski* shatters the fragile, repressed romantic delusions of *Blanche DuBois*.
- Analyzed in literary theory through *René Girard*'s concept of triangular or mimetic desire, which posits that characters do not desire objects autonomously but copy the longing of a mediator or rival, inevitably converting erotic attraction into competitive violence.

## connections

- [[othello]] — *Shakespearean* tragedy tracing how intense marital passion is inverted into murderous suspicion and fear of cuckoldry.
- [[the-kreutzer-sonata]] — *Tolstoy*'s polemical novella portraying carnal lust as a savage impulse that domestic marriage hypocritically sanitizes.
- [[jealousy]] — the psychological transformation that occurs when possessive desire is threatened by perceived loss or rivalry.
- [[marriage-and-sexuality]] — the social and institutional framework designed to govern, legalize, and restrain erotic impulse.
- [[tragedy]] — the dramatic form wherein uncontrollable personal appetites inevitably violate cosmic or moral order.
- [[william-shakespeare]] — dramatist whose works repeatedly contrast public civic duty with volatile, ungovernable romantic attachments.
- [[realism]] — nineteenth-century literary movement chronicling the friction between individual erotic longing and bourgeois domestic conventions.
- [[leo-tolstoy]] — Russian novelist whose later moral philosophy demanded total ascetic renunciation of sensual and carnal gratification.
