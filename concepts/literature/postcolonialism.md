## summary

**Postcolonialism** is an intellectual, cultural, and literary movement that interrogates, critiques, and dismantles the ideological, political, and psychological legacies of European imperial rule. Emerging prominently in the mid-twentieth century alongside global decolonization across Africa, the Caribbean, and South Asia, **postcolonial literature** addresses enduring themes of displacement, linguistic subversion, and national identity. It fundamentally transformed literary studies by recentering marginalized voices, challenging Eurocentric canons, and examining how imperial hegemony continues to shape global geopolitics, diaspora communities, and contemporary cultural production.

## you gotta know

- *Edward Said's* 1978 landmark study *Orientalism* established the foundational framework of **postcolonial theory**, demonstrating how Western scholarship, literature, and art historically constructed a distorted, exoticized image of the "Orient" to legitimize colonial domination by the [[british-empire]] and [[france]].
- *Chinua Achebe* provided an indispensable fictional counter-narrative with his 1958 novel *Things Fall Apart*, which restored complex African history and directly answered the dehumanizing colonial tropes found in European works like *Joseph Conrad's* *Heart of Darkness*.
- *Frantz Fanon*, a Martinican psychiatrist who joined the Algerian independence struggle, analyzed the severe psychological damage, internalized racial inferiority, and cultural alienation inflicted by colonization in *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) and *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961).
- *Homi Bhabha* introduced influential theoretical concepts including mimicry, ambivalence, and hybridity in *The Location of Culture* (1994), arguing that colonial power is inherently unstable because compelling colonized subjects to imitate Western customs produces subversive mockery rather than docile obedience.
- *Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak* articulated a foundational critique of Western representation in her 1988 essay "Can the Subaltern Speak?", arguing that the doubly marginalized subaltern—particularly third-world women—is systematically silenced by imperialist and indigenous patriarchal hierarchies.
- The debate over language and decolonial aesthetics divided major authors: Kenyan novelist *Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o* argued in *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) that writers must abandon European tongues for indigenous languages, while Caribbean poets like *Derek Walcott* and *Kamau Brathwaite* reclaimed and hybridized European idioms alongside [[creole-language]] traditions.
- Authors often deploy [[magical-realism]] and fragmented, non-linear chronology to reject Western narrative [[realism]], as seen in *Salman Rushdie's* *Midnight's Children* (1981), which maps Indian partition and independence through myth, memory, and diasporic [[exile]].

## connections

- [[british-empire]] — the dominant global imperial power whose administrative structures, territorial partitions, and imposed education systems created the socio-political crises critiqued throughout Anglophone postcolonial writing.
- [[magical-realism]] — the narrative mode frequently adopted by postcolonial novelists to challenge rationalist European realism and depict the layered, mythic realities of postcolonial societies.
- [[modernism]] — the twentieth-century aesthetic movement whose formal experiments with fractured perspective influenced postcolonial authors, who in turn contested its underlying Eurocentric worldviews.
- [[realism]] — the traditional Western literary convention that postcolonial authors deconstruct to accommodate oral storytelling, collective memory, and non-Western epistemologies.
- [[latin-american-literature]] — a rich regional tradition whose early explorations of colonial subjugation, mestizo identity, and political resistance prefigured formal academic postcolonial theory.
- [[france]] — the imperial power whose violent colonial presence across North Africa and the Caribbean catalyzed the revolutionary political philosophy of *Frantz Fanon* and *Aimé Césaire*.
- [[diaspora]] — the scattering of populations through slavery, indenture, and post-independence economic migration, constituting one of the central thematic and geographic concerns of postcolonial fiction.
