## summary

**Gloucester** is the Earl of Gloucester in *King Lear* and the protagonist of the play's parallel plot, a tragedy within a tragedy. Deceived by his bastard son *Edmund* into believing his legitimate heir *Edgar* has betrayed him, Gloucester suffers a catastrophe that mirrors and intensifies Lear's own downfall, ultimately suggesting that blindness—literal and metaphorical—is the human condition in Shakespeare's darkest play.

## you gotta know

- A subsidiary noble whose subplot mirrors Lear's central tragedy: both are deceived by children they trust, abandoned by those they favor, and destroyed by misjudgment of character.
- Easily manipulated by *Edmund*, who exploits his father's credulity and paternal anxiety to convince him that *Edgar*, his legitimate son, plots against him.
- Initially sympathetic to *Edmund* partly because Edmund is illegitimate but charming, while Edgar is legitimate but bookish and reserved—showing how superficial traits can blind a parent to deeper truths.
- Attempts to protect *Lear* after the king is cast out, earning him torture by *Regan* and *Goneril*; in a scene of almost unwatchable brutality, his eyes are plucked out on stage.
- His literal blindness becomes the play's central metaphor: he was metaphorically blind to Edmund's villainy and Edgar's loyalty while sighted, and achieves spiritual sight only after losing his eyes.
- After being blinded and cast out, he wanders in despair and eventually attempts suicide by leaping from "Dover Cliff"—though he is actually saved by the disguised Edgar.
- The revelation that *Edgar* (now disguised as "Poor Tom") has been wandering mad beside him while he thought him a traitor creates a final tragic recognition.
- His suffering at the hands of his children parallels *Lear's* but reaches a greater extreme: while Lear retains his inner sight and rage, Gloucester is literally maimed and spiritually broken before redemption.
- In the final act, learns that Edgar is alive and legitimate just before dying, suggesting Shakespeare's position that understanding often arrives too late to prevent catastrophe.
- The subplot is not merely parallel to Lear's tragedy but deepens it: Gloucester's blindness makes the play's central metaphor (seeing and not seeing moral truth) literal and visceral.
