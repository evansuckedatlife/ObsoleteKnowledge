## summary

**Homosexuality in literature** encompasses the explicit and coded representation of same-sex desire, queer identity, and transgressive sexuality across literary traditions. From encrypted metaphor to direct statement, this strand of literature charts both the reality of desire and the historical violence and shame that have surrounded its expression. Modernism became the crucial moment when LGBTQ+ writers could render same-sex desire with unprecedented psychological and emotional honesty.

## you gotta know

- For centuries, same-sex desire appeared in literature primarily through classical allusion (Orpheus and *Eurydice*, the trials of *Achilles* and *Patroclus*) or religious condemnation; direct representation was dangerous and largely absent in Western literary tradition.
- The decadent and aesthetic movements of the late 19th century (*Oscar Wilde*, fin-de-siècle culture) opened space for coded homoerotic expression, though *Wilde's* trials (1895) reaffirmed legal and social danger; aestheticism became a coded language for queer desire.
- *Hart Crane* (1899–1932), American poet, rendered same-sex desire and masculine eroticism with unprecedented directness and lyrical intensity in *The Bridge* and other modernist works—a revolutionary act; *Crane* proved modernism could accommodate queer subjectivity.
- *Marcel Proust* (1871–1922) included detailed representation of male homosexuality in *In Search of Lost Time*; *Proust's* honest, psychologically nuanced treatment of queer desire was globally influential and nearly unprecedented for its candor and depth.
- Modernism generally created artistic license for more explicit representation; the focus on interiority and stream-of-consciousness allowed writers to render desire from within consciousness rather than through narrative judgment; form itself became queer.
- The 20th century saw progressive movements toward visibility: from coded modernist expression to mid-century underground literature to post-Stonewall (1969) literature where queer identity becomes central rather than marginal; each era produced its own literary forms.
- Contemporary literature, drama, and poetry centre queer and trans voices, sexuality, and joy as worthy subjects rather than shameful secrets or tragic necessities; queer writing now encompasses diverse forms, voices, and communities.
- The literary representation of **homosexuality in literature** thus charts a history of shame, resistance, encoded expression, and ultimately, visibility; literature became essential to forging queer identity and community despite social erasure and violence.
