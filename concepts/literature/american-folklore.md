## summary

**American folklore** encompasses the rich tapestry of myths, legends, tall tales, ballads, and oral traditions cultivated across the [[united-states]] from the colonial era through rapid industrialization. Rooted in the cultural convergence of Indigenous oral lore, West African trickster tales, and European settler traditions, it reflects the frontier experience, regional labor cultures, and social tensions of a rapidly expanding nation. These stories deeply shaped early [[american-literature]] and national identity, immortalizing iconic figures who embodied superhuman physical resilience, comic frontier exaggeration, or tragic rebellion against encroaching technological change.

## you gotta know

- *Paul Bunyan* and his companion *Babe the Blue Ox* embody the quintessential American tall tale; while rooted in northern logging camp yarns told to explain geographic features like the Great Lakes, Bunyan was heavily commercialized into "fakelore" by advertising copywriter *William B. Laughead*.
- *John Henry*, the celebrated African American "steel-driving man," became a legendary folk hero after racing and defeating a steam-powered rock drill at the Big Bend Tunnel in West Virginia, dying with his hammer in his hand as a tragic symbol of human labor defying the [[industrial-revolution]].
- *Washington Irving* pioneered the formal literary adaptation of regional folk legends in early [[american-literature]], transposing German supernatural motifs into colonial New York Dutch history to create lasting classics such as *The Legend of Sleepy Hollow* and *Rip Van Winkle*.
- Trickster narratives featuring *Br'er Rabbit*, rooted in West African storytelling and adapted by enslaved laborers in the American South, depicted small, vulnerable creatures continually outwitting stronger adversaries such as *Br'er Fox* through clever deceit and the famous briar patch plea.
- *Pecos Bill*, the legendary cowboy folk hero of the Southwestern frontier, was famously described as being raised by coyotes, riding a bucking cyclone through the sky, and using a live rattlesnake as a lasso.
- Historical frontier figures like *Johnny Appleseed* (*John Chapman*), a wandering Swedenborgian nurseryman, and politician *Davy Crockett* were heavily mythologized during their own lifetimes, blurring the boundary between documented biographical fact and exuberant frontier legend.
- Influential folklorist *Richard Dorson* coined the critical term "fakelore" in 1950 to protest synthetic, mass-market fabrications of frontier characters that overshadowed authentic oral traditions collected from real regional communities.

## connections

- [[american-literature]] — absorbed oral storytelling, regional dialect, and tall-tale exaggerations through major authors like *Washington Irving* and *Mark Twain*.
- [[united-states]] — provided the evolving geographic frontier, diverse immigrant waves, and multi-ethnic historical crucible where distinct regional folklore took root.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — prompted legendary labor ballads and cautionary folk songs about mechanization displacing manual workers, exemplified by the tragedy of *John Henry*.
- [[romanticism]] — inspired nineteenth-century American writers and antiquarians to seek a distinct national mythology through remote landscapes, local color, and supernatural folklore.
- [[colonialism]] — suppressed and displaced Indigenous oral traditions while establishing early Anglo-European superstition and puritanical legends across settler communities.
- [[american-civil-war]] — spawned enduring soldier lore, spectral camp ballads, and mythic folk narratives surrounding figures such as *Stonewall Jackson* and *Abraham Lincoln*.
- [[gothic-literature]] — drew directly from regional ghost stories, cursed local histories, and wilderness superstitions to evoke dark, atmospheric psychological dread.
