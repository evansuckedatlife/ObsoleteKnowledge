## summary

**A Modest Proposal** is a landmark Juvenalian satirical essay published anonymously by the Anglo-Irish clergyman and polemicist *Jonathan Swift* in 1729 in Dublin. It ostensibly argues that the impoverished Roman Catholic tenant farmers of Ireland should alleviate their crushing destitution and starvation by selling their one-year-old children as gourmet food to the wealthy Anglo-Irish aristocracy. Regarded across the Western canon as the supreme masterpiece of sustained ironic deadpan, the work skewers British economic exploitation, moral apathy, and the cold calculations of early modern political arithmetic.

## you gotta know

- Recommends butchering impoverished infants at one year of age to serve as wholesome, nourishing food, noting that a single baby would make two dishes at an entertainment for friends and four when boiled with a little pepper or salt.
- Adopts the calm, rational mask of a scientific economic projector who uses meticulous quantitative calculations, reckoning that out of 120,000 annual infants, 20,000 should be preserved for breeding stock while 100,000 are sold for eight shillings net profit.
- Attributes the cannibalistic premise to an *American acquaintance* living in London, who assured the narrator that a healthy, well-nursed infant is a delicious luxury food whether stewed, roasted, baked, or broiled.
- Proposes auxiliary economic uses for the child carcasses, noting that their flayed skins would produce admirable fine leather gloves for aristocratic ladies and stylish summer boots for gentlemen.
- Rejects a companion's idea to harvest older adolescents between twelve and fourteen to replace dwindling deer venison, judging that adolescent boys would yield tough, lean meat from exercise and girls would soon reach childbearing age.
- Highlights six explicit socio-economic benefits, arguing the scheme would reduce the number of Papists, provide tenants with tangible property to pay overdue rent, foster marital harmony, and stimulate local Dublin tavern commerce.
- Explicitly reveals *Swift's* actual, constructive reform agenda by having the narrator contemptuously brush aside realistic measures, such as taxing absentee landlords, promoting domestic cloth manufacturing, and demanding honesty from merchants.
- Concludes with the narrator's boast of absolute personal impartiality and civic virtue, asserting he cannot profit personally because his youngest child is nine years old and his wife is past childbearing years.

## connections

- [[satire]] — the literary mode perfected through sustained deadpan irony, weaponizing a monstrous persona to enforce moral clarity.
- [[enlightenment]] — the philosophical era whose rising faith in mathematical utilitarianism and cold political arithmetic the pamphlet savagely burlesques.
- [[england]] — the imperial power whose crushing trade restrictions, Navigation Acts, and grain exports reduced eighteenth-century Ireland to famine.
- [[british-empire]] — the political system that entrenched the Protestant Ascendancy and treated Irish colonial subjects as disposable commodities.
- [[colonialism]] — the exploitative dynamic whereby imperial landlords metaphorically devour their subjects, made literal through cannibalism.
- [[charles-dickens]] — Victorian novelist who later inherited *Swift's* indignation against callous political economists and utilitarian indifference.
- [[gulliver-travels]] — *Swift's* major satirical prose masterpiece published three years earlier, sharing the same misanthropic disgust with human cruelty.
