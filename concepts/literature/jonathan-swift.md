## summary

**Jonathan Swift** (1667–1745) was an Irish author and satirist whose savage wit and misanthropic vision made him one of the defining literary voices of the *Enlightenment*. Through his masterpiece *Gulliver's Travels*, the essay *A Modest Proposal*, and dozens of polemical works, Swift deployed ironic rage to expose the follies and cruelties of his age—political corruption, religious hypocrisy, intellectual pretension, and colonial violence. His combination of formal brilliance and moral indignation established him as the gold standard of English-language satire.

## you gotta know

- Born in Dublin but educated and lived much of his life in England; his Irishness gave him a perspective of outsider-within on English politics and society.
- *Gulliver's Travels* (1726) is framed as a voyage narrative but functions as a systematic satire of human nature, politics, and the conceits of empirical science.
- *A Modest Proposal* (1729) is his most famous essay: a deadpan suggestion that the Irish solve famine by selling their children as food to the English, exposing English indifference to Irish suffering.
- Swift's irony is so complete and sustained that readers sometimes miss the moral outrage beneath it; his satire operates on the principle of letting the reader discover the horror.
- His later years brought increasing anger and isolation; works like *A Tale of a Tub* grow more obscure and vicious in their attack on human presumption.
- Swift suffered from mental illness and was diagnosed with dementia late in life; he famously wrote, "I shall die like a poisoned rat in a hole."
- He left much of his estate to found a mental hospital in Dublin, a gift befitting a writer obsessed with human folly and breakdown.

## connections

- [[gulliver-travels]] — his masterwork; the definitive eighteenth-century satirical novel.
- [[a-modest-proposal]] — the essay that defines satire through ironic extremity and moral clarity.
- [[satire]] — Swift is the canonical master of English satire; *A Modest Proposal* is the genre's gold standard.
- [[enlightenment]] — the intellectual movement Swift inhabited and critiqued through reason turned against human presumption.
- [[irony]] — ironic inversion is Swift's primary weapon; sustained irony makes moral critique bite.
- [[alexander-pope]] — Swift's contemporary; both used wit and satire to expose folly and corruption.
- [[ireland]] — Swift's birthplace and the site of his greatest moral outrage; Irish suffering is the true subject beneath his satire.
