## summary

**American Naturalism** was a literary movement that flourished from the 1890s through the 1910s in the [[united-states]], developing as a severe, deterministic extension of nineteenth-century [[realism]]. Drawing upon evolutionary theory and socioeconomic analysis, naturalist writers portrayed characters whose fates were governed by heredity, instinct, and environmental forces beyond their control. By confronting urban squalor, biological drives, and cosmic indifference, the movement transformed [[american-literature]] and laid the intellectual groundwork for twentieth-century [[modernism]].

## you gotta know

- Emerged during the late nineteenth century as authors applied evolutionary determinism and scientific objectivity to narrative prose, drawing inspiration from *Émile Zola* in [[france]] while rejecting the moral optimism and heroic agency of [[romanticism]].
- *Stephen Crane* helped inaugurate the movement with his 1893 novella *Maggie: A Girl of the Streets*, set in the squalid tenements of [[new-york-city]], and dramatized nature's utter indifference toward shipwrecked men in his classic short story *The Open Boat*.
- *Frank Norris* championed naturalist theory in critical essays like *The Responsibilities of the Novelist*, creating terrifying portraits of atavistic degeneration in *McTeague* and exposing the strangling power of the railroad conglomerate over California farmers in *The Octopus*.
- *Theodore Dreiser* established the urban naturalist novel through *Sister Carrie*, chronicling the rise of an actress alongside the biological and social decline of *George Hurstwood*, who descends into destitution and suicide in a Bowery flophouse.
- *Jack London* transposed naturalist conflict to the frozen Klondike in *The Call of the Wild* and *White Fang*, exploring primitive survival instincts, while his story *To Build a Fire* pitted solitary human endurance against lethal Arctic cold.
- Characterized philosophically by a belief in the "brute within," biological heredity, and economic determinism, portraying humans as pawns caught in mechanistic systems rather than morally free agents.
- Influenced the early American theatrical tradition through playwrights like *Eugene O'Neill*, whose works *The Hairy Ape* and *The Iceman Cometh* merged naturalist pessimism with emerging [[existentialism]], as well as mid-century fiction such as *Richard Wright*'s *Native Son*.

## connections

- [[realism]] — the broader aesthetic precursor that naturalism radicalized by replacing objective social observation with strict biological and environmental determinism.
- [[american-literature]] — the national tradition that naturalism modernized by breaking Victorian taboos concerning sexuality, poverty, and institutional corruption.
- [[united-states]] — the rapidly urbanizing nation whose post-[[american-civil-war]] Gilded Age upheavals generated the grim material conditions depicted in naturalist fiction.
- [[industrial-revolution]] — the technological and economic transformation that produced mechanized factory labor, tenement slums, and corporate monopolies central to naturalist plots.
- [[new-york-city]] — the primary metropolitan landscape whose stark wealth divides and immigrant slums provided settings for *Stephen Crane* and *Theodore Dreiser*.
- [[existentialism]] — the philosophical tradition that shared naturalism's vision of an unfeeling universe, while insisting on human freedom rather than deterministic helplessness.
- [[modernism]] — the subsequent literary movement that inherited naturalism's disillusionment with social progress while pioneering radical narrative experimentation.
