## summary

**Supernatural cinema** is the film genre devoted to stories of ghosts, demons, witches, curses, and the eruption of impossible or otherworldly forces into the real world. It differs from horror by treating the supernatural not as a source of visceral revulsion but as a mystery to be solved, often through psychological investigation. Contemporary directors like *M. Night Shyamalan* have shown that the supernatural, filtered through cinematography and editing, can generate profound suspense and thematic richness.

## you gotta know

- The supernatural has been a staple of cinema since its earliest years; ghost stories translated naturally to the visual medium, which could render the invisible visible through camera tricks and editing.
- Cinema's unique advantage over literature is *ambiguity of perception*: a shadow could be supernatural or natural; a sound could be explained or unexplained; camera angles can suggest a presence or withhold confirmation.
- *Shyamalan*'s innovation was treating the supernatural as *psychological truth* rather than external horror: ghosts represent trauma, unresolved loss, and the persistence of the past in consciousness.
- The twist ending—where the supernatural is reframed as psychological or the reveal occurs late—has become canonical to the genre, with *The Sixth Sense* (1999) serving as the modern prototype.
- Cinematography is central to the genre: overhead shots, reflective surfaces, and the use of negative space create an atmosphere where the supernatural feels *plausible* within the film's internal logic.
- Supernatural cinema often operates through ambiguity: the viewer is unsure whether phenomena are real, hallucinated, or metaphorical, sustaining suspense through interpretive uncertainty.
- The genre's power lies in its fusion of outer threat and inner conflict: the supernatural is simultaneously an external menace and a manifestation of character psychology.
