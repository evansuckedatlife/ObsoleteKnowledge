## summary

**Beat Generation** was a literary and cultural movement of the 1950s–1960s, centered on American poets and writers who rejected conformity, materialism, and Cold War militarism. Beat writers pioneered spontaneous composition, championed Eastern philosophy, celebrated sexuality and drug use, and adopted a countercultural stance that influenced rock music, youth rebellion, and the broader counterculture of the 1960s. Figures like *Allen Ginsberg*, *Jack Kerouac*, and *Lawrence Ferlinghetti* redefined what American literature could be and what poets could address.

## you gotta know

- The Beats rejected the formalism and established literary institutions of their era, favoring raw emotional expression, spontaneous composition, and oral performance over polished formal verse and academic poetics.
- Allen Ginsberg's poem *Howl* (1956) became the iconic Beat text, featuring long lines, prophetic rage, and explicit sexual and spiritual content that shocked the literary establishment and sparked obscenity trials.
- Jack Kerouac's *On the Road* (1957) captured Beat restlessness and spontaneity, defining a generation's appetite for travel, experience, and liberation from suburban conformity and institutional constraints.
- The movement drew heavily on Zen Buddhism and Eastern philosophy, seeing spiritual search and enlightenment as central to the poet's mission and life, not just literary technique.
- Beatniks — the stereotypical Beat figures with goatees, berets, and bongo drums in coffeehouses — emerged partly as media caricature that both misrepresented and popularized a more serious literary movement.
- The Beats were direct forerunners of the 1960s counterculture and youth rebellion, anticipating its rejection of authority, drug experimentation, sexual liberation, and passionate anti-war political activism.
- Beat writers valued authenticity, immediacy, and confession, seeing the personal voice and lived experience as more truthful and urgent than literary polish or academic detachment from the world.
- The Beats' embrace of jazz rhythm and bebop language influenced American poetry's adoption of oral and vernacular styles, breaking away from previous generations' formal poetic diction and constraints.
