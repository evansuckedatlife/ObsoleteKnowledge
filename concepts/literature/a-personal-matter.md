## summary

**A Personal Matter** (*Kojinteki na taiken*) is a landmark 1964 existential novel by *Kenzaburō Ōe*, depicting a young father's moral unraveling and eventual redemption following the birth of a brain-damaged child. Set in Tokyo, [[japan]], during the [[showa-period]], the narrative follows a dissatisfied twenty-seven-year-old cram-school teacher nicknamed *Bird* who dreams of escaping domestic entrapment by traveling to Africa. When his wife gives birth to an infant afflicted with a severe brain hernia, *Bird* plunges into alcohol, deceit, and existential despair before confronting his parental duty. Grounded in the author's personal trauma following the birth of his own disabled son, the work achieved international acclaim for its unflinching psychological [[realism]] and stands as a central achievement behind his 1994 [[nobel-prize-in-literature]].

## you gotta know

- Follows *Bird*, a dissatisfied twenty-seven-year-old cram-school teacher whose romanticized dream of escaping to Africa is abruptly shattered when his wife gives birth to a son with an encephalocele, a brain hernia that doctors describe as giving the infant two heads.
- Inspired by the real-life 1963 birth of *Kenzaburō Ōe*'s son *Hikari Ōe*, who survived complex cranial surgery to become a celebrated classical composer despite significant cognitive disabilities.
- Deeply informed by European [[existentialism]]—particularly the philosophies of *Jean-Paul Sartre* and *Albert Camus*—the novel examines bad faith, moral evasion, and the heavy burden of authentic personal responsibility in postwar [[japan]].
- Fleeing the hospital, *Bird* takes refuge in the apartment of *Himiko*, a former university lover who drives a sports car, practices free love, and grapples with her own existential guilt following her husband's suicide.
- Descending into alcoholism and debasement, *Bird* is assaulted by youth delinquents in a gay bar, vomits during a private English tutoring session with a diplomat, and repeatedly attempts to disassociate from his parental responsibilities.
- *Bird* conspires with an unscrupulous physician to starve the infant on sugar water and transports the dying child to an illicit abortion clinic in the red-light district, intending to let the baby quietly expire.
- In the novel's climactic turning point, *Bird* experiences a revulsion against his cowardice, abandons his escapist fantasies, and rescues the infant from the clinic to authorize dangerous corrective surgery.
- Translated into English by *John Nathan* in 1968, the work won the prestigious *Shinchosha Literary Prize* and established the author's international stature alongside other postwar literary figures of the [[showa-period]].

## connections

- [[japan]] — setting of the novel, reflecting the postwar generation's disillusionment, spiritual vacuum, and search for identity.
- [[existentialism]] — core philosophical movement underpinning the protagonist's battle against bad faith and cowardly moral evasion.
- [[realism]] — narrative tradition exemplified by the novel's unsparing, visceral depiction of physical deformation and emotional trauma.
- [[fyodor-dostoevsky]] — literary forebear whose themes of criminal guilt, moral torment, and painful spiritual resurrection directly parallel the plot.
- [[world-war-ii]] — historical catastrophe whose aftermath fractured traditional authority and cast a long shadow over the characters' youth.
- [[modernism]] — literary tradition that informs the fragmented psychology, symbolic motifs, and antiheroic characterization of the text.
- [[tragedy]] — classical dramatic structure echoed in the hero's catastrophic error, descent into chaos, and climactic moral catharsis.
