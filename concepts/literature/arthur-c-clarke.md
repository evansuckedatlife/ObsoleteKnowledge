## summary

**Arthur C. Clarke** (1917–2008) was a British science fiction author and futurist whose prolific career spanned seven decades. A trained physicist and engineer, Clarke brought rigor and optimism to science fiction, imagining technological futures grounded in plausible science. His work influenced both literature and actual space exploration; he conceived the geostationary satellite decades before its realization and collaborated with filmmaker Stanley Kubrick on the iconic film *2001: A Space Odyssey*.

## you gotta know

- Published over 100 books and 1,000 short stories; one of the most prolific and celebrated science fiction authors of the 20th century.
- Formulated "Clarke's Three Laws," the most famous: "Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic."
- Pioneered the concept of the geostationary orbit (1945), now called the "Clarke orbit," decades before the first satellites were launched.
- *2001: A Space Odyssey* (novel and film adaptation, 1968) remains his best-known work; the collaboration with Kubrick produced one of cinema's most philosophically ambitious films.
- His fiction often centered on human contact with alien intelligence, the limits of knowledge, and humanity's place in a vast cosmos.
- Distinguished by technical plausibility and optimistic futurism: Clarke believed science and reason could solve human problems.
- Spent much of his later life in Sri Lanka; wrote extensively about diving and marine biology, weaving underwater adventure into some of his fiction.
- Influenced a generation of scientists and engineers; many cite Clarke as a formative inspiration for pursuing careers in space exploration and technology.
- His legacy endures: Clarke's vision of technology and optimism about human potential remain touchstones in science fiction and scientific discourse.
