## summary

**American poetry** encompasses the diverse and evolving poetic traditions developed in the United States, from colonial-era devotional verse through contemporary experimental work. American poets have drawn inspiration from romanticism, modernism, and indigenous voices to forge a distinctive tradition that emphasizes individualism, democratic energy, and formal innovation. Figures like *Hart Crane* and *Allen Ginsberg* exemplify the movement's range and its commitment to pushing the boundaries of language.

## you gotta know

- Early American poetry was deeply influenced by *British Romanticism* and *Transcendentalism*, particularly *Ralph Waldo Emerson* and *Henry David Thoreau*.
- The 19th century saw the emergence of distinctly American voices, including *Emily Dickinson's* experimental metrics and *Walt Whitman's* expansive free verse.
- *Hart Crane's* modernist epics synthesized American industrial imagery, classical mythology, and formally daring language to capture the American landscape and psyche.
- *Allen Ginsberg's* *Howl* (1956) became an anthem of the *Beat Generation*, combining long prophetic lines, autobiographical intensity, and social protest.
- American poetry encompasses diverse regional and cultural traditions: African American poetry, Chicano poetry, Native American poetry, and immigrant poetic voices, each enriching the mainstream.
- The *Harlem Renaissance* produced canonical African American poets exploring racial identity, jazz aesthetics, and modernist form.
- Contemporary American poetry remains experimental and plural, with no single dominant school; poets draw on confessional, language, slam, and mixed-media traditions.
- American poetry prizes colloquial speech, personal experience, and formal innovation, often rejecting the "high formality" associated with British tradition.
- The relationship between technical precision and accessibility remains a defining tension in American poetry.
