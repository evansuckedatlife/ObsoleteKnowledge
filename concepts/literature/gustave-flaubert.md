## summary

**Gustave Flaubert** (1821–1880) was the French novelist who elevated realism into an obsessive aesthetic philosophy and established himself as a master of psychological insight and linguistic precision. Through works like *Madame Bovary* and *Salammbô*, he demonstrated that the mundane provincial life and internal monologue of ordinary people could be as artistically significant as historical epics or romantic adventure, fundamentally reshaping how literature understood itself.

## you gotta know

- Born in Rouen, the son of a doctor; his provincial, petit-bourgeois childhood and family life became the template for his fictional explorations of provincial ennui and moral disappointment.
- *Madame Bovary* (1856), his masterpiece, depicts an adulterous provincial wife's self-destructive romantic fantasies; its realism and frank treatment of female sexuality scandalized France and led to his prosecution for immorality (he was acquitted).
- Pioneered the concept of *style* as moral and philosophical commitment: he believed perfect prose was a supreme good, rewrote passages obsessively, and believed an author should be everywhere present yet nowhere visible in the text.
- Spent years researching each novel, traveling to historical locations and amassing documentation; for *Salammbô*, he journeyed to Tunisia to faithfully recreate Carthage's material culture.
- His famous dictum "Madame Bovary, c'est moi" (Madame Bovary is me) paradoxically asserts both the author's distance from their creation and the author's complete psychological investment in it.
- Mentored *Guy de Maupassant*, the great short-story writer, and influenced virtually every realist and naturalist writer after him, from the Russians to the American modernists.
- Pioneered the technique of *style indirect libre* (free indirect discourse), allowing readers access to characters' consciousness while maintaining narrative distance and irony.
- Struggled with poverty and depression throughout his career, particularly after *Madame Bovary's* success; the legal case surrounding its publication haunted him and reinforced his conviction that the artist must stand apart from society.
- His aesthetic principle of *impassibilité* (authorial detachment and objectivity) deliberately rejected Romantic emotionalism and sentimental narrative, forcing readers to draw their own moral judgments from unmoralized observation.
