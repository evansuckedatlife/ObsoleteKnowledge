## summary

The **heroic couplet** is a verse form of two consecutive lines of iambic pentameter that rhyme (AA BB CC...), which became the dominant English verse form for narrative and satirical poetry from the 17th through 19th centuries. Alexander Pope perfected the form and demonstrated its full expressive range—from grave moral statement to biting satire—making it the vehicle for some of English literature's most quotable and technically brilliant poetry.

## you gotta know

- Developed in English during the 16th century and became the standard form for dramatic heroic (tragic) verse, hence the name; it dominated English poetry for two centuries.
- The form's constraint—the closed couplet rhyming on every other line—creates formal closure and epigrammatic concision; each couplet is potentially a self-contained thought; the form rewards wit and compression.
- *John Dryden* (1631–1700) elevated the **heroic couplet** to supreme status, using it for epic translation, satire, and dramatic verse with unprecedented technical control; he proved the form's range and dignity.
- *Alexander Pope* (1688–1744) became synonymous with the form: *The Rape of the Lock* uses the couplet for witty social satire, while his translation of *Homer's Iliad* proves the form can sustain epic grandeur and formal beauty.
- *Pope's* couplets are famous for embedding moral observation and satirical wit in formally perfect lines: "To err is human, to forgive, divine" is a heroic couplet that became a proverb; his technical mastery is unmatched.
- The form's constraint creates a particular aesthetic: meaning must be compressed, syntax must accommodate the rhyme without awkwardness, and wit flows from the tension between thought and form; restriction generates brilliance.
- By the Romantic period (early 19th century), poets began to resist the **heroic couplet** as overly artificial and restrictive; they sought more flexible forms that could accommodate individual voice and natural speech rhythms.
- Yet despite Romantic resistance, the form retained prestige and influence; Victorian poets revived it, and modern poets still employ it for comic and satirical purposes; its technical discipline never loses relevance.
