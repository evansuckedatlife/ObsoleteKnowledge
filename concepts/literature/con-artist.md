## summary

**The con artist** is a stock literary character—a charming charlatan who preys on others' greed, vanity, or naïveté, achieving success through manipulation and performance rather than honest labour. Rooted in medieval and folk traditions of the trickster, the con artist became a central figure in Renaissance satirical drama and remains a perennial subject of fiction, satire, and moral inquiry. The archetype reveals uncomfortable truths about human nature: the desire to believe in easy wealth, the slipperiness of identity, and the fine line between cleverness and villainy.

## you gotta know

- The term "con artist" derives from "confidence man"—one who exploits the victim's trust, or "confidence," to defraud them.
- Appears prominently in Ben Jonson's *Volpone* and *The Alchemist*, where elaborate deceptions expose human greed and delusion.
- The literary con artist often succeeds through a combination of flattery, false authority, and careful study of human psychology.
- Typically presented with ambivalence: partly sympathetic (quick-witted, underdog), partly dangerous (predatory, amoral, destructive).
- Related to the medieval *trickster* archetype (like Reynard the Fox), who uses cunning to survive and prosper in an unjust world.
- In modern literature, the con artist figures in works ranging from *The Great Gatsby* to *Catch-22*, often serving as a mirror held up to societal corruption and hypocrisy.

## connections

- [[volpone]] — Ben Jonson's masterpiece of con-artistry and moral satire, wherein a merchant fakes illness to trap legacy-hunters.
- [[the-alchemist]] — another Jonson satire centring on fraudulent alchemy as the ultimate con, exploiting desire for transformation.
- [[satire]] — the con artist thrives as a vehicle for satirical exposure of greed, credulity, and social vice.
- [[deception]] — the thematic centre of con-artist narratives, exploring how illusion and performance operate.
- [[trickster]] — the archetypal predecessor to the con artist, a figure who survives through cunning and rhetoric.
- [[morality]] — con-artist narratives inevitably raise questions about the ethics of survival, cleverness, and exploitation.
