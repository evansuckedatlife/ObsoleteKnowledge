## summary

The **American frontier** refers both to the historical westward expansion of the United States from the 18th through 20th centuries and to the literary and cultural mythology surrounding it. In fiction and popular imagination, the frontier embodies individualism, courage, conflict, and the taming of wilderness—themes that captivated American writers from *Mark Twain* to the present. The frontier myth has shaped American identity and literature as profoundly as any single concept.

## you gotta know

- Historically marks the boundary between settled and unsettled territory, moving westward from the Atlantic coast; the U.S. Census officially declared it "closed" in 1890.
- In literature and culture, the frontier became a symbol of American exceptionalism, opportunity, and the clash between civilization and nature.
- *Mark Twain* captured the frontier experience in *The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* and *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer*, setting them on the Mississippi River and immortalizing the freedom and moral complexity of frontier life.
- The frontier archetype includes the self-made man, the outlaw, the lawman, the rancher, the prospector, and the pioneer—all figures of American popular mythology.
- Associated with *manifest destiny*, the doctrine that American expansion across North America was inevitable and divinely ordained.
- Frontier narratives often elide or romanticize the displacement and genocide of *Native Americans*, making the myth a contested and problematic force in American letters.
- Western fiction, cattle-drive sagas, and gold-rush stories draw on frontier mythology and remain central to American genre fiction.
- The frontier persists in American consciousness and literature as a symbol of escape, reinvention, and the allure of the open wilderness.
