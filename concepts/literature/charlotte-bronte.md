## summary

**Charlotte Brontë** (1818–1848) was a British novelist whose *Jane Eyre* became a cornerstone of English literature, pioneering the female bildungsroman and the depiction of female independence. Born in Yorkshire to an Irish clergyman's family, she and her siblings—including the similarly gifted *Emily* and *Anne*—created a rich imaginative world that would fuel their later published works. Her unflinching portrayal of a woman's inner emotional and moral life transformed the novel as a form capable of expressing female consciousness.

## you gotta know

- Wrote under the pseudonym *Currer Bell* to bypass contemporary prejudice against female authors.
- *Jane Eyre* (1847) broke convention by centering a poor, plain, ordinary heroine who speaks her mind, resists patriarchal authority, and demands respect and equality in love.
- Incorporated gothic elements—the mysterious laugh in the attic, the burning of Thornfield—into a deeply introspective psychological narrative.
- Her heroine refuses to become a "kept woman" or a missionary's dutiful wife; she insists on marriage based on mutual respect and equality.
- Also authored *The Professor* (1857, published posthumously), which drew on her experience as a governess in Belgium.
- Died at 30, likely from complications of pregnancy, leaving a relatively small but enormously influential body of work.
- Her upbringing in isolated Yorkshire moorland and her creative collaboration with her siblings (the "Brontë Parsonage Legends") shaped her imaginative power and her unflinching treatment of isolation and intensity.
- *Jane Eyre* was initially controversial for its passionate language, its heroine's refusal to be submissive, and its suggestion that female desire and agency were as legitimate as male experience.

## connections

- [[emily-bronte]] — her sister, author of *Wuthering Heights*; both revolutionised the novel's emotional and narrative possibilities.
- [[jane-eyre-twist]] — her masterwork, which redefined the heroine in English literature.
- [[gothic-literature]] — she wielded gothic devices with psychological subtlety rather than melodrama.
- [[feminism]] — her assertion of female subjectivity and agency made her a foundational voice for women's writing.
- [[victorian-literature]] — she participated in and shaped the great novel tradition of her era.
- [[governessing]] — a crucial biographical element; her experience as a governess directly informed *Jane Eyre*.
