## summary

**Algeria** occupies a pivotal place in twentieth-century literature as both the cradle of *Albert Camus*’s philosophical absurdism and the primary crucible for postcolonial writing in North Africa. Ruled for over a century by [[france]] until winning independence in 1962, the country provided the sun-drenched coastal backdrops of Algiers and Oran for foundational masterpieces exploring detachment, pestilence, and mortal dread. The violent struggle against French [[colonialism]] in the Algerian War profoundly shaped contemporary debates across [[existentialism]], forcing prominent European and Maghrebi intellectuals to reckon with violence, justice, and human solidarity.

## you gotta know

- Serves as the iconic setting of *Albert Camus*’s novel *The Stranger* (*L'Étranger*), in which the emotionally detached clerk *Meursault* shoots an unnamed Arab five times on a sweltering beach near Algiers.
- Provides the setting for *Camus*’s allegorical novel *The Plague* (*La Peste*), which chronicles an outbreak of bubonic plague that seals off the port city of Oran and prompts doctor *Bernard Rieux* to organize civilian sanitation squads.
- Formed the homeland and cultural heritage of the *pied-noir* European settler community into which *Camus* was born, a background captured poignantly in his posthumous, autobiographical novel *The First Man* (*Le Premier Homme*).
- Sparked intense intellectual controversy during the Algerian War of Independence (1954–1962), particularly when *Camus* controversially defended a ceasefire rather than outright Algerian independence, asserting in Stockholm that he would defend his mother before abstract justice.
- Inspired *Kamel Daoud*’s acclaimed 2013 retelling *The Meursault Investigation* (*Meursault, contre-enquête*), which gives a name, *Moussa*, and a complex history to the nameless Arab slain in *The Stranger*, narrated by his surviving brother *Harun*.
- Served as the focal point of revolutionary psychiatrist *Frantz Fanon*’s anti-colonial manifesto *The Wretched of the Earth* (*Les Damnés de la Terre*), written with a celebrated preface by *Jean-Paul Sartre* analyzing the psychological trauma inflicted by French imperial domination.
- Produced landmark works of indigenous Francophone literature including *Kateb Yacine*’s polyphonic, anti-colonial novel *Nedjma*, which portrays the titular woman as an elusive symbol of a fractured nation under French occupation.
- Featured centrally in the bilingual memoirs and historical fiction of feminist author *Assia Djebar*, whose polyphonic quartet opener *Fantasia: An Algerian Cavalcade* juxtaposes the 1830 French conquest of Algiers with women’s roles during the war of liberation.

## connections

- [[france]] — the colonial ruler whose occupation from 1830 to 1962 framed the historical tensions of modern Algerian fiction.
- [[existentialism]] — the philosophical movement popularized by *Jean-Paul Sartre* and *Albert Camus* whose core themes were staged against the Algerian sun and sea.
- [[colonialism]] — the overarching geopolitical system dissected by Maghrebi writers and contested in twentieth-century polemics.
- [[world-war-ii]] — the global conflict during which *Camus* wrote and published his foundational Algerian narratives while in the French Resistance.
- [[modernism]] — the broader literary movement that influenced Francophone experiments in fragmented narrative structure and alienation.
- [[islam]] — the dominant religious and cultural tradition that shaped the indigenous North African identities marginalized in colonial literature.
- [[cold-war]] — the geopolitical backdrop under which Algerian revolutionary independence became a global flashpoint for anti-imperialist solidarity.
