## summary

**Anglo-Saxon literature**, also known as **Old English literature**, encompasses the surviving corpus of vernacular poetry and prose produced in early medieval England from the seventh century until the Norman Conquest of 1066. Copied predominantly by monastic scribes across Northumbria, Mercia, and Wessex, this body of writing uniquely fuses pre-Christian Germanic warrior culture with the theological doctrines of Christianity. As the earliest extensive vernacular literary tradition in post-Roman Western Europe, it established enduring motifs of exile, earthly impermanence, heroic loyalty, and elegiac mourning that fundamentally shaped the trajectory of English poetry.

## you gotta know

- *Beowulf* stands as the undisputed masterpiece and longest surviving heroic epic poem of the tradition, chronicling the Geatish prince's legendary battles against the monster *Grendel*, Grendel's vengeful mother, and a fiery dragon.
- Surviving verse is composed in unrhymed alliterative meter, dividing each four-stress line with a central caesura into two rhythmic hemistichs bound together by alliterating initial consonants rather than end-rhymes.
- Poets relied heavily on specialized figurative diction known as kennings, which are vivid compound metaphors such as *hronrad* (whale-road) for the ocean, *banhus* (bone-house) for the body, and *gold-wine* (gold-friend) for a generous king.
- Nearly all surviving Old English poetry is preserved in just four late-tenth-century manuscripts: the *Nowell Codex* (containing *Beowulf*), the *Exeter Book*, the *Vercelli Book*, and the *Junius manuscript*.
- The *Exeter Book* preserves a famous group of elegiac lyric monologues, notably *The Wanderer*, *The Seafarer*, and *The Wife's Lament*, which dwell mournfully upon solitude, sea journeys, banished kinship, and the transience of mortal joy (*ubi sunt*).
- Early religious verse includes *Caedmon's Hymn*, an alliterative praise song recorded by the Venerable *Bede* that represents the earliest attested English poem, and *The Dream of the Rood*, a visionary work wherein the True Cross recounts the crucifixion of Christ as a heroic battle.
- Royal patronage under King *Alfred the Great* of Wessex sparked a major prose renaissance, commissioning the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* and translating seminal Latin texts by *Boethius*, *Gregory the Great*, and *Augustine* into the West Saxon dialect.
- The tragic heroic poem *The Battle of Maldon* commemorates the historic 991 defeat of Earl *Byrhtnoth* by Viking invaders, articulating the classic Germanic warrior code that courage and loyalty must heighten as defeat becomes inevitable.

## connections

- [[beowulf]] — the paramount Old English epic poem dramatizing Scandinavian heroic legend and the conflict between human heroism and inevitable mortality.
- [[poetry]] — the foundational formal art of the Anglo-Saxon scop, governed by four-beat alliterative meter, medial pauses, and oral-formulaic conventions.
- [[england]] — the historical realm unified from fragmented Anglo-Saxon kingdoms where this distinct vernacular writing was composed and preserved.
- [[christianity]] — the faith that introduced monastic scriptoria and Latin learning, transforming pagan heroic ethos into theological allegory.
- [[linguistics]] — the academic discipline that studies the synthetic grammar, phonology, and West Saxon dialect that define Old English texts.
- [[roman-empire]] — the vanished imperial power whose abandoned stone ruins inspired the mournful Old English elegiac poem *The Ruin*.
- [[english-renaissance]] — the subsequent literary revival whose antiquarians, including *Sir Robert Cotton*, salvaged and safeguarded surviving medieval manuscripts.
