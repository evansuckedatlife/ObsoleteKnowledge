# Psychological Horror

## summary

**Psychological horror** is a mode of terror that privileges the internal landscape of the mind over external supernatural menace. Rather than relying on ghosts, monsters, or explicit violence, it induces dread through unreliable perception, ambiguity, isolation, and the disintegration of reason. The haunting unfolds within the protagonist's consciousness: madness, paranoia, and the collapse of certainty become the true horror. This mode flourished in *Henry James*, *Edgar Allan Poe*, and the Gothic tradition, and it established a crucial equivalence—that a haunted house is inseparable from a haunted mind.

## you gotta know

- Psychological horror emerges from uncertainty: the reader cannot determine whether supernatural phenomena are real or products of mental illness, creating unresolved dread.
- *The Turn of the Screw* (1898) by *Henry James* is the canonical text: a governess at a country estate becomes convinced that the children in her care are being possessed or haunted, but the text provides no definitive proof, leaving ambiguity as the engine of horror.
- *The Haunting of Hill House* (1959) by *Shirley Jackson* treats the house itself as a psychological entity: its architecture and isolation erode the protagonist's sanity, and by the novel's end, the boundary between external threat and internal breakdown dissolves.
- Isolation is structural: psychological horror often confines the protagonist (a house, an island, a hospital) so that escape becomes impossible and external validation unreachable.
- Unreliable narration is essential: the reader is trapped in the protagonist's perspective and cannot distinguish delusion from reality, experiencing their descent into madness directly.
- *Edgar Allan Poe*'s tales—especially *The Fall of the House of Usher* and *The Cask of Amontillado*—established the mode through first-person narration where the narrator's grip on sanity weakens with each revelation.
