# Haiku

## summary

**Haiku** is a traditional *Japanese* poetic form consisting of three lines following a 5-7-5 syllable pattern, typically focusing on nature, seasons, and moments of insight or beauty. Emerging in the 16th century and refined into an art form by masters like *Matsuo Bashō* in the 17th century, haiku embodies *Zen Buddhist* aesthetics and has become one of the world's most recognized and imitated poetic forms.

## you gotta know

- The form adheres strictly to 5-7-5 syllable (or morae) counts across its three lines, creating a compressed structure that demands precision and economy of language.
- *Matsuo Bashō* (1644–1694) elevated haiku from a parlor game into a serious literary art; his works exemplify the form's aesthetic ideals: simplicity, nature imagery, and moments of spiritual insight.
- Haiku traditionally incorporates a *kigo* (seasonal reference) and *kireji* (cutting word)—poetic devices that ground the poem in seasonal time and create sharp juxtaposition or insight; these are debated in modern haiku.
- The form reflects *Zen Buddhist* influence: emphasis on direct observation of nature, the beauty of impermanence, and sudden enlightenment (*satori*) conveyed through precise, minimal language.
- Common haiku themes include landscapes, wildlife, weather, seasons, and transient moments; many haiku juxtapose two images to create metaphorical meaning through contrast.
- The form was initially called *hokku* (opening verse) and was part of *renga* (linked-verse poetry), where multiple poets contributed stanzas; over time it became a standalone form.
- Haiku spread globally in the 20th century, influencing *American* and *European* poets; it was adopted by modernists (*Imagism*) and adapted into dozens of languages, though debates persist about how to preserve the form outside *Japanese*.
